\section{Personalised Medical Reports in Neurogenetic Disorders}
\subsection{Neurogenetic and Neurodevelopmental Disorders}
Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a heterogeneous group of conditions that emerge during childhood and affect brain development and functioning, often resulting in difficulties with cognition, communication, behaviour, learning, and social functioning. Genetic factors contribute substantially to many NDDs, leading to considerable variation in symptoms, severity, and combinations of intellectual, behavioural, motor, and neurological features \cite{gao_genetic_2025, assadourian_precision_2025}.

KBG syndrome (KBGS) is a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder primarily associated with pathogenic variants or deletions involving the ANKRD11 gene. It presents with a broad spectrum of features, including developmental delay, intellectual and learning difficulties, behavioural and neurological abnormalities, distinctive craniofacial features, macrodontia, short stature, and skeletal abnormalities \cite{martinez-cayuelas_clinical_2023}. The combination and severity of these features can vary substantially between children, highlighting the need to represent each child's individual clinical profile rather than relying solely on generic descriptions.

For families, understanding rare genetic disorders can be challenging due to limited condition-specific information and complex medical terminology. Personalised and accessible patient reports can help consolidate information about a child's symptoms, medical history, and care needs into a clear, patient-specific summary, supporting parents in understanding their child's condition and communicating with healthcare professionals \cite{low_identifying_2025, burbury_exploring_2025}. This motivates the use of automated patient report generation to produce personalised, understandable summaries from complex clinical and background information.

\subsection{LLM-Based Personalised Medical Report Generation}
Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4o and BioGPT, have demonstrated strong capabilities in medical report generation, clinical summarisation, and healthcare documentation, highlighting their potential to synthesise complex clinical information \cite{yuan_continued_2024, rehman_advancement_2025, alabed_large_2026, che_llm-driven_2026, makram_ai_2024}. These capabilities make LLMs promising for personalised patient report generation, as they can integrate patient-specific information and generate summaries tailored to individual clinical contexts. For example, Ganzinger et al. \cite{ganzinger_automated_2025} used open-source LLMs to generate German discharge summaries from structured EHR data, while Tariq et al. \cite{tariq_patient_2024} developed a patient-sensitive model for generating radiology summaries adapted to different levels of medical knowledge. These studies demonstrate the potential of LLMs for generating both clinically useful and patient-oriented medical reports.
\subsection{Challenges of LLM-Based Patient Report Generation}
Despite their potential, deploying large, cloud-hosted LLMs in clinical settings presents significant privacy, regulatory, and resource challenges. Patient–doctor consultations contain highly sensitive medical information, while regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR restrict the transfer of clinical data to external commercial APIs, raising important privacy and compliance concerns \cite{annas_hipaa_2003, noauthor_eu_nodate, zhong_considerations_2025, al-dhabyani_data_2026}. Furthermore, many community clinics and regional hospitals have limited computational resources and may lack the specialised GPU infrastructure required to locally deploy and efficiently run large open-weight models \cite{li_collm_2024}.

\subsection{SLM-Based Personalised Medical Report Generation}
Small Language Models (SLMs) have emerged as efficient alternatives to large language models (LLMs), offering lower computational, memory, and storage requirements while maintaining competitive performance on domain-specific tasks \cite{wang_comprehensive_2025, garg_rise_2026}. These advantages make SLMs attractive for healthcare, where local deployment can improve privacy and reduce reliance on cloud infrastructure when processing sensitive patient information \cite{wang_comprehensive_2025}.

SLMs have demonstrated potential in medical applications. For example, Meerkat showed improved reasoning performance across medical benchmarks while maintaining a lightweight architecture \cite{kim_small_2025}, while Rohanian et al. \cite{rohanian_effectiveness_2022} developed lightweight biomedical models that achieved performance comparable to larger models on downstream tasks. This suggests that SLMs can support clinical text processing and generation with more practical deployment requirements.

For personalised patient report generation, SLMs could enable patient-specific clinical information to be processed locally, reducing the need to transmit sensitive data to external APIs. However, their smaller size can limit complex reasoning and specialised medical knowledge \cite{turki_evaluating_2025, wang_comprehensive_2025}. Knowledge distillation can address this limitation by transferring useful capabilities from larger teacher models to smaller student models \cite{gou_knowledge_2021}.
\section{Knowledge Distillation}
\subsection{Fundamentals of Knowledge Distillation}
Knowledge distillation (KD) is a model compression and knowledge-transfer technique in which a smaller student model learns useful knowledge from a larger and more capable teacher model \cite{hinton_distilling_2015, wang_knowledge_2022}. Unlike conventional compression approaches such as pruning, quantisation, and low-rank factorisation, which reduce or modify the model's parameters or architecture, KD transfers knowledge from the teacher to the student without necessarily altering the underlying model architecture \cite{wang_knowledge_2022, mansourian_comprehensive_2025}. The teacher provides additional learning signals, such as its output distributions, allowing the student to learn aspects of the teacher's behaviour beyond conventional hard target labels \cite{hinton_distilling_2015}.

The central principle of KD is therefore knowledge transfer, whereby information learned by a large model is transferred to a smaller model during training. This enables the student to acquire useful capabilities of the teacher while retaining the computational and memory advantages of a smaller architecture. Consequently, KD can reduce the resources required for model inference and deployment while aiming to preserve much of the teacher's predictive or generative performance \cite{hinton_distilling_2015, wang_knowledge_2022}. Beyond model compression, KD can also facilitate knowledge transfer when labelled data are limited or when knowledge must be transferred across different tasks or models. In privacy-sensitive settings, data-free distillation provides another alternative by enabling knowledge transfer without directly requiring access to the original training data \cite{wang_knowledge_2022}.

The effectiveness of KD depends on several design choices, including what knowledge is transferred, how this knowledge is transferred, and how the teacher and student models are configured \cite{mansourian_comprehensive_2025}. These considerations have led to a range of distillation approaches based on different teacher–student configurations and forms of transferred knowledge, which are discussed in the following subsection.

\subsection{Types of Knowledge Distillation}
Knowledge distillation can be categorised according to the training strategy, accessibility of the teacher model, and type of knowledge transferred. These classifications describe different aspects of the teacher–student framework and are not mutually exclusive.
\subsubsection{Offline, Online, and Self-Distillation}
Offline distillation is the most common form of knowledge distillation and typically involves a pretrained teacher model whose parameters remain frozen while a smaller student learns from its outputs \cite{hinton_distilling_2015, wang_knowledge_2022}. Although straightforward and effective, it requires the teacher to remain available during student training and may transfer biases or limitations present in the teacher \cite{guo_reducing_2021, huang_knowledge_2022-1}.

In online distillation, the teacher and student are trained simultaneously, allowing knowledge to be transferred dynamically during the training process \cite{chen_online_2020}. The teacher may be constructed from multiple student models or branches, removing the need for a separately pretrained teacher, although this can increase training complexity. Self-distillation is a related approach in which knowledge is transferred within the same model, such as from an earlier training stage, deeper layer, or previous version of the model \cite{furlanello_born_2018, zhang_be_2019}. It can improve model performance without requiring a separate, larger teacher network.

\subsubsection{Black-Box and White-Box Distillation}
White-box distillation assumes access to the teacher model's internal information, such as logits, hidden representations, attention patterns, or model parameters. This allows the student to learn from richer signals than the teacher's final outputs, but requires access to the teacher's internal architecture and computations \cite{donisch_inference_2024}.

In contrast, black-box distillation relies only on the teacher's externally observable outputs, without access to its internal parameters or representations. For language models, the teacher can therefore be prompted to generate responses, explanations, or rationales that are subsequently used as supervision for the student \cite{shi_knowledge_2025}. This makes black-box distillation particularly suitable for proprietary or API-based LLMs, where internal model information is inaccessible.

The key distinction is therefore the level of access to the teacher: white-box distillation exploits internal model information, whereas black-box distillation transfers knowledge primarily through the teacher's observable outputs.
\subsubsection{Response-, Logit-, and Feature-Based Distillation}
Knowledge distillation can also be classified according to the form of knowledge transferred from the teacher to the student. In response-based distillation, the student learns from the teacher's final outputs, such as predicted labels or generated text. For language models, this can involve using teacher-generated responses as training targets, making the approach particularly suitable for generative tasks \cite{yang_categories_2023}.

In logit-based distillation, the student learns from the teacher's logits or probability distributions rather than only the final predicted token. These soft targets provide information about the teacher's confidence across alternative predictions and can therefore provide richer supervision than hard labels \cite{habib_knowledge_2024}.

Feature-based distillation transfers knowledge from the teacher's intermediate representations, encouraging the student to learn similar internal features or representations. This can provide richer information about how the teacher processes the input, although it generally requires access to the teacher's internal architecture \cite{park_relational_2019}.
\subsection{Knowledge Distillation for LLMs}
Knowledge distillation for language models extends the teacher–student framework to generative tasks, where a smaller student model learns to reproduce useful capabilities of a larger teacher model. Rather than transferring knowledge for a single prediction task, the process aims to transfer aspects of the teacher's language generation, instruction following, domain knowledge, and response structure \cite{chen_distilling_2021}.

A common approach is to use a teacher LLM to generate instruction–response pairs or synthetic training examples, which are then used to fine-tune the student model\cite{wang_self-instruct_2023}. This provides a practical way of transferring capabilities from large, computationally expensive models to smaller models that are more suitable for efficient deployment. It is particularly useful when the teacher is proprietary or accessed through an API, as the student can be trained using the teacher's generated outputs without requiring access to its internal architecture.

However, distillation of generative language models introduces important challenges. Errors, hallucinations, and undesirable behaviours in teacher-generated responses may be transferred to the student, while the smaller model may not have sufficient capacity to reproduce the full range of the teacher's capabilities\cite{zhang_lifting_2023}. Therefore, the quality and relevance of the teacher-generated training data become important determinants of student performance . This is particularly critical in clinical report generation, where generated content must be accurate, coherent, relevant, and grounded in the individual patient's information. These considerations motivate the need for quality-aware approaches to knowledge distillation, discussed in the following section.

\section{Evaluation}
The evaluation of generated clinical reports requires multiple complementary measures, as no single metric captures all aspects of quality. This study evaluates clinical and linguistic quality, similarity to reference or teacher-generated reports, and model efficiency.
\subsection{Clinical Report Quality Dimensions}
Clinical report quality encompasses multiple dimensions that assess both the accuracy of the generated information and the effectiveness of its presentation. In this study, five key dimensions are considered: factuality, coverage, conciseness, readability, and medical tone, each capturing a distinct aspect of report quality. The following provides a brief description of each dimension.\\
\textbf{Factuality:} Whether the generated report accurately reflects the provided clinical information without false, contradictory, or unsupported claims \cite{munnangi_factehr_2025}.\\
\textbf{Coverage:} Whether the generated report is supported by and adequately represents the relevant information provided in the source \cite{moramarco_towards_2021}.\\
\textbf{Conciseness:} Whether the report communicates relevant information clearly and efficiently without unnecessary repetition, irrelevant content, or excessive detail \cite{schoonbeek_quality_2025}.\\
\textbf{Readability:} Whether the report is clear, coherent, and easy for the intended audience to understand.
\textbf{Medical Tone:} Whether the report uses professional, clinically appropriate, and audience-suitable language \cite{seo_evaluation_2024}.

\subsection{Rubric-Based and LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation}
Rubric-based evaluation uses a predefined set of criteria and scoring guidelines, known as a rubric, to systematically assess generated reports. Each report is evaluated across specific quality dimensions using the same criteria, enabling consistent and structured assessment of different aspects of report quality. This allows complex qualitative properties, such as factuality and readability, to be represented through standardised scores \cite{hashemi_llm-rubric_2024}. Rubrics are particularly useful for generated clinical reports, where quality is inherently multidimensional and cannot be adequately captured by a single metric. A multidimensional rubric allows these aspects to be assessed independently while providing a common framework across different outputs. Furthermore, rubrics can guide automated evaluation by providing an LLM with explicit criteria for assessing generated reports, reducing the need for manual assessment and enabling evaluation of large collections of outputs \cite{fan_sedareval_2024}.

LLM-as-a-Judge extends this approach by using a language model to evaluate generated reports according to the predefined rubric. The judge assesses each report across the specified dimensions and assigns corresponding scores, enabling scalable evaluation while capturing qualitative properties that may be difficult to measure using conventional automatic metrics \cite{zheng_judging_2023}. In clinical report generation, this approach provides a unified framework for assessing dimensions such as factuality, coverage, conciseness, readability, and medical tone.

Despite its scalability and flexibility, LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation presents several challenges. Its reliability depends on the clarity and design of the rubric, as ambiguous criteria may lead to inconsistent scoring. The judging model may also exhibit evaluator bias or produce variable scores across evaluation runs \cite{pombal_self-preference_2026}. In clinical settings, an additional challenge is the ability to identify subtle medical errors and distinguish clinically supported information from statements that are merely plausible. Therefore, clearly defined rubrics and consistent evaluation procedures are important for obtaining reliable and meaningful assessment scores.
\subsection{Automatic Evaluation and Model Efficiency}
Automatic evaluation metrics provide quantitative measures that complement rubric-based assessment. Text similarity metrics, such as ROUGE \cite{lin_rouge_2004}, BLEU \cite{ward_bleu_2001}, BERTScore \cite{zhang_bertscore_2020}, and embedding-based semantic similarity, assess the lexical or semantic correspondence between generated reports and reference or teacher-generated reports. While lexical metrics focus on word or phrase overlap, semantic metrics capture similarities in meaning despite differences in wording. Model efficiency is also important when evaluating an SLM, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Measures such as parameter count, inference time, memory usage, and token generation speed indicate the model's computational cost and practical deployability \cite{wani_benchmarking_2026}. Together, these measures provide a broader assessment of both report quality and model performance.

\section{Overview of the Proposed Framework}
\section{Synthetic Patient Profile Generation}
\subsection{Patient Profile Design}

Synthetic patient profiles were designed as structured representations of the
patient characteristics required to generate diverse and personalised
doctor--patient consultations. Each profile combined clinical features drawn
from predefined KBG syndrome-related domains with a parent communication
persona, enabling variation in both clinical content and communication style.
Basic demographic attributes, including age and gender, were also included to
provide contextual information. The resulting profiles provided a consistent
input structure for the subsequent consultation generation process.

\subsection{Controlled Random Sampling Strategy}

To introduce controlled variability across synthetic patients, profile
attributes were randomly sampled from predefined clinical feature and persona
pools. Clinical features were organised into domains including vision,
hearing, dental, cardiac, neurological, gastrointestinal, and other medical
characteristics. Features were sampled from the corresponding domain-specific
pools, with additional characteristics drawn from the other medical feature
pool. A parent communication persona was independently sampled from a
predefined set representing different communication styles. This strategy
allowed the profiles to vary systematically while maintaining a consistent
overall structure.

The sampling of a clinical feature from a domain can be represented as:

\[
x_d \sim P_d
\]

where $x_d$ denotes the sampled feature from domain $d$, and $P_d$ represents
the corresponding predefined feature pool. The complete clinical feature
pools and parent communication personas used for sampling are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:profile_pools}.

\subsection{Profile Generation Procedure}

For each synthetic patient, demographic attributes and a parent communication
persona were first selected from their respective predefined pools. Clinical
features were then sampled from the relevant domain-specific pools, followed
by additional features from the other medical feature pool. The selected
attributes were combined into a structured profile and assigned a unique
patient identifier. This process was repeated until the required number of
profiles had been generated. The resulting profiles were stored in JSON
format, providing a consistent and machine-readable representation for the
subsequent consultation generation stage.

\section{Doctor–Patient Consultation Ibrahim}

\subsection{Consultation Generation Framework}
Synthetic doctor--patient consultations were generated to create realistic and diverse clinical interactions for subsequent personalised report generation. Each synthetic patient profile was provided to \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct} using a structured prompt. The prompt incorporated the patient-specific clinical features, parent communication persona, fixed consultation structure, and explicit generation constraints. A representative consultation was also included as an in-context example to guide the expected response format and style, constituting a few-shot prompting approach.

The model was instructed to reproduce a fixed sequence of ten predefined doctor questions covering the major clinical domains relevant to KBG syndrome, while generating corresponding parent responses grounded in the supplied profile. The parent persona controlled variation in linguistic style, vocabulary, and level of detail. Generation constraints were used to minimise unsupported clinical information and maintain consistency between the source profile and generated consultation.

Each consultation was generated independently and stored with its patient identifier and source profile in JSON Lines format. The resulting synthetic consultations were subsequently used for clinical report generation. \subsection{Diversity Assessment}
The diversity of the generated consultations was evaluated to determine whether the generation process produced sufficiently varied parent responses rather than repeatedly reproducing similar linguistic patterns. Diversity was assessed across lexical, structural, grammatical, and semantic dimensions.

Distinct-2 \cite{guo_curious_2024} was used to measure lexical variation by calculating the proportion of unique bigrams within parent responses. Standard deviation of Mean Length of Utterance (MLU) \cite{potratz_measurement_2022} captured variation in the length of parent responses, providing an indication of differences in response structure and level of detail. POS Shannon entropy \cite{arora_estimating_2022} measured the distribution and variability of grammatical categories used across the consultations. Finally, pairwise cosine similarity between consultation embeddings was used to assess semantic similarity between generated consultations \cite{noauthor_evaluating_2026}.

Together, these complementary measures assessed whether the consultations varied not only in their surface-level wording, but also in response length, grammatical composition, and overall semantic content. This provided a broader assessment of the diversity of the synthetic consultation dataset.
\section{Teacher-Guided Clinical Report Generation}

Following the generation of synthetic doctor--patient consultations, the
consultations were transformed into personalised clinical reports using a
large language model. This stage was designed to generate high-quality
reference reports that could subsequently be evaluated and used in the
student-model training and knowledge distillation stages. Each report was
grounded in the corresponding consultation, while a clinical reference
context provided additional syndrome-level information required for
interpretation and management recommendations.

The report generation process was formulated as a conditional text generation
task:

\[
y = f_{\theta}(x,c,p)
\]

where $x$ represents the doctor--patient consultation, $c$ represents the
KBG syndrome clinical reference context, $p$ represents the structured
generation prompt, $f_{\theta}$ denotes the language model with parameters
$\theta$, and $y$ represents the generated clinical report. The consultation
provided the patient-specific evidence, while the clinical reference
provided supporting syndrome-level information.

The \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct} model
(\texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct}) was used for report generation.
The model was instruction prompted to transform each consultation into a
structured clinical report while maintaining factual grounding and
patient-specificity. To reduce GPU memory requirements during inference, the
model was loaded using 4-bit NF4 quantisation with double quantisation and
bfloat16 computation.

A structured prompting strategy was used to constrain the generation process.
The prompt provided the model with the KBG clinical reference guidelines and
the corresponding doctor--parent consultation. The model was instructed to
first identify the clinical findings present in the consultation, associate
each finding with its appropriate clinical category, and then generate the
final report. This intermediate processing was performed internally and was
not included in the generated output.

To reduce unsupported clinical information, patient-specific statements were
required to originate from the consultation, while syndrome-level information
was restricted to the supplied clinical reference. Recommendations were
generated only when supported by reported patient findings. Where information
was unavailable for a category, the model was instructed to return
``N/A''. This grounding strategy reduced the likelihood of introducing
symptoms or clinical assumptions that were not supported by the source
material.

The generated reports followed a fixed 13-category schema covering major
clinical domains, including cardiology, gastroenterology, immunology,
neurology, ENT, ophthalmology, dental, education, behaviour and development,
and skeletal features, followed by screening and treatment recommendations.
For each clinical domain, the report distinguished between
\textit{patient-specific effects} and \textit{syndrome-level information}.
This consistent schema enabled systematic comparison of reports across
patients and provided a standardised target format for subsequent
distillation.

Generation was performed independently for each consultation. A relatively
low sampling temperature ($T=0.3$) was used to favour factual consistency
and adherence to the prescribed report structure. The resulting outputs
were stored in JSONL format together with the source consultation and
associated generation fields, preserving the correspondence between each
input consultation and its generated report. These consultation--report
pairs formed the teacher-generated reference dataset.

The teacher-generated reports were subsequently subjected to rubric-based
quality assessment. Reports were evaluated against predefined criteria
before being considered for subsequent student-model training and
knowledge-distillation experiments. The complete report-generation prompt,
including the clinical reference context and output schema, is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:report_prompt}.

The overall process is illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:teacher_report_generation}, showing the transformation from
the synthetic consultation and clinical reference context through structured
prompting and language-model generation to the final structured clinical
report.

\section{Evaluation of Generated Personalised Medical Reports}
This section evaluates whether teacher-generated reports are of sufficient quality to serve as training data for the student model. Reports are assessed based on factuality, coverage, medical tone, readability, and conciseness to determine their suitability for knowledge distillation.
\subsection{Factuality}


Factuality was assessed using a fine-grained, claim-level verification procedure to determine whether generated reports were supported by their designated source information. The approach was motivated by methods that decompose generated text into atomic factual units and subsequently verify each unit against an evidence source \cite{min_factscore_2023, scire_fenice_2024}. Similar claim-level approaches have also been applied to medical text, where fine-grained factuality assessment is particularly important for identifying unsupported clinical information \cite{bn_fact-controlled_2025, munnangi_factehr_2025}.

For each generated report, the factual content was first decomposed into atomic clinical claims. An atomic fact is a minimal, self-contained unit of information that expresses a single, distinct proposition and cannot be broken down further without losing its structural meaning \cite{min_factscore_2023}. A structured extraction prompt was used to identify patient-specific information explicitly supported by the doctor--parent consultation, while excluding questions, non-informative responses, inference, and external medical knowledge. Closely related details representing the same clinical fact were retained as a single claim. The complete extraction prompt is provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_claim_prompt}.

Each extracted claim was subsequently verified against its corresponding evidence source. Patient-specific claims, including reported symptoms and clinical history, were checked against the original doctor--parent consultation, whereas syndrome-level claims were checked against the KBG syndrome background information used during report generation. Verification therefore assessed whether each claim was grounded in the information available to the report-generation process rather than relying on external medical knowledge.

Each claim $c_i$ was assigned a binary verification label:

\begin{equation}
v_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } c_i \text{ is supported by its corresponding source},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

A claim was considered supported when the source explicitly conveyed the same clinical meaning, allowing for differences in wording, grammar, or clinically equivalent terminology. Unsupported, contradictory, or source-unverifiable claims were assigned a value of zero. The complete verification prompt and evaluation criteria are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_verification_prompt}.

The report-level factuality score was calculated as the proportion of supported atomic claims:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Factuality}(R)
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i}{N},
\end{equation}

where $N$ denotes the total number of extracted claims in report $R$. The resulting score ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater source-grounded factuality.

The complete evaluation process can therefore be represented as:

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    enhanced,
    colback=blue!3,
    colframe=blue!45!black,
    boxrule=0.7pt,
    arc=1.5mm,
    left=4mm,
    right=4mm,
    top=3mm,
    bottom=3mm
]
\centering
\textbf{Factuality Evaluation Pipeline}

\vspace{2mm}

\text{Patient Report}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Atomic Claims}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Source Verification}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Binary Labels}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Factuality Score}

\end{tcolorbox}

For example, a generated statement such as ``The patient experiences speech delay'' would be treated as an atomic claim and verified against the corresponding consultation. If the consultation explicitly supports the statement, the claim receives $v_i=1$; otherwise, it receives $v_i=0$. Further examples of claim decomposition, evidence sources, and verification decisions are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_verification_prompt}.

This procedure provides a fine-grained measure of source-grounded factuality and allows unsupported information to be identified at the individual claim level rather than being masked within an overall document-level judgement.

\subsection{Coverage}

Coverage was evaluated to measure the extent to which clinically relevant
information from the source material was retained in the generated reports.
Unlike factuality, which assesses whether generated claims are supported by
source evidence, coverage assesses whether relevant source information is
represented in the report \cite{noauthor_faithfulness_2026, song_finesure_2024}.
Because the report integrates information from different sources, coverage
was evaluated separately for patient-specific information, syndrome-level
background information, and recommendations.

\subsubsection{Patient-Specific Coverage}

Patient-specific coverage was assessed using a two-stage claim-based
procedure. First, clinically meaningful patient-specific claims were
extracted from the doctor--parent consultation. Only information explicitly
stated by the parent about the child was retained; doctor's questions,
\textit{N/A} responses, emotions, uncertainty, opinions, intentions, and
unsupported inferences were excluded. Complex statements were decomposed
into distinct clinical facts while avoiding duplicate claims \cite{dejl_comprehensiveness_2026, kim_atomic_2026}. The extraction prompt is
provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:patient_coverage_prompt}.

Each claim was then evaluated against the generated report. A claim was
considered covered if the report conveyed the same clinical meaning,
irrespective of differences in wording or sentence structure. Claims that
were absent, unclear, or unsupported were assigned $0$. Thus,

\begin{equation}
v_i^{P} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if claim } c_i \text{ is covered by the report},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The patient-specific coverage score was:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_P}\sum_{i=1}^{N_P}v_i^{P},
\end{equation}

where $N_P$ is the number of extracted patient-specific claims.

\subsubsection{Syndrome Background and Recommendation Coverage}

Syndrome-level coverage was evaluated using feature-specific questions.
For each clinical feature explicitly reported by the parent, two questions
were generated: one assessing coverage of relevant KBG syndrome background
information (\textit{syndrome\_background}) and one assessing coverage of
relevant recommendations, screening, treatment, monitoring, referral, or
therapy (\textit{recommendation}). Question generation was restricted to
features present in the consultation \cite{scialom_questeval_2021, moramarco_towards_2021}. The corresponding prompts are provided
in Appendix~\ref{appx:syndrome_coverage_prompt}.

Each question was evaluated against the generated report and assigned:

\begin{equation}
v_j =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if the requested information is explicitly addressed},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Separate scores were calculated for syndrome background and recommendations:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_B}\sum_{j=1}^{N_B}v_j^{B},
\qquad
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_{Rec}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{Rec}}v_j^{Rec}.
\end{equation}

Here, $N_B$ and $N_{Rec}$ denote the numbers of background and
recommendation questions, respectively. Background coverage required the
report to address how the relevant feature affects people with KBG syndrome,
while recommendation coverage required explicit, feature-relevant clinical
advice. Missing, ambiguous, or inferred information was assigned $0$.

The three component scores were retained separately and subsequently
averaged to obtain the overall coverage score:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Overall}(R)
=
\frac{
\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}(R)
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}(R)
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}(R)
}{3}.
\end{equation}

This provides separate and overall measures of retention of patient-specific
findings, syndrome-level information, and recommendations.
\subsection{Conciseness Assessment}
\label{sec:conciseness}

Conciseness was evaluated as a composite measure of information retention,
factual reliability, and report brevity. This reflects the requirement that
generated clinical reports should preserve relevant information and factual
accuracy without unnecessary verbosity.

For each report, token length $L_i$ was calculated using the tokenizer of the
evaluated model. Token lengths were min--max normalised across the evaluation
set:

\begin{equation}
L_i^{\mathrm{norm}}
=
\frac{L_i-L_{\min}}
{L_{\max}-L_{\min}},
\end{equation}

where $L_{\min}$ and $L_{\max}$ are the minimum and maximum report lengths.
A brevity factor was then defined as:

\begin{equation}
B_i = 1-L_i^{\mathrm{norm}},
\end{equation}

so that shorter reports receive higher brevity scores. The final conciseness
score was calculated using the geometric mean of coverage, factuality, and
brevity:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Conciseness}_i
=
\left(
\mathrm{Coverage}_i
\cdot
\mathrm{Factuality}_i
\cdot
B_i
\right)^{1/3}.
\end{equation}

The geometric mean ensures that high performance in one dimension cannot
fully compensate for poor performance in another. Thus, a high score requires
the report to be sufficiently informative, factually reliable, and concise.
\subsection{Readability Assessment}
\label{sec:readability}

Readability was evaluated to assess the linguistic accessibility of the
generated clinical reports using four established text-based measures. The
Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) score provides a general measure of reading ease,
with higher scores indicating greater readability \cite{flesch_new_1948}. The Flesch--Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) estimates the
educational grade level required to comprehend the text \cite{tanprasert_flesch-kincaid_2021}. The Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) index
estimates reading difficulty based primarily on the use of polysyllabic words \cite{mclaughlin_smog_1969}. The Dale--Chall score assesses readability based
on word familiarity and sentence length \cite{crossley_large-scaled_2023}.

All four measures were computed for each generated report using the cleaned
report text and the \texttt{textstat} implementation. The measures were
retained independently to capture complementary aspects of textual
difficulty and account for their different scoring interpretations. The
resulting readability scores were summarised across the evaluation set using
the mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and quartiles.
\subsection{Medical Tone Assessment}
\label{sec:medical_tone}

Medical tone was evaluated to assess whether generated reports used
professional, respectful, and patient-centred clinical language. The
assessment followed a two-stage LLM-based procedure. First, patient-specific
content was extracted from each report, including statements describing how
the condition affects the patient and recommendations for screening or
treatment. Syndrome-level background information and general medical
information were excluded. The extraction used few-shot prompting, with the
complete prompt provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:medical_tone_prompt}.

The extracted statements were then evaluated using a separate few-shot
evaluation prompt across three binary dimensions: \textit{professionalism},
\textit{respectfulness}, and \textit{patient-centred communication}. These
dimensions reflect the importance of clear, appropriate, respectful, and
patient-focused communication in clinical contexts \cite{bowden_evaluating_nodate, rattanapitoon_beyond_2025, omar_impact_2025}.
Each statement received a binary score:

\begin{equation}
s_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if statement } i \text{ satisfies criterion } j,\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $j \in \{P,R,C\}$ represents professionalism, respectfulness, and
patient-centred communication, respectively. For each report, the score for
criterion $j$ was calculated as the proportion of extracted statements
receiving a positive evaluation:

\begin{equation}
S_j =
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{ij},
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the number of extracted patient-specific statements. The three
dimensions were retained separately to distinguish different aspects of
medical tone rather than combining them into a single score. Scores were
calculated for all generated reports and subsequently summarised across the
evaluation set.
\section{Knowledge Distillation}
\subsection{Teacher and Student Models}
The proposed framework adopts a teacher--student knowledge
distillation paradigm, in which knowledge generated by a larger teacher
model is transferred to a substantially smaller student model
\cite{hinton2015distilling}. This architecture separates
knowledge generation from efficient deployment, allowing
the student to acquire task-specific capabilities from the teacher while
operating with considerably lower computational and memory requirements.

The teacher model was
\texttt{Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507}, an instruction-tuned
mixture-of-experts (MoE) model with approximately 30 billion total
parameters and approximately 3 billion activated parameters per token
\cite{qwen3technicalreport2025}. The teacher was used to generate the
synthetic doctor--patient consultations and corresponding personalised
medical reports that formed the primary source of supervision for
subsequent student training. During inference, the teacher was loaded using
4-bit NormalFloat-4 (NF4) quantisation with bfloat16 computation to reduce
memory requirements while preserving the required generation capability.

The student model was
\texttt{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct}, an instruction-tuned model containing
approximately 1.5 billion parameters \cite{qwen25technicalreport2024}. It
was selected as the compact deployment model because its substantially
smaller parameter count makes it more suitable for resource-constrained and
local inference. Rather than reproducing the full capacity of the teacher,
the student is trained to acquire the task-specific behaviour required for
personalised medical report generation.

Let $T$ denote the teacher and $S_{\theta}$ denote the student parameterised
by $\theta$. Given a consultation $x$, the teacher generates a target
response $y_T$:

\begin{equation}
y_T = T(x).
\end{equation}

The resulting teacher-generated response provides supervision for the
student, which models the conditional distribution

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y\mid x).
\end{equation}

The substantial difference in model scale provides a direct measure of the
intended efficiency gain. Let $N_T$ and $N_S$ denote the total parameter
counts of the teacher and student, respectively. The relative parameter
reduction is defined as

\begin{equation}
R = 1-\frac{N_S}{N_T}.
\end{equation}

Using approximately $30$B parameters for the teacher and $1.5$B for the
student gives

\begin{equation}
R \approx 1-\frac{1.5}{30}=0.95,
\end{equation}

corresponding to an approximately 95\% reduction in total
parameter count.

The resulting pipeline therefore consists of a large
teacher model for knowledge and supervision generation and a
compact student model for efficient task execution. The student
was subsequently established through supervised fine-tuning and further
optimised using the proposed quality-weighted distillation and
preference-based learning approaches. This provides the foundation for
investigating whether clinically relevant report-generation capabilities
can be transferred from a large model to a substantially smaller model
without compromising the desired report quality.
\subsection{Supervised Fine-Tuning of the Student Model}
\label{sec:student_sft}

Following the teacher--student model selection, the student model was first
adapted to the personalised medical report generation task using supervised
fine-tuning (SFT). This stage establishes the baseline task-specific student
before applying the subsequent quality-weighted distillation and preference
optimisation approaches. The training data consisted of
teacher-generated consultation--report pairs, where each doctor--parent
consultation was used as the input and its corresponding personalised
medical report served as the target output. This formulation allows the
student to learn the reporting structure, clinical information selection,
and response characteristics demonstrated by the teacher
\cite{hinton2015distilling}.

The student, \texttt{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct}, was adapted using
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), a parameter-efficient fine-tuning
method that introduces trainable low-rank updates while keeping the
pretrained model parameters frozen \cite{hu2021lora}. LoRA reduces the
number of parameters requiring optimisation and consequently lowers the
memory requirements of fine-tuning while retaining the pretrained
representations of the underlying language model.

For a pretrained weight matrix
$W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, LoRA parameterises its task-specific
adaptation as

\begin{equation}
W = W_0 + \Delta W,
\qquad
\Delta W = BA,
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r},
\qquad
A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k},
\qquad
r \ll \min(d,k).
\end{equation}

Here, $r$ denotes the LoRA rank. The pretrained matrix $W_0$ is frozen
during optimisation, while the low-rank matrices $A$ and $B$ are updated
to capture the task-specific parameter adaptation. In this work, LoRA
adapters were applied to the attention projection layers
(\texttt{q\_proj}, \texttt{k\_proj}, \texttt{v\_proj}, and
\texttt{o\_proj}) and the feed-forward projection layers
(\texttt{gate\_proj}, \texttt{up\_proj}, and \texttt{down\_proj}).
The complete LoRA and optimisation configuration is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

Given a training dataset

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i)
\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ represents a formatted doctor--parent consultation and $y_i$
represents the corresponding teacher-generated report, the student was
optimised using the standard autoregressive language-modelling objective.
The conditional probability of the target sequence is factorised
token-by-token as

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y_i\mid x_i)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T_i}
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right),
\end{equation}

where $T_i$ is the number of target tokens and $y_{i,<t}$ denotes the
sequence of target tokens preceding position $t$. The corresponding
negative log-likelihood objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\end{equation}

Thus, SFT minimises the token-level cross-entropy between the student's
predicted distribution and the teacher-provided target sequence. Only the
trainable LoRA parameters are updated during optimisation, while the
pretrained student parameters remain frozen \cite{hu2021lora}.

The implementation used the \texttt{SFTTrainer} framework from TRL for
causal language-model fine-tuning \cite{trl_sft}. Training was performed
with a maximum sequence length of 4096 tokens and an effective batch size
of 8, achieved through gradient accumulation. The optimisation employed
AdamW with 8-bit parameter states, a cosine learning-rate schedule, and a
warm-up phase. The student was trained for five epochs, with validation
performed at the end of each epoch. The complete training configuration,
including the LoRA rank, scaling factor, target modules, learning rate,
batch configuration, optimiser, scheduler, and sequence length, is
reported in Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

The resulting SFT model provides the principal student baseline against
which the subsequent distillation strategies are evaluated. However, the
standard SFT objective treats each training pair uniformly, irrespective
of the quality or clinical reliability of the teacher-generated target.
Consequently, lower-quality examples can contribute to the optimisation
objective with the same magnitude as higher-quality examples. This
limitation motivates the subsequent Quality-Weighted Knowledge
Distillation stage, where the contribution of individual teacher-generated
examples is explicitly modulated according to their evaluated quality.
\subsection{Quality-Weighted Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:quality_weighted_distillation}

The supervised fine-tuning stage treats all teacher-generated
consultation--report pairs as equally informative. However, the quality of
synthetic supervision can vary across examples. In the present clinical
report generation task, this variation is particularly important because
the generated reports are required to preserve clinically relevant
information, remain factually grounded, and maintain an appropriate medical
tone. Consequently, uniformly weighting all training examples may cause
lower-quality teacher outputs to exert the same optimisation influence as
higher-quality outputs.

To address this limitation, a \textbf{quality-weighted response-based
knowledge distillation} objective was introduced. The method retains the
hard teacher-generated responses used during SFT, while incorporating an
example-level quality signal into the optimisation objective. This extends
the teacher--student distillation framework introduced by
Hinton et al.~\cite{hinton2015distilling} by explicitly accounting for the
reliability of individual teacher demonstrations.

Let the teacher-generated training dataset be

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i)
\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ denotes a doctor--parent consultation and $y_i$ denotes the
corresponding personalised report generated by the teacher. For each
report, three independently evaluated quality dimensions were obtained:
clinical information coverage ($C_i$), factuality ($F_i$), and medical
tone ($M_i$). Each dimension was represented as a normalised score in
$[0,1]$.

A single supervision-quality score was obtained by combining these
dimensions using their geometric mean:

\begin{equation}
q_i =
\left(
C_i F_i M_i
\right)^{1/3}.
\label{eq:quality_score}
\end{equation}

The geometric mean was selected because it imposes a multiplicative
interaction between the quality dimensions. In contrast to an arithmetic
mean, a high score in one dimension cannot fully compensate for a
substantially lower score in another. Thus, the quality signal favours
teacher demonstrations that provide a balanced combination of clinical
coverage, factual reliability, and appropriate medical communication.

For an autoregressive student model parameterised by $\theta$, the
conditional probability of a target report is factorised as

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y_i\mid x_i)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T_i}
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right),
\end{equation}

where $T_i$ is the number of target tokens and $y_{i,<t}$ denotes the
target-token prefix preceding position $t$. The conventional SFT
negative log-likelihood objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\label{eq:sft_loss}
\end{equation}

The proposed quality-weighted objective modifies this loss by introducing
the example-specific quality coefficient $q_i$:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QKD}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
q_i
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\label{eq:qkd_loss}
\end{equation}

Hence, the gradient contribution of training example $i$ is scaled by its
quality score:

\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathrm{QKD}}
=
q_i
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathrm{SFT}}.
\end{equation}

This formulation provides a direct mechanism through which the quality of
teacher supervision influences student learning. A teacher-generated
example with a high value of $q_i$ contributes strongly to the optimisation
gradient, whereas an example with a lower quality score contributes less.
Importantly, the weighting is applied at the example level, meaning that
all target tokens belonging to a given consultation--report pair inherit
the same quality coefficient. The autoregressive token-prediction
objective itself is therefore unchanged; only the relative importance of
the training examples is modified.

The proposed approach can consequently be viewed as
\textit{quality-aware selective knowledge transfer}. Standard SFT assumes
that every teacher demonstration provides equally reliable supervision,
whereas the proposed objective induces a quality-dependent weighting over
the teacher-generated dataset. The student is therefore encouraged to
prioritise behaviours demonstrated by examples that simultaneously exhibit
strong clinical coverage, factual consistency, and appropriate medical
tone.

This formulation also establishes a direct link between the evaluation and
distillation stages of the framework. Rather than using coverage,
factuality, and medical tone solely as post-hoc evaluation metrics, their
combined score is transformed into a training signal that determines the
relative contribution of each synthetic example. The evaluation framework
therefore functions both as a measure of generated-report quality and as a
mechanism for controlling the transfer of teacher knowledge.

The student architecture, LoRA parameterisation, and underlying
autoregressive training procedure remain unchanged from the SFT baseline.
The principal methodological difference is therefore the introduction of
the quality-dependent weighting term $q_i$ in Equation~\ref{eq:qkd_loss}.
The complete optimisation and implementation configuration is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

This formulation enables a controlled comparison between uniformly
weighted SFT and quality-weighted distillation, isolating the effect of
supervision quality on the transfer of teacher-generated knowledge to the
compact student model.
\section{Direct Preference Optimisation}

\label{sec:dpo}

Following supervised fine-tuning and quality-weighted knowledge
distillation, Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) was investigated as a
preference-based optimisation stage. Unlike SFT, which learns from a single
target response, and quality-weighted distillation, which assigns different
importance to teacher-generated examples, DPO learns from \textit{relative
preferences} between two candidate responses for the same input
\cite{rafailov2023direct}.

For each consultation $x_i$, a preference pair was constructed consisting
of a preferred response $y_i^{+}$ and a rejected response $y_i^{-}$.
The preferred response represents the higher-quality candidate according
to the established report-evaluation framework, while the rejected response
represents the comparatively lower-quality candidate. The resulting
preference dataset is therefore defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i^{+},y_i^{-})
\right\}_{i=1}^{N}.
\end{equation}

This formulation allows the student to learn not only what constitutes a
valid response, but also which of two plausible responses is preferable
for the same clinical input.

\subsubsection*{DPO Objective}

DPO provides a direct optimisation objective for preference learning without
requiring a separately trained reward model
\cite{rafailov2023direct}. Let $\pi_{\theta}$ denote the trainable student
policy and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ denote a frozen reference policy. For a
given consultation $x_i$, the DPO objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)
=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+},y^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}}
\left[
\log
\sigma
\left(
\beta
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}\mid x)}
-
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}\mid x)}
\right]
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:dpo_loss}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function and
$\beta$ controls the strength of the preference optimisation relative to
the reference policy.

For an autoregressive language model, the sequence-level log-probability
appearing in Equation~\ref{eq:dpo_loss} is decomposed into token-level
conditional probabilities:

\begin{equation}
\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\log
\pi_{\theta}
\left(
y_t
\mid
x,y_{<t}
\right).
\end{equation}

Consequently, the optimisation compares the relative likelihood assigned
by the student to the preferred and rejected responses, while simultaneously
measuring their deviation from the reference policy.

The central quantity in the DPO objective is therefore the relative
log-probability difference

\begin{equation}
\Delta_i =
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i^{+}\mid x_i)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{+}\mid x_i)}
\right]
-
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i^{-}\mid x_i)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{-}\mid x_i)}
\right].
\end{equation}

Minimising Equation~\ref{eq:dpo_loss} encourages $\Delta_i$ to increase,
thereby increasing the student's relative preference for $y_i^{+}$ over
$y_i^{-}$. The reference-policy terms constrain this optimisation by
penalising uncontrolled deviation from the behaviour represented by the
reference model. This provides a preference-learning mechanism without
requiring explicit reward-model training or reinforcement-learning policy
updates \cite{rafailov2023direct}.

\subsubsection*{Preference Construction}

Preference pairs were constructed from candidate personalised reports
generated for the same doctor--parent consultation. The previously defined
quality evaluation framework provided the basis for determining the
relative quality of candidate responses. Coverage, factuality, and medical
tone were used to assess the clinical suitability of the candidates.

For a candidate report $y$, the corresponding composite quality score was
defined as

\begin{equation}
q(y)
=
\left(
C(y)\,F(y)\,M(y)
\right)^{1/3},
\end{equation}

where $C(y)$, $F(y)$, and $M(y)$ represent the coverage, factuality, and
medical-tone scores, respectively. For two candidate responses generated
for the same consultation, $y_a$ and $y_b$, the preferred response was
selected according to their relative quality:

\begin{equation}
y^{+}
=
\arg\max_{y\in\{y_a,y_b\}} q(y),
\qquad
y^{-}
=
\arg\min_{y\in\{y_a,y_b\}} q(y).
\end{equation}

This construction converts the multidimensional report-quality assessment
into pairwise supervision. Rather than requiring an absolute quality label
during optimisation, DPO receives only the ordering between the preferred
and rejected responses.

\subsubsection*{Relationship to SFT and Quality-Weighted Distillation}

The three optimisation stages differ in the form of supervision supplied
to the student. SFT learns from individual input--target pairs and
minimises the negative log-likelihood of the teacher-generated target.
Quality-weighted distillation retains this formulation but scales each
example according to its composite quality score. DPO instead provides
pairwise preference supervision and explicitly optimises the relative
probability of preferred versus rejected responses.

Formally, the progression can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
\text{SFT}
\rightarrow
\text{quality-weighted response distillation}
\rightarrow
\text{preference optimisation}.
\end{equation}

SFT therefore answers the question of \textit{what response should be
generated}, quality-weighted distillation additionally determines
\textit{which demonstrations should have greater training influence}, and
DPO learns \textit{which of two candidate responses should be preferred}.
This provides three progressively different mechanisms for transferring
and refining the teacher-derived behaviour of the compact student model.

Unlike reinforcement-learning-based preference optimisation, DPO does not
require explicit reward-model fitting or online policy sampling during the
optimisation stage \cite{rafailov2023direct}. Its direct likelihood-based
objective makes it particularly suitable for controlled offline
optimisation using the preference pairs constructed from the generated
clinical reports.

The student model and parameter-efficient LoRA configuration remained
consistent with the preceding optimisation stages, allowing the effect of
the preference objective to be evaluated without introducing changes to
the underlying student architecture. The complete DPO hyperparameters and
preference-pair construction configuration are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.
\section{Evaluation of Student-Generated Reports}
\label{sec:student_evaluation}

Following supervised fine-tuning, quality-weighted knowledge distillation,
and preference optimisation, the resulting student models were evaluated
on a held-out test set to determine whether the knowledge transferred from
the teacher was retained while reducing model size and computational cost.
The same evaluation framework established for the teacher-generated reports
was applied to the student-generated reports, ensuring that performance
could be compared consistently across models.

The evaluation was performed across two complementary aspects: 
\textit{clinical report quality} and \textit{output-level knowledge
transfer}. Clinical quality was assessed using the previously defined
coverage, factuality, conciseness, readability, and medical-tone measures.
These metrics were applied without modification to the student outputs,
allowing the quality of the distilled reports to be compared directly with
the corresponding teacher/reference reports. Their definitions and
calculation procedures are provided in the preceding evaluation sections.

In addition, output-level knowledge transfer was assessed using lexical and
semantic similarity between student-generated reports and the corresponding
teacher/reference reports. ROUGE and BLEU were used to measure lexical
correspondence, while BERTScore and embedding-based similarity were used to
assess semantic alignment. These measures provide complementary evidence
of whether the student reproduces the information and semantic content
expressed by the teacher, while allowing for differences in surface wording
\cite{lin2004rouge,papineni2002bleu,zhang2020bertscore}.

The evaluation was conducted independently for the SFT, quality-weighted
distillation, and DPO student models. For each model, the same held-out
test consultations were used and reports were generated using a consistent
inference procedure. This controlled evaluation isolates differences
arising from the training strategy rather than differences in the
evaluation data or protocol.

complement the quality evaluation by determining whether improvements in
efficiency are achieved without unacceptable degradation in report quality.

The overall evaluation therefore examines three related outcomes:
\textit{clinical quality}, \textit{knowledge transfer}, and
\textit{computational efficiency}. The resulting measurements enable direct
comparison of SFT, quality-weighted distillation, and DPO and provide the
basis for determining whether the proposed distillation strategies retain
teacher-level behaviour while enabling substantially more efficient
student-model inference.

\chapter{Atomic Claim Factuality Verification Prompt}
\label{appx:factuality_verification_prompt}

The following zero-shot prompt was used to evaluate the factual
grounding of the atomic clinical claims extracted from each generated
patient report. Each claim was verified against its designated
Reference Document, with patient-specific claims evaluated against
patient-level source information and syndrome-specific claims evaluated
against the corresponding KBG syndrome reference information.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    breaklines=true,
    breakatwhitespace=false,
    basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,
    columns=fullflexible,
    keepspaces=true
]
SYSTEM

You are an expert clinical factuality evaluator.

Your task is to determine whether each atomic claim is directly
supported by the provided Reference Document.

For each claim, output:

1. "claim": The exact claim being evaluated.
2. "supported": "YES" or "NO".

Strict evaluation criteria:

- Use ONLY the provided Reference Document.
- Do not use external medical knowledge.
- Do not make clinical assumptions or logical extensions.
- A claim is YES only if the information is explicitly stated or is
  a direct paraphrase of information in the Reference Document.

  claim as NO.

  in the Reference Document.
- Evaluate syndrome-specific claims only against syndrome information
  in the Reference Document.

Claim: "The patient has hearing difficulties." -> YES
Claim: "The patient may need hearing aids." -> NO
Claim: "The patient may have permanent hearing loss." -> NO

You MUST respond ONLY with valid JSON:

\chapter{Patient-Specific Coverage Evaluation Prompts}
\label{appx:patient_coverage_prompt}

The following prompts were used to assess coverage of patient-specific
clinical information. A two-stage, zero-shot instruction-prompting strategy
was employed. The first stage performed atomic patient-specific clinical
claim extraction from the doctor--parent consultation, while the second
stage independently verified whether each extracted claim was represented
in the generated patient report. No task-specific demonstrations or
labelled examples were provided during either stage.

\section*{Stage 1: Patient-Specific Claim Extraction}

A zero-shot instruction-based extraction prompt was used to identify
clinically meaningful patient-specific claims. Only information explicitly
stated by the parent about the child was retained. The prompt excluded
doctor's questions, \textit{N/A} responses, emotions, uncertainty, opinions,
intentions, unsupported inferences, and duplicate claims. Complex statements
were decomposed into distinct clinical facts while closely related
information was merged where appropriate.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    breaklines=true,
    breakatwhitespace=false,
    basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,
    columns=fullflexible,
    keepspaces=true
]
SYSTEM

You are a clinical information extraction assistant.
Extract patient-specific clinical claims from a doctor-parent
consultation.

Rules:

- Extract only clinical information explicitly stated by the parent
  about the child.
- Do not extract the parent's emotions, uncertainty, opinions,
  concerns, intentions, or plans.
- Do not infer information or use external medical knowledge.
- Ignore doctor's questions and N/A responses.
- Each claim should represent one clinically meaningful fact.
- Split a statement only when it contains multiple distinct clinical facts.
- Do not create multiple claims that describe the same clinical fact
  using different wording.
- Do not split out minor details such as duration, examples,
  comparisons, or context unless they represent an important
  clinical fact.
- Merge closely related symptoms or details into a single meaningful
  clinical claim.
- Keep the original meaning with minimal rewriting.
- Return only valid JSON containing the extracted claims.
\end{lstlisting}

The consultation was provided as the user input:

\begin{lstlisting}[
    breaklines=true,
    breakatwhitespace=false,
    basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,
    columns=fullflexible,
    keepspaces=true
]
USER

Now extract atomic patient claims from this consultation:

{doctor-parent consultation}
\end{lstlisting}


\section*{Stage 2: Claim-Level Coverage Verification}

Each extracted claim was subsequently evaluated independently against the
generated Clinical Patient Report using a zero-shot semantic verification
prompt. The evaluator was instructed to assess whether the report conveyed
the same clinical meaning as the target claim, allowing differences in
wording, grammar, terminology, and sentence structure. Exact lexical
matching was therefore not required.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    breaklines=true,
    breakatwhitespace=false,
    basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,
    columns=fullflexible,
    keepspaces=true
]
SYSTEM

You are a clinical summary coverage evaluator.
Your task is to evaluate whether a generated patient summary covers
a clinical claim extracted from a doctor-parent consultation.

Rules:

- Evaluate ONLY the provided Clinical Patient Report and Target Claim.
- Do not use external medical knowledge or add assumptions.
- Mark YES if the patient report conveys the same clinical meaning
  as the target claim, even if different wording is used.
- Treat clinically equivalent expressions as the same information.
- Do not require exact wording.
- Ignore minor differences in wording, grammar, tense, pronouns,
  or non-essential details.
- Mark NO if the clinical information in the claim is not present
  or not supported by the patient report.

Examples:

"tooth pain when eating cold foods" ↔
"tooth sensitivity to cold foods" → YES

"valve issue" ↔
"heart valve abnormality" → YES

"delayed milestones" ↔
"takes longer to achieve milestones" → YES

You MUST respond ONLY with valid JSON:

{
  "results": [
    {
      "claim": "string",
      "covered": "YES/NO"
    }
  ]
}
\end{lstlisting}

The generated report and target claim were provided as the user input:

    keepspaces=true

{generated patient report}

Target Claim:

{extracted patient-specific claim}

Determine whether the patient report includes this clinical information.
Return the evaluation only in the required JSON format.
\end{lstlisting}

\chapter{Syndrome Background and Recommendation Coverage Prompt}
\label{appx:syndrome_coverage_prompt}

The following prompts were used to evaluate coverage of syndrome-level
background information and clinical recommendations in the generated
personalised reports. The procedure consisted of two stages. First, a
few-shot prompting strategy was used to generate feature-specific evaluation
questions from the doctor--parent consultation. For each clinical feature
explicitly reported by the parent, one question assessed coverage of
syndrome-level background information and one assessed coverage of relevant
recommendations. Second, a zero-shot evaluation prompt was used to determine
whether the generated report explicitly addressed each question.

\section*{Question Generation Prompt}

The question-generation prompt used the consultation as input and was
provided with one illustrative doctor--parent consultation and its expected
question set as few-shot examples.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    breaklines=true,
    breakatwhitespace=false,
    basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,
    columns=fullflexible,
    keepspaces=true
]
SYSTEM

You are a clinical question generation assistant.
Your task is to read a doctor-parent consultation and generate evaluation
questions to assess whether the patient report provides:

1. Background information about how the relevant feature affects other
   people with KBG syndrome.
2. Relevant recommendations, screening, treatment, or management advice
   for that feature.

Rules:

- Use only information explicitly stated by the parent.
- Ignore doctor's questions and all N/A responses.
- Do not use external medical knowledge.
- Do not infer missing information.
- For each clinical feature mentioned by the parent:
  - Generate one syndrome_background question.
  - Generate one recommendation question.
- Keep questions short, direct, and specific.

[Illustrative doctor-parent consultation]

    "category": "recommendation"
  }
  ...

USER

\end{lstlisting}

The complete consultation and corresponding few-shot example used during
question generation are retained in the experimental implementation.

\section*{Question Validation Prompt}

The generated questions were subsequently evaluated against the corresponding
patient report using a zero-shot prompting strategy. The evaluator was
instructed to rely exclusively on the generated report and not to introduce
external medical knowledge or infer information not explicitly stated.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    breaklines=true,
    breakatwhitespace=false,
    basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,
    columns=fullflexible,
    keepspaces=true
]
SYSTEM

You are a clinical report coverage evaluator.
Your task is to determine whether a patient report contains information
that answers the given question.

Rules:

- Use only information explicitly stated in the patient report.
- Do not use external medical knowledge.
- Do not infer missing information.
- Give YES only if the report provides the information requested.
- Give NO if the information is missing, unclear, or not directly addressed.
- For recommendation questions, give YES if the report provides any
  explicit recommendation, screening, monitoring, treatment, referral,
  therapy, or management advice relevant to the feature.
- For syndrome_background questions, give YES only if the report explains
  how the relevant feature affects people with KBG syndrome.
- Preserve the category field from each input question.

Return ONLY valid JSON:

{
  "results": [
    {
      "question": "string",
      "category": "syndrome_background or recommendation",
      "covered": "YES/NO"
    }
  ]
}

USER

Patient Report:

{generated patient report}

Questions:

{generated evaluation questions}

Evaluate every question against the report and return the results only
in the required JSON format.
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507}. The model was loaded using 4-bit
NormalFloat4 (NF4) quantisation with bfloat16 computation. Deterministic
generation was used by disabling sampling.
