\section{Personalised Clinical Report Generation}
\label{sec:personalised_clinical_report_generation}

\subsection{Neurogenetic and Neurodevelopmental Disorders}
\label{sec:neurogenetic_disorders}

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a heterogeneous group of
conditions affecting brain development and functioning, with consequences
for cognition, communication, learning, behaviour, and motor and social
development. Genetic factors contribute substantially to many NDDs,
resulting in considerable variation in clinical presentation and severity
\cite{gao_genetic_2025,assadourian_precision_2025}. This heterogeneity
makes individual patient representation important when describing and
managing these conditions.

KBG syndrome (KBGS) is a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder primarily
associated with pathogenic variants or deletions involving the
\textit{ANKRD11} gene. It encompasses a broad spectrum of features,
including developmental delay, intellectual and learning difficulties,
behavioural and neurological abnormalities, craniofacial characteristics,
macrodontia, short stature, and skeletal abnormalities
\cite{martinez-cayuelas_clinical_2023}. The presence and severity of these
features vary between individuals \cite{martinez-cayuelas_clinical_2023},
making generic disease descriptions insufficient to fully represent an
individual patient's clinical profile.

For families affected by rare genetic disorders, patient-oriented reports
can consolidate complex clinical information into an accessible
representation of the individual's condition and support communication
with healthcare professionals \cite{low_identifying_2025,
burbury_exploring_2025}. This motivates automated systems capable of
generating personalised clinical reports from patient-specific information.

\subsection{Clinical Personalisation and Grounding}
\label{sec:clinical_personalisation_grounding}

Personalised clinical reporting requires generated reports to preserve
patient-specific information while appropriately incorporating general
knowledge about the underlying condition. Generated claims should therefore
remain grounded in identifiable evidence rather than relying solely on a
model's general medical knowledge \cite{yang_knowledge_2022}.This is
particularly important because information that is medically plausible for
a disorder in general may still be incorrect when attributed to an
individual patient \cite{rossi_future_2023}. 

In this study, grounding is operationalised through two complementary
sources: the doctor--parent consultation provides patient-specific evidence,
while the KBG syndrome clinical reference provides syndrome-level knowledge.
This separation supports personalised reporting while providing a basis for
assessing whether generated reports retain relevant information without
introducing unsupported patient-specific claims.

\subsection{LLM-Based Clinical Report Generation}
\label{sec:llm_clinical_report_generation}

Large language models (LLMs) have expanded automated clinical text
generation across tasks including clinical summarisation, medical
documentation, and patient information generation
\cite{yuan_continued_2024,rehman_advancement_2025,alabed_large_2026,
che_llm-driven_2026,makram_ai_2024}. Their ability to condition outputs on
clinical context makes them particularly relevant to personalised report
generation, where patient information can be transformed into structured,
patient-specific narratives. For example, Ganzinger et al.
\cite{ganzinger_automated_2025} generated German discharge summaries from
structured electronic health records, while Tariq et al.
\cite{tariq_patient_2024} developed patient-sensitive radiology summaries
adapted to the intended audience. These studies demonstrate the potential
of LLMs for context-sensitive clinical reporting.

In rare-disease settings, LLMs can further combine patient-specific
observations with condition-level knowledge to generate clinical findings
and recommendations \cite{piya_contextual_2025}. However, fluent generation does not guarantee
clinical reliability, as LLM-generated clinical text may contain
hallucinations, omissions, or inconsistencies \cite{zeba_mitigating_2026}. Clinical evaluation must therefore consider factual grounding
and completeness alongside linguistic quality \cite{dahlberg_measuring_2026}.

Large LLMs also present deployment challenges in clinical environments.
Processing sensitive patient information through external cloud services
raises privacy, governance, and regulatory concerns
\cite{annas_hipaa_2003,noauthor_eu_nodate,zhong_considerations_2025,
al-dhabyani_data_2026}, while local deployment requires substantial memory,
computational resources, and specialised hardware
\cite{li_collm_2024}. 
This creates a trade-off between model capability and practical
deployability, motivating investigation of smaller models that can support
local inference with lower resource requirements.

\subsection{Small Language Models}
\label{sec:small_language_models}

Small Language Models (SLMs) offer an alternative to large language models
with lower memory, storage, and inference requirements
\cite{wang_comprehensive_2025,garg_rise_2026}. This makes them attractive for
healthcare applications requiring resource-efficient and potentially local
processing of sensitive clinical information \cite{garg_rise_2026}.

Compact models have demonstrated competitive performance on specialised
medical tasks, including medical reasoning and biomedical language
processing \cite{kim_small_2025,rohanian_effectiveness_2022}. However, their
smaller capacity can limit reasoning ability, knowledge coverage, and
generation quality \cite{turki_evaluating_2025,wang_comprehensive_2025}.


Knowledge distillation provides a means of addressing this capability gap by
transferring useful behaviour from a larger teacher to a smaller student
\cite{gou_knowledge_2021}. In clinical report generation, teacher-generated
demonstrations can expose a compact model to task-specific reporting
behaviour while retaining the deployment advantages of smaller models.
However, teacher outputs may vary in quality and can contain inaccurate or
incomplete information \cite{woo_synthetic_2025}. This motivates considering teacher-response
quality when transferring clinical reporting capabilities to compact models.













\section{Knowledge Distillation}
\subsection{Fundamentals of Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:kd_fundamentals}

Knowledge distillation (KD) is a knowledge-transfer and model-compression
technique in which a smaller student model learns from a larger and
typically more capable teacher model \cite{hinton_distilling_2015,
wang_knowledge_2022}. Rather than directly modifying the student through
parameter reduction techniques such as pruning or quantisation, KD transfers
information learned by the teacher through additional supervision signals,
allowing the student to acquire aspects of the teacher's behaviour beyond
conventional hard targets \cite{hinton_distilling_2015,mansourian_comprehensive_2025}.

The general KD framework consists of a teacher that provides informative
training signals and a student that is optimised to reproduce the desired
teacher behaviour while retaining the computational advantages of its
smaller architecture \cite{wang_knowledge_2022}. The transferred knowledge
may take different forms, including output distributions, generated
responses, intermediate representations, or other teacher-derived signals.
The choice of transferred knowledge therefore depends on the teacher--student
setting and the objective of distillation.

The effectiveness of KD depends on how knowledge is defined, what level of
access is available to the teacher, and how the transferred information is
used to train the student \cite{mansourian_comprehensive_2025}. These
considerations have led to several complementary classifications of KD,
including distinctions based on the training configuration, teacher access,
and form of transferred knowledge. The approaches most relevant to
language-model distillation are discussed below.

\subsection{Distillation Paradigms}
\label{sec:distillation_paradigms}

Knowledge distillation can be characterised according to the training
configuration, level of access to the teacher, and form of knowledge
transferred. These perspectives describe complementary aspects of the
teacher--student relationship rather than mutually exclusive categories
\cite{wang_knowledge_2022,mansourian_comprehensive_2025}.

Based on the training configuration, \textit{offline distillation} uses a
pretrained teacher whose parameters remain fixed while the student learns
from teacher-derived supervision. This is the most common setting and is
particularly suitable when a capable teacher has already been trained
\cite{hinton_distilling_2015,wang_knowledge_2022}. In \textit{online
distillation}, teacher and student models are trained jointly, allowing
knowledge to be transferred dynamically during training
\cite{chen_online_2020}. \textit{Self-distillation} instead transfers
knowledge within the same model, for example across different training
stages or model components, without requiring a separate larger teacher
\cite{furlanello_born_2018,zhang_be_2019}.

Distillation can also be distinguished by the level of access available to
the teacher. \textit{White-box distillation} uses internal teacher
information, such as logits, hidden representations, or attention patterns,
whereas \textit{black-box distillation} relies only on information exposed
through the teacher's outputs \cite{donisch_inference_2024,shi_knowledge_2025}.
Black-box approaches are particularly relevant to large language models
whose parameters and internal representations may be inaccessible, as
teacher-generated responses can instead serve as the transferable
supervision.

Finally, the transferred knowledge may be represented as teacher responses,
output probability distributions, or intermediate representations.
Accordingly, \textit{response-based}, \textit{logit-based}, and
\textit{feature-based} distillation provide different levels of supervision
\cite{yang_categories_2023,habib_knowledge_2024,park_relational_2019}.
For generative language models, response-based distillation is especially
relevant because the teacher's generated text can directly provide
task-specific supervision without requiring access to internal model
states. This paradigm therefore forms the basis for the LLM-focused
distillation considered in the following subsection.

\subsection{Response-Based Distillation and Teacher-Generated Supervision}
\label{sec:response_based_distillation_llms}

Response-based distillation transfers knowledge from a teacher LLM to a
smaller student through teacher-generated responses rather than internal
representations or logits. A common approach is to generate
instruction--response pairs with a teacher and use them as supervision for
student fine-tuning \cite{chen_distilling_2021,yang_categories_2023,
wang_self-instruct_2023}. This is particularly suitable for LLMs because
task-specific behaviours, such as instruction following, response
structure, and domain knowledge, can be transferred through the teacher's
observable outputs.

For clinical report generation, a teacher can generate reports conditioned
on patient-specific information and relevant clinical knowledge, providing
task-specific supervision for a compact student model \cite{woo_synthetic_2025}. However, the student learns directly from teacher
outputs rather than independently verified targets. Consequently, errors,
omissions, unsupported claims, or undesirable patterns in teacher responses
may be incorporated into the student's training data
\cite{zhang_lifting_2023, montenegro_what_2025}.

The effectiveness of response-based distillation therefore depends not
only on teacher capability but also on the quality of individual responses.
Teacher-generated examples may vary in factual correctness, information
coverage, and clinical communication quality \cite{davidson_reasoning-driven_2026}.
A straightforward response to this variability is to filter out
low-quality examples; however, this can also discard potentially useful
training information. An alternative is to assign greater training
importance to higher-quality responses while reducing the contribution of
lower-quality ones \cite{kuo_not_2025}. This provides the
motivation for quality-aware knowledge distillation, in which the quality of
teacher-generated supervision is explicitly incorporated into the learning
process.
\subsection{Quality-Aware Knowledge Distillation and Research Gap}
\label{sec:quality_aware_kd_research_gap}

The effectiveness of response-based knowledge distillation depends not only
on teacher capability but also on the quality of the supervision it provides.
Teacher-generated responses may vary in factual correctness, relevance,
information coverage, and consistency, making it problematic to treat all
examples as equally reliable. Existing work has therefore explored
quality-aware strategies including data filtering, example selection, and
quality- or importance-weighted supervised fine-tuning
\cite{CITATION_NEEDED_QUALITY_AWARE_SFT,
CITATION_NEEDED_WEIGHTED_SYNTHETIC_DATA,
CITATION_NEEDED_IMPORTANCE_WEIGHTED_SFT}. These approaches demonstrate that
the contribution of individual training examples can be adjusted according
to their estimated usefulness rather than assuming uniform supervision.

A key distinction is between filtering and weighting. Filtering removes
examples that fail a predefined quality criterion and can reduce the
propagation of unreliable teacher outputs. However, it imposes a binary
decision on examples that may contain both useful and problematic
information. Weighting provides a more flexible alternative by allowing
examples to contribute to training to different degrees. Higher-quality
responses can therefore exert greater influence, while lower-quality
responses can remain available as weaker supervision rather than being
discarded entirely. [CITATION NEEDED: Literature comparing or motivating
quality-based filtering and graded/weighted supervision.]

This distinction is particularly relevant to personalised clinical report
generation, where quality cannot necessarily be captured by a single
criterion. A report may be factually accurate but omit important
patient-specific information, or contain appropriate clinical content while
being unnecessarily verbose or poorly expressed for the intended audience.
Clinical language-generation research consequently increasingly evaluates
outputs across multiple dimensions, including factuality, completeness,
relevance, and communication quality. [CITATION NEEDED: Multidimensional
evaluation framework for clinical LLM or medical text generation.]

The literature therefore provides mechanisms for incorporating example
quality into model training, but leaves an important question concerning
how quality should be defined and operationalised for teacher-generated
clinical reports. In particular, limited attention has been given to
combining clinically meaningful, multi-dimensional quality assessment with
response-based knowledge distillation for personalised medical report
generation. [CITATION NEEDED: Existing work on quality-aware LLM
distillation in clinical or medical generation.] This suggests that the
quality signal used to weight supervision may be as important as the
weighting mechanism itself.

Motivated by this gap, this thesis investigates whether a clinically
motivated quality rubric can be used to determine the relative contribution
of teacher-generated reports during distillation. Rather than treating all
teacher responses uniformly or removing lower-quality examples through hard
filtering, the proposed approach retains the generated supervision while
varying its influence according to estimated quality. This motivates the
following research question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Can quality-aware knowledge distillation improve the clinical quality
of reports generated by compact language models compared with conventional
supervised fine-tuning?}
\end{quote}
\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Evaluation of Clinical Report Generation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The evaluation of generated clinical reports is challenging because clinical
quality extends beyond linguistic fluency or similarity to a reference text.
A report may be well written while containing unsupported patient-specific
claims, omitting relevant clinical information, or using inappropriate
language for its intended audience. Evaluation therefore requires
complementary measures that capture distinct dimensions of clinical quality.

\subsection{Clinical Report Quality Dimensions}
\label{sec:clinical_report_quality_dimensions}

Clinical report quality is inherently multidimensional, as different
criteria capture different aspects of a report's clinical usefulness. In
this study, five dimensions are considered: factuality, coverage,
conciseness, readability, and medical tone.

\textit{Factuality} assesses whether the generated report accurately reflects
the available clinical evidence without introducing false, contradictory,
or unsupported claims \cite{munnangi_factehr_2025}. \textit{Coverage}
assesses whether relevant information from the available evidence is
adequately represented in the generated report \cite{moramarco_towards_2021}.
Together, these dimensions address whether a report is both clinically
correct and sufficiently informative.

\textit{Conciseness} assesses whether relevant information is communicated
efficiently without unnecessary repetition, irrelevant content, or
excessive detail \cite{schoonbeek_quality_2025}. \textit{Readability}
concerns the clarity and coherence of the report for its intended audience,
while \textit{medical tone} assesses whether the language is professional,
clinically appropriate, and suitably patient-oriented
\cite{seo_evaluation_2024}. These dimensions capture the communicative
quality of a report beyond its factual content.

No single dimension is therefore sufficient to characterise overall
clinical report quality. A report may be factually accurate but incomplete,
comprehensive but unnecessarily verbose, or clinically appropriate but
difficult for its intended audience to understand. This multidimensional
nature motivates the use of structured evaluation criteria capable of
assessing different aspects of generated clinical text independently.

\subsection{Challenges in Clinical Text Evaluation and Rubric-Based LLM Judging}
\label{sec:clinical_text_evaluation_llm_judging}

Evaluating generated clinical text is challenging because conventional
automatic metrics do not directly capture the multidimensional nature of
clinical quality. Lexical measures such as ROUGE and BLEU primarily assess
overlap with a reference text and may therefore penalise clinically valid
paraphrases while failing to identify unsupported or medically incorrect
content \cite{noauthor_semantic_2025}.Human evaluation can provide more meaningful
judgements of clinical quality, but it is costly, time-consuming, and
difficult to scale across large numbers of generated reports \cite{yan_automatic_2026}. 

Rubric-based evaluation provides a structured approach by defining explicit
criteria and scoring guidelines for each quality dimension. Rather than
reducing report quality to similarity with a reference, a rubric can assess
properties such as factuality, coverage, conciseness, readability, and
medical tone independently \cite{hashemi_llm-rubric_2024}. This makes
rubric-based evaluation particularly suitable for clinical report
generation, where different types of errors may have different clinical
implications.

LLM-as-a-Judge extends rubric-based evaluation by using a language model to
assess generated reports against predefined criteria. This provides a
scalable means of evaluating qualitative properties that are difficult to
capture through conventional automatic metrics
\cite{zheng_judging_2023,fan_sedareval_2024}. However, LLM-based evaluation
is not inherently reliable. Judgements may be affected by the formulation
of the rubric and prompt, evaluator bias, variability between evaluation
runs, or preferences for fluent responses over clinically accurate ones
\cite{pombal_self-preference_2026, norman_reliability_2026}. 

These limitations are particularly important for clinical text, where a
medically plausible statement may still be unsupported by the available
patient evidence. Reliable evaluation therefore requires clearly defined
criteria and consistent judging procedures that distinguish factual
grounding from general linguistic quality \cite{li_llm-as--judge_2026}. 
Consequently, rubric-based LLM evaluation should be viewed as a structured
and scalable complement to, rather than a complete replacement for,
conventional automatic and human evaluation. In this study, explicit
quality criteria are used to guide the assessment of generated reports,
providing a common framework for comparing different models across the
clinical and communicative dimensions relevant to personalised report
generation.

\subsection{Automatic Evaluation and Model Efficiency}
\label{sec:automatic_evaluation_efficiency}

Automatic evaluation metrics provide complementary quantitative evidence
for assessing generated clinical reports. Lexical measures such as ROUGE
\cite{lin_rouge_2004} and BLEU \cite{ward_bleu_2001} quantify overlap between
generated and reference texts, while semantic measures such as BERTScore
\cite{zhang_bertscore_2020} and embedding-based similarity assess alignment
at the representation or meaning level. However, these measures primarily
capture textual or semantic correspondence and do not directly establish
clinical factuality, patient-specific coverage, or appropriateness of
communication \cite{dahlberg_measuring_2026}.

Consequently, automatic metrics are most useful when interpreted alongside
clinical quality assessment rather than as standalone measures of report
quality. They can provide consistent quantitative comparisons between
models, while rubric-based evaluation captures dimensions that are less
amenable to conventional automatic measurement.

For compact language models, evaluation must also consider whether quality
improvements are achieved with meaningful reductions in computational cost.
Relevant efficiency measures include parameter count, memory usage,
inference time, and token generation speed, which provide complementary
indications of model size, computational requirements, and practical
deployability \cite{wani_benchmarking_2026, pham_slm-bench_2025}.

Considering quality and efficiency together is particularly important when
comparing a large teacher model with a compact student model. A student
model that produces clinically useful reports while substantially reducing
memory and inference requirements may offer greater practical value than a
model that achieves higher quality at significantly greater computational
cost. Thus, combining clinical quality assessment, automatic text metrics,
and efficiency measures provides a more comprehensive basis for evaluating
the effectiveness and practical utility of compact clinical language
models.
\section{Personalised Medical Reports in Neurogenetic Disorders}
\subsection{Neurogenetic and Neurodevelopmental Disorders}
Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a heterogeneous group of conditions that emerge during childhood and affect brain development and functioning, often resulting in difficulties with cognition, communication, behaviour, learning, and social functioning. Genetic factors contribute substantially to many NDDs, leading to considerable variation in symptoms, severity, and combinations of intellectual, behavioural, motor, and neurological features \cite{gao_genetic_2025, assadourian_precision_2025}.

KBG syndrome (KBGS) is a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder primarily associated with pathogenic variants or deletions involving the ANKRD11 gene. It presents with a broad spectrum of features, including developmental delay, intellectual and learning difficulties, behavioural and neurological abnormalities, distinctive craniofacial features, macrodontia, short stature, and skeletal abnormalities \cite{martinez-cayuelas_clinical_2023}. The combination and severity of these features can vary substantially between children, highlighting the need to represent each child's individual clinical profile rather than relying solely on generic descriptions.

For families, understanding rare genetic disorders can be challenging due to limited condition-specific information and complex medical terminology. Personalised and accessible patient reports can help consolidate information about a child's symptoms, medical history, and care needs into a clear, patient-specific summary, supporting parents in understanding their child's condition and communicating with healthcare professionals \cite{low_identifying_2025, burbury_exploring_2025}. This motivates the use of automated patient report generation to produce personalised, understandable summaries from complex clinical and background information.

\subsection{LLM-Based Personalised Medical Report Generation}
Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4o and BioGPT, have demonstrated strong capabilities in medical report generation, clinical summarisation, and healthcare documentation, highlighting their potential to synthesise complex clinical information \cite{yuan_continued_2024, rehman_advancement_2025, alabed_large_2026, che_llm-driven_2026, makram_ai_2024}. These capabilities make LLMs promising for personalised patient report generation, as they can integrate patient-specific information and generate summaries tailored to individual clinical contexts. For example, Ganzinger et al. \cite{ganzinger_automated_2025} used open-source LLMs to generate German discharge summaries from structured EHR data, while Tariq et al. \cite{tariq_patient_2024} developed a patient-sensitive model for generating radiology summaries adapted to different levels of medical knowledge. These studies demonstrate the potential of LLMs for generating both clinically useful and patient-oriented medical reports.
\subsection{Challenges of LLM-Based Patient Report Generation}
Despite their potential, deploying large, cloud-hosted LLMs in clinical settings presents significant privacy, regulatory, and resource challenges. Patient–doctor consultations contain highly sensitive medical information, while regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR restrict the transfer of clinical data to external commercial APIs, raising important privacy and compliance concerns \cite{annas_hipaa_2003, noauthor_eu_nodate, zhong_considerations_2025, al-dhabyani_data_2026}. Furthermore, many community clinics and regional hospitals have limited computational resources and may lack the specialised GPU infrastructure required to locally deploy and efficiently run large open-weight models \cite{li_collm_2024}.

\subsection{SLM-Based Personalised Medical Report Generation}
Small Language Models (SLMs) have emerged as efficient alternatives to large language models (LLMs), offering lower computational, memory, and storage requirements while maintaining competitive performance on domain-specific tasks \cite{wang_comprehensive_2025, garg_rise_2026}. These advantages make SLMs attractive for healthcare, where local deployment can improve privacy and reduce reliance on cloud infrastructure when processing sensitive patient information \cite{wang_comprehensive_2025}.

SLMs have demonstrated potential in medical applications. For example, Meerkat showed improved reasoning performance across medical benchmarks while maintaining a lightweight architecture \cite{kim_small_2025}, while Rohanian et al. \cite{rohanian_effectiveness_2022} developed lightweight biomedical models that achieved performance comparable to larger models on downstream tasks. This suggests that SLMs can support clinical text processing and generation with more practical deployment requirements.

For personalised patient report generation, SLMs could enable patient-specific clinical information to be processed locally, reducing the need to transmit sensitive data to external APIs. However, their smaller size can limit complex reasoning and specialised medical knowledge \cite{turki_evaluating_2025, wang_comprehensive_2025}. Knowledge distillation can address this limitation by transferring useful capabilities from larger teacher models to smaller student models \cite{gou_knowledge_2021}.

















\subsection{Fundamentals of Knowledge Distillation}
Knowledge distillation (KD) is a model compression and knowledge-transfer technique in which a smaller student model learns useful knowledge from a larger and more capable teacher model \cite{hinton_distilling_2015, wang_knowledge_2022}. Unlike conventional compression approaches such as pruning, quantisation, and low-rank factorisation, which reduce or modify the model's parameters or architecture, KD transfers knowledge from the teacher to the student without necessarily altering the underlying model architecture \cite{wang_knowledge_2022, mansourian_comprehensive_2025}. The teacher provides additional learning signals, such as its output distributions, allowing the student to learn aspects of the teacher's behaviour beyond conventional hard target labels \cite{hinton_distilling_2015}.

The central principle of KD is therefore knowledge transfer, whereby information learned by a large model is transferred to a smaller model during training. This enables the student to acquire useful capabilities of the teacher while retaining the computational and memory advantages of a smaller architecture. Consequently, KD can reduce the resources required for model inference and deployment while aiming to preserve much of the teacher's predictive or generative performance \cite{hinton_distilling_2015, wang_knowledge_2022}. Beyond model compression, KD can also facilitate knowledge transfer when labelled data are limited or when knowledge must be transferred across different tasks or models. In privacy-sensitive settings, data-free distillation provides another alternative by enabling knowledge transfer without directly requiring access to the original training data \cite{wang_knowledge_2022}.

The effectiveness of KD depends on several design choices, including what knowledge is transferred, how this knowledge is transferred, and how the teacher and student models are configured \cite{mansourian_comprehensive_2025}. These considerations have led to a range of distillation approaches based on different teacher–student configurations and forms of transferred knowledge, which are discussed in the following subsection.

\subsection{Types of Knowledge Distillation}
Knowledge distillation can be categorised according to the training strategy, accessibility of the teacher model, and type of knowledge transferred. These classifications describe different aspects of the teacher–student framework and are not mutually exclusive.
\subsubsection{Offline, Online, and Self-Distillation}
Offline distillation is the most common form of knowledge distillation and typically involves a pretrained teacher model whose parameters remain frozen while a smaller student learns from its outputs \cite{hinton_distilling_2015, wang_knowledge_2022}. Although straightforward and effective, it requires the teacher to remain available during student training and may transfer biases or limitations present in the teacher \cite{guo_reducing_2021, huang_knowledge_2022-1}.

In online distillation, the teacher and student are trained simultaneously, allowing knowledge to be transferred dynamically during the training process \cite{chen_online_2020}. The teacher may be constructed from multiple student models or branches, removing the need for a separately pretrained teacher, although this can increase training complexity. Self-distillation is a related approach in which knowledge is transferred within the same model, such as from an earlier training stage, deeper layer, or previous version of the model \cite{furlanello_born_2018, zhang_be_2019}. It can improve model performance without requiring a separate, larger teacher network.

\subsubsection{Black-Box and White-Box Distillation}
White-box distillation assumes access to the teacher model's internal information, such as logits, hidden representations, attention patterns, or model parameters. This allows the student to learn from richer signals than the teacher's final outputs, but requires access to the teacher's internal architecture and computations \cite{donisch_inference_2024}.

In contrast, black-box distillation relies only on the teacher's externally observable outputs, without access to its internal parameters or representations. For language models, the teacher can therefore be prompted to generate responses, explanations, or rationales that are subsequently used as supervision for the student \cite{shi_knowledge_2025}. This makes black-box distillation particularly suitable for proprietary or API-based LLMs, where internal model information is inaccessible.

The key distinction is therefore the level of access to the teacher: white-box distillation exploits internal model information, whereas black-box distillation transfers knowledge primarily through the teacher's observable outputs.
\subsubsection{Response-, Logit-, and Feature-Based Distillation}
Knowledge distillation can also be classified according to the form of knowledge transferred from the teacher to the student. In response-based distillation, the student learns from the teacher's final outputs, such as predicted labels or generated text. For language models, this can involve using teacher-generated responses as training targets, making the approach particularly suitable for generative tasks \cite{yang_categories_2023}.

In logit-based distillation, the student learns from the teacher's logits or probability distributions rather than only the final predicted token. These soft targets provide information about the teacher's confidence across alternative predictions and can therefore provide richer supervision than hard labels \cite{habib_knowledge_2024}.

Feature-based distillation transfers knowledge from the teacher's intermediate representations, encouraging the student to learn similar internal features or representations. This can provide richer information about how the teacher processes the input, although it generally requires access to the teacher's internal architecture \cite{park_relational_2019}.
\subsection{Knowledge Distillation for LLMs}
Knowledge distillation for language models extends the teacher–student framework to generative tasks, where a smaller student model learns to reproduce useful capabilities of a larger teacher model. Rather than transferring knowledge for a single prediction task, the process aims to transfer aspects of the teacher's language generation, instruction following, domain knowledge, and response structure \cite{chen_distilling_2021}.

A common approach is to use a teacher LLM to generate instruction–response pairs or synthetic training examples, which are then used to fine-tune the student model\cite{wang_self-instruct_2023}. This provides a practical way of transferring capabilities from large, computationally expensive models to smaller models that are more suitable for efficient deployment. It is particularly useful when the teacher is proprietary or accessed through an API, as the student can be trained using the teacher's generated outputs without requiring access to its internal architecture.

However, distillation of generative language models introduces important challenges. Errors, hallucinations, and undesirable behaviours in teacher-generated responses may be transferred to the student, while the smaller model may not have sufficient capacity to reproduce the full range of the teacher's capabilities\cite{zhang_lifting_2023}. Therefore, the quality and relevance of the teacher-generated training data become important determinants of student performance . This is particularly critical in clinical report generation, where generated content must be accurate, coherent, relevant, and grounded in the individual patient's information. These considerations motivate the need for quality-aware approaches to knowledge distillation, discussed in the following section.

\section{Evaluation}
The evaluation of generated clinical reports requires multiple complementary measures, as no single metric captures all aspects of quality. This study evaluates clinical and linguistic quality, similarity to reference or teacher-generated reports, and model efficiency.
\subsection{Clinical Report Quality Dimensions}
Clinical report quality encompasses multiple dimensions that assess both the accuracy of the generated information and the effectiveness of its presentation. In this study, five key dimensions are considered: factuality, coverage, conciseness, readability, and medical tone, each capturing a distinct aspect of report quality. The following provides a brief description of each dimension.\\
\textbf{Factuality:} Whether the generated report accurately reflects the provided clinical information without false, contradictory, or unsupported claims \cite{munnangi_factehr_2025}.\\
\textbf{Coverage:} Whether the generated report is supported by and adequately represents the relevant information provided in the source \cite{moramarco_towards_2021}.\\
\textbf{Conciseness:} Whether the report communicates relevant information clearly and efficiently without unnecessary repetition, irrelevant content, or excessive detail \cite{schoonbeek_quality_2025}.\\
\textbf{Readability:} Whether the report is clear, coherent, and easy for the intended audience to understand.
\textbf{Medical Tone:} Whether the report uses professional, clinically appropriate, and audience-suitable language \cite{seo_evaluation_2024}.

\subsection{Rubric-Based and LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation}
Rubric-based evaluation uses a predefined set of criteria and scoring guidelines, known as a rubric, to systematically assess generated reports. Each report is evaluated across specific quality dimensions using the same criteria, enabling consistent and structured assessment of different aspects of report quality. This allows complex qualitative properties, such as factuality and readability, to be represented through standardised scores \cite{hashemi_llm-rubric_2024}. Rubrics are particularly useful for generated clinical reports, where quality is inherently multidimensional and cannot be adequately captured by a single metric. A multidimensional rubric allows these aspects to be assessed independently while providing a common framework across different outputs. Furthermore, rubrics can guide automated evaluation by providing an LLM with explicit criteria for assessing generated reports, reducing the need for manual assessment and enabling evaluation of large collections of outputs \cite{fan_sedareval_2024}.

LLM-as-a-Judge extends this approach by using a language model to evaluate generated reports according to the predefined rubric. The judge assesses each report across the specified dimensions and assigns corresponding scores, enabling scalable evaluation while capturing qualitative properties that may be difficult to measure using conventional automatic metrics \cite{zheng_judging_2023}. In clinical report generation, this approach provides a unified framework for assessing dimensions such as factuality, coverage, conciseness, readability, and medical tone.

Despite its scalability and flexibility, LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation presents several challenges. Its reliability depends on the clarity and design of the rubric, as ambiguous criteria may lead to inconsistent scoring. The judging model may also exhibit evaluator bias or produce variable scores across evaluation runs \cite{pombal_self-preference_2026}. In clinical settings, an additional challenge is the ability to identify subtle medical errors and distinguish clinically supported information from statements that are merely plausible. Therefore, clearly defined rubrics and consistent evaluation procedures are important for obtaining reliable and meaningful assessment scores.
\subsection{Automatic Evaluation and Model Efficiency}
Automatic evaluation metrics provide quantitative measures that complement rubric-based assessment. Text similarity metrics, such as ROUGE \cite{lin_rouge_2004}, BLEU \cite{ward_bleu_2001}, BERTScore \cite{zhang_bertscore_2020}, and embedding-based semantic similarity, assess the lexical or semantic correspondence between generated reports and reference or teacher-generated reports. While lexical metrics focus on word or phrase overlap, semantic metrics capture similarities in meaning despite differences in wording. Model efficiency is also important when evaluating an SLM, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Measures such as parameter count, inference time, memory usage, and token generation speed indicate the model's computational cost and practical deployability \cite{wani_benchmarking_2026}. Together, these measures provide a broader assessment of both report quality and model performance.

\section{Problem Formulation and Overall Framework}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

This study investigates the transfer of personalised clinical
report-generation capabilities from a large language model to a compact
student model, with particular emphasis on whether the quality of teacher
supervision influences the effectiveness of this transfer. The target task
is the generation of clinically grounded, personalised reports for patients
with KBG syndrome from doctor--parent consultation information and
syndrome-level clinical knowledge. The central research question is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Can quality-aware knowledge distillation improve the clinical
quality of reports generated by compact language models compared with
conventional supervised fine-tuning?}
\end{quote}

The project begins with a controlled synthetic-data generation framework designed to produce diverse and patient-specific clinical examples. Curated patient profiles are first used to introduce clinically relevant variation, which is then reflected in synthetically generated doctor–parent consultations. These consultations are subsequently processed by a large teacher model to generate personalised clinical reports. The resulting consultation–report pairs provide task-specific supervision for training the compact student model.

Let $x_i$ denote the doctor--parent consultation for patient $i$, 
$c$ denote the fixed KBG syndrome clinical reference, and $T$ denote the 
teacher model. The teacher generates a personalised report $y_i$ using 
both patient-specific and syndrome-level information:

\begin{equation}
y_i = T(x_i,c),
\qquad
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{teacher}}
=
\left\{(x_i,c,y_i)\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ provides the patient-specific evidence, $c$ provides 
syndrome-level clinical knowledge and management guidance, and 
$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{teacher}}$ denotes the resulting synthetic 
supervision dataset. The student model $S_{\theta}$ is subsequently 
adapted to model the conditional distribution

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y\mid x,c),
\end{equation}

using the teacher-generated demonstrations.

The framework separates four methodological stages:
\textit{synthetic data construction, teacher supervision, quality
assessment, and student optimisation}. Following consultation generation,
diversity assessment is used to determine whether the synthetic
interactions exhibit sufficient lexical, structural, grammatical, and
semantic variation. The validated consultations are then passed to the
teacher model for report generation. Teacher-generated reports are
subsequently assessed using the clinical-quality framework, providing
quality information for quality-weighted supervision and preference-pair
construction.

Student adaptation is evaluated under four configurations. The pretrained
student provides a baseline before task-specific learning. Standard
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) provides the conventional response-based
baseline, treating teacher demonstrations with equal importance.
Quality-weighted response-based distillation extends this baseline by
modulating the contribution of individual demonstrations according to their
assessed quality. Finally, Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) is applied
after distillation as a preference-alignment stage, refining the student
using relative preferences between alternative clinical reports.

The use of a common compact student architecture, initialisation, task, and
evaluation protocol across these conditions allows the effect of the
different supervision strategies to be examined independently. In
particular, quality weighting changes the influence of teacher
demonstrations without changing the underlying student model, while DPO
provides a subsequent preference-based refinement stage.

All student variants are evaluated on the same held-out test set across
three complementary dimensions: \textit{clinical quality, knowledge
transfer, and computational efficiency}.

Clinical quality captures factuality, coverage, medical tone, conciseness,
and readability. Knowledge-transfer measures quantify lexical and semantic
correspondence between student-generated and teacher-generated reports,
while computational-efficiency measures characterise model size and
inference requirements. Together, these dimensions provide a consistent
basis for assessing both the quality of the generated reports and the
efficiency gained through transfer to the compact student.

Figure~\ref{fig:overall_framework}. The following sections provide the

supervised and quality-weighted training, preference alignment, and the
evaluation framework.

\section{Synthetic Data Construction}
\label{sec:data_construction}

A controlled synthetic data-generation pipeline was developed to construct
a diverse set of doctor--parent consultations and personalised clinical
reports for student-model training. The pipeline was designed to introduce
patient-level and communicative variation while maintaining consistency
with established KBG syndrome knowledge. It comprised three stages:
\textit{(i)} synthetic patient-profile construction,
\textit{(ii)} doctor--parent consultation generation, and
\textit{(iii)} consultation diversity assessment.

The process was separated from subsequent teacher-guided report generation
and student training. Structured patient profiles were first used to
generate synthetic consultations, which were then assessed for diversity
in clinical content and communication. The resulting consultations were
subsequently passed to the teacher model for personalised clinical report
generation.


\subsection{Synthetic Patient Profile Generation}
\label{sec:patient_profile_generation}

Synthetic patient profiles were constructed to introduce controlled
heterogeneity prior to language generation. This design addresses the
tendency of unconstrained language models to reproduce similar clinical
patterns across examples. Profile-based sampling therefore provides an
explicit mechanism for varying patient characteristics and communication
styles while preserving clinical plausibility.

Each profile comprised demographic attributes, KBG syndrome-related
clinical features, and a parent communication persona. Clinical features
were organised into predefined syndrome-relevant domains, including
vision, hearing, dental, cardiac, neurological, gastrointestinal, and
other medical characteristics. The feature pools were derived from the
supplied KBG syndrome clinical reference material, while communication
personas captured distinct parent communication styles. The complete
feature pools and personas are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:profile_pools}.

For each clinical domain $d$, a feature $z_d$ was sampled from its
corresponding predefined pool:

\begin{equation}
z_d \sim P_d,
\end{equation}

where $P_d$ denotes the sampling distribution for domain $d$. A
communication persona was sampled independently, and the selected
attributes were then combined into a structured profile with a unique
patient identifier.

This controlled sampling strategy separates the introduction of clinical
variation from subsequent language generation, providing explicit
provenance for patient characteristics while reducing repetitive
generation patterns and promoting greater diversity across synthetic
cases.


\subsection{Doctor--Parent Consultation Generation}
\label{sec:consultation_generation}

The structured patient profiles were used to generate synthetic
doctor--parent consultations with \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct}. For each
case, the generation prompt incorporated the corresponding patient profile,
parent communication persona, consultation structure, and explicit
generation constraints. A representative consultation was included as an
in-context example to guide the model towards the desired consultation
format, following a few-shot prompting approach. The complete prompt is
provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:consultation_prompt}.

To ensure consistency across cases, the doctor followed the same fixed
sequence of ten predefined questions in every consultation. These
questions were designed to cover the principal clinical domains while
providing a common structure for comparison across generated cases.
Variation between consultations was introduced through the patient-specific
features and parent communication personas. Consequently, consultations
could differ in clinical content, vocabulary, response style, and level of
detail while retaining the same underlying structure. Parent responses were
restricted to the information specified in the corresponding patient
profile to minimise the generation of unsupported clinical information.

Each consultation was generated independently and stored in JSON Lines
format together with its patient identifier and source profile. The
resulting consultations were then passed to the subsequent teacher-guided
clinical report-generation stage.

\subsection{Assessment of Consultation Diversity}
\label{sec:consultation_diversity}

Controlled variation in patient profiles does not necessarily result in
diverse generated language. Consultation diversity was therefore assessed
to determine whether the generation process produced meaningful variation
across cases rather than repeatedly generating similar linguistic patterns.

Diversity was evaluated across four complementary dimensions:
\textit{lexical, response-length, grammatical, and semantic variation}.
Lexical diversity was measured using Distinct-2, which captures the
proportion of unique bigrams in the generated consultations
\cite{guo_curious_2024}. Variation in parent-response length was measured
using the standard deviation of Mean Length of Utterance (MLU)
\cite{potratz_measurement_2022}. Grammatical variation was assessed using
POS Shannon entropy, which measures the diversity of part-of-speech
compositions across consultations \cite{arora_estimating_2022}. Semantic
variation was evaluated using pairwise cosine similarity between
consultation embeddings, where lower similarity indicates greater
variation between generated consultations
\cite{noauthor_evaluating_2026}.

These measures capture complementary aspects of variation at the lexical,
structural, grammatical, and semantic levels. Together, they provide an
empirical assessment of whether the profile-based sampling and
persona-conditioned generation strategy produced sufficiently diverse
consultations while limiting repetitive generation patterns.

\section{Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:teacher_student_models}

This section describes the teacher--student framework used to transfer
task-specific clinical report-generation capabilities from a large language
model to a compact student model. The framework comprises three stages:
\textit{(i)} generation of task-specific supervision using a large teacher
model, \textit{(ii)} student training through standard supervised
fine-tuning (SFT), and \textit{(iii)} quality-aware training through
quality-weighted SFT. The same student architecture and training data are
used across these conditions to enable a controlled comparison of the
different training strategies.

\subsection{Teacher and Student Models}
\label{sec:teacher_student_models}

The proposed framework uses a teacher--student architecture in which a
large language model generates task-specific clinical reports that serve
as supervision for a substantially smaller student model. The teacher
provides the target behaviour, while the student learns to reproduce the
same report-generation task with considerably lower model capacity.

\paragraph{Teacher Model:}The teacher model was \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct}. Its larger capacity
provides a strong source of language generation and task-specific clinical
knowledge for constructing the supervision used by the student. The
teacher therefore acts as the source of task-specific supervision rather
than the final deployment model.

For each synthetic consultation, the teacher received two explicitly
separated information sources: the doctor--parent consultation and a fixed
KBG syndrome clinical reference. The consultation served as the sole source
of patient-specific information, while the clinical reference provided
general syndrome-level information, including relevant screening and
treatment recommendations. This separation distinguished individual
patient evidence from general syndrome knowledge and helped reduce
unsupported patient-specific claims.

A structured generation prompt guided the teacher to identify
patient-specific findings, organise them into predefined clinical
categories, and generate a personalised report. When information for a
category was absent from the consultation, the teacher was instructed to
output ``N/A'' rather than infer unsupported characteristics.
Recommendations were similarly constrained by the available patient
information and the provided syndrome reference.

The generated reports followed a fixed 13-part structure covering the
principal clinical domains, together with screening and treatment
recommendations. Within each domain, \textit{patient-specific findings}
were distinguished from \textit{syndrome-level information}, providing a
consistent target representation across cases.

Each generated report was stored with its corresponding consultation and
metadata, preserving the provenance of the supervision for subsequent
quality assessment and student training. The complete generation prompt,
clinical reference, and inference configuration are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:report_prompt}.

\paragraph{Student Model:}The teacher-generated reports were subsequently used to train the compact
student model, \textit{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct}, containing approximately
1.5 billion parameters. Its substantially smaller size provides a
meaningful setting for examining whether the capabilities expressed in the
teacher's reports can be transferred while reducing model scale and
computational requirements.

The student was trained to learn the task-specific mapping from a
doctor--parent consultation to a personalised clinical report. This
includes learning the fixed report structure and organisation, identifying
and presenting patient-specific findings, incorporating appropriate
syndrome-level knowledge, and generating clinically relevant screening and
treatment recommendations. The student also learns to distinguish
patient-specific evidence from general syndrome knowledge and to reproduce
the consistent reporting style and level of detail established by the
teacher.

The same \textit{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct} backbone and initialisation were
used across all student experiments, ensuring that differences in
performance are attributable to the training strategy rather than
differences in model capacity or initialisation. The specific mechanisms
used to train the student, including standard SFT and quality-weighted
SFT, are described in the following sections.

Detailed model configurations and training hyperparameters are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:student_training_config}.
\subsection{Standard Supervised Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:standard_sft}

Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) was used as the primary baseline for
transferring the teacher model's task-specific report-generation behaviour
to the compact student model. SFT provides a direct and established
approach for adapting a pretrained language model to a task-specific
generation task, making it an appropriate baseline for evaluating the
effect of quality-aware supervision \cite{CITATION_SFT_REFERENCE}.

The student was trained on teacher-generated supervision consisting of a
doctor--parent consultation, the fixed KBG syndrome clinical reference,
and the corresponding teacher-generated report. The consultation provided
patient-specific information, while the clinical reference supplied
general syndrome-level knowledge. The teacher-generated report served as
the target that the student was trained to reproduce. Thus, each training
example can be represented as
$\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,c,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, where $x_i$ denotes the
doctor--parent consultation, $c$ the KBG syndrome clinical reference, and
$y_i$ the corresponding teacher-generated report.

Given this supervision, SFT minimises the autoregressive negative
log-likelihood:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
p_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,c,y_{i,<t}
\right),
\label{eq:sft_loss}
\end{equation}

where $y_{i,t}$ denotes the target token at position $t$,
$y_{i,<t}$ denotes the preceding target tokens, and
$p_{\theta}$ is the conditional token distribution produced by the
student. The objective therefore trains the student to generate the
teacher's personalised report from the same clinical information
available to the teacher.

The teacher-generated reports provide supervision for both the content and
organisation of the task. Through SFT, the student learns to identify and
present patient-specific findings, incorporate relevant syndrome-level
knowledge, follow the prescribed report structure, and generate appropriate
screening and treatment recommendations. Standard SFT assigns equal
importance to all retained training examples, regardless of the quality of
the corresponding teacher-generated report. This provides a controlled
baseline against which quality-aware supervision can be evaluated.

The student was adapted using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which freezes the
pretrained model parameters and learns low-rank updates to selected weight
matrices \cite{hu_lora_2022}. For a pretrained weight matrix
$W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, the adapted weight is represented as

\begin{equation}
W = W_0 + \Delta W
  = W_0 + BA,
\label{eq:lora_update}
\end{equation}

where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and
$A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k}$ are trainable matrices with
$r \ll \min(d,k)$. The corresponding forward transformation is

\begin{equation}
h = W_0x + \frac{\alpha}{r}BAx,
\label{eq:lora_forward}
\end{equation}

where $x$ is the input representation and $\alpha$ is the LoRA scaling
factor. Only the low-rank parameters are updated during training, while
the pretrained parameters remain frozen. This substantially reduces the
number of trainable parameters and memory requirements while retaining the
student's pretrained representations \cite{hu_lora_2022}.

The same student initialisation, training data, input formatting, and
parameter-efficient adaptation framework were maintained for the
subsequent quality-weighted SFT experiments. Consequently, the comparison
between standard and quality-weighted SFT isolates the effect of
incorporating teacher-response quality into the training objective rather
than differences in model architecture or adaptation methodology.

Detailed LoRA configuration and optimisation hyperparameters are provided
in Appendix~\ref{appx:student_training_config}

\subsection{Quality-Weighted Supervision}
\label{sec:quality_weighted_sft}

Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) treats all teacher-generated
demonstrations equally, implicitly assuming that they provide equally
reliable supervision. In synthetic clinical data, this assumption may not
hold because generated reports can differ in factual reliability, clinical
coverage, and adherence to the intended reporting style. Quality-weighted
supervision was therefore introduced to make the contribution of each
demonstration proportional to its assessed quality.

For each retained training report $y_i$, a scalar quality score $q_i$ was
constructed from three rubric dimensions: \textit{factuality},
\textit{coverage}, and \textit{medical tone}. These dimensions were selected
because they directly characterise the quality of information being
transferred. Factuality measures whether the reported information is
supported by the available evidence, coverage measures whether relevant
clinical information is retained, and medical tone measures whether the
information is expressed in the intended clinical reporting style.

Conciseness and readability were not included as additional weighting
dimensions. Conciseness was already defined as a composite of factuality,
coverage, and brevity; including it directly would therefore introduce
overlap with two dimensions already present in the quality signal.
Readability was assessed separately using Flesch Reading Ease (FRE),
Flesch--Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), SMOG, and Dale--Chall. These measures
primarily capture linguistic accessibility and reading difficulty rather
than the clinical reliability or completeness of the information being
transferred. They were therefore retained as evaluation criteria rather
than used to determine training importance.

The three selected dimensions were combined using a geometric mean:

\begin{equation}
q_i = \left(F_i C_i M_i\right)^{1/3},
\end{equation}

where $F_i$, $C_i$, and $M_i$ denote the factuality, coverage, and
medical-tone scores for report $i$. The geometric mean was chosen because
these dimensions represent complementary requirements for useful clinical
supervision. It limits compensation between dimensions: a report cannot
obtain a high quality score through strong performance in one dimension
while performing poorly in another. This makes the resulting weight reflect
\textit{consistent quality} rather than average performance across
potentially compensating criteria.

The raw quality scores were normalised within each training batch using the
batch mean:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{q}_i =
\frac{q_i}
{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}}q_j},
\qquad i\in\mathcal{B}.
\end{equation}

Mean normalisation preserves the relative ordering and differences between
examples while maintaining an average weight of one. Thus, the weighting
mechanism changes which examples have greater influence without
systematically changing the scale of the loss.

Let $\ell_{i,t}$ denote the causal language-model cross-entropy loss for
token $t$ of example $i$, and let $m_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether that
token contributes to the supervised objective. The quality-weighted loss
is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QW}}
=
\frac{
\displaystyle
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_t
m_{i,t}\tilde{q}_i\ell_{i,t}}
{
\displaystyle
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_t m_{i,t}
}.
\end{equation}

The same example-level weight is applied to all supervised tokens of its
corresponding report, while masked tokens are excluded from the objective.
This matches the granularity of the quality assessment, which evaluates
each complete report rather than individual tokens.

Quality control was applied at two levels. Reports failing the predefined
quality criteria were excluded from student supervision, while the
remaining reports were retained and assigned continuous quality weights.
This avoids both extremes of treating all demonstrations equally and
discarding all lower-quality examples. The resulting objective preserves
training-data diversity while giving stronger demonstrations greater
influence during optimisation.

All other aspects of student training were held consistent with standard
SFT, including the student architecture, initialisation, LoRA adaptation,
training data, and optimisation procedure. Consequently, the principal
methodological difference between the two conditions is the incorporation
of quality into the supervision objective. Detailed quality-score
construction and training configuration are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:student_training_config}.
\section{Direct Preference Optimization}
\label{sec:dpo}

Following supervised and quality-weighted adaptation, Direct Preference
Optimization (DPO) was applied as a preference-alignment stage. While SFT
learns to reproduce teacher-generated responses and quality-weighted
supervision adjusts the influence of individual demonstrations, DPO provides
a relative learning signal by teaching the student which of two candidate
responses is preferable. DPO was selected because it optimises this
preference relationship directly, without requiring a separately trained
reward model or an additional reinforcement-learning stage
\cite{rafailov2023direct}.

For each consultation $x_i$, a preference pair was constructed comprising
a preferred response $y_i^{w}$ and a less-preferred response $y_i^{l}$.
The preferred response was the higher-quality alternative according to the
established clinical-quality assessment, while the less-preferred response
represented the corresponding lower-quality alternative. Both responses
were evaluated within the same clinical context, allowing the model to
learn a relative preference rather than simply reproduce a fixed target.
Preference pairs were constructed exclusively from the training data,
ensuring that the held-out test set remained independent of preference
optimisation.

DPO optimises the student policy relative to a fixed reference policy
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. For a preference triple
$(x,y^{w},y^{l})$, the objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
=
-\mathbb{E}
\left[
\log \sigma
\left(
\beta
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{w}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{w}\mid x)}
-
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{l}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{l}\mid x)}
\right]
\right)
\right],
\end{equation}

where $\pi_{\theta}$ denotes the trainable student policy,
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ the fixed reference policy, $\sigma$ the sigmoid
function, and $\beta$ controls the strength of the preference signal
\cite{rafailov2023direct}. The objective increases the relative likelihood
of the preferred response over the less-preferred response while measuring
this change relative to the reference policy. The reference therefore
provides a stable behavioural baseline and helps limit unnecessary policy
drift during preference optimisation.

DPO was applied after quality-weighted supervised adaptation so that the
student first acquired the underlying clinical reporting task, evidence
grounding, and required report structure. Preference optimisation could
then operate as a refinement stage rather than being responsible for
learning the task from scratch. The resulting training sequence provides
three complementary forms of supervision: SFT establishes task-specific
generation, quality weighting prioritises stronger teacher demonstrations,
and DPO subsequently refines the relative preference between alternative
responses.

A key methodological trade-off is that quality-weighted supervision
retains a continuous quality signal, whereas DPO converts this information
into a pairwise ordering. The effectiveness of DPO therefore depends on
the reliability of the preference pairs. Ambiguous or poorly differentiated
pairs could introduce an unreliable optimisation signal. Preference
construction was consequently grounded in the established clinical-quality
assessment rather than superficial differences in wording or fluency.

The student architecture, initialisation, LoRA adaptation, and clinical
task were kept consistent with the preceding training conditions. DPO could
therefore be evaluated specifically as a preference-alignment intervention,
rather than as a change in model capacity or task formulation. Detailed
preference-pair construction and DPO training configuration are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:dpo_training_config}.

\section{Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:evaluation_framework}

The generated clinical reports were evaluated using a rubric-guided
\textit{LLM-as-a-Judge} framework, with
\textit{Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507} serving as the evaluator. This approach
was adopted because clinical properties such as factual grounding,
completeness, and appropriate medical expression are not fully captured by
lexical overlap or conventional text-similarity metrics.

Evaluation was conducted at two stages for different purposes. Teacher
reports were assessed for \textit{clinical quality} to characterise the
synthetic supervision and derive the quality information used for
quality-weighted training and preference construction. The trained student
variants were subsequently evaluated on the held-out test set across
\textit{clinical quality, knowledge transfer, and computational efficiency}.

Clinical quality comprised \textit{factuality, coverage, medical tone,
conciseness, and readability}. Factuality, coverage, and medical tone were
assessed using the rubric-guided LLM evaluator, while conciseness and
readability were measured using dedicated quantitative procedures.
Conciseness captures the balance between \textit{information retention,
factual reliability, and brevity}, ensuring that shorter reports are not
implicitly rewarded for omitting clinically relevant information.
Readability captures linguistic accessibility through established
text-based measures.

Knowledge transfer was assessed using lexical and semantic similarity
measures, while computational efficiency was characterised using model size
and inference-cost measures. These metrics complement clinical-quality
assessment by determining how effectively the compact student retains
teacher-derived behaviour while reducing computational requirements.

Explicit scoring criteria, evaluation instructions, and relevant clinical
evidence were provided to the evaluator to promote consistent,
evidence-grounded judgements. The complete evaluation procedures and prompts
are described in the following subsections.
\subsection{Factuality}
\label{sec:factuality}

Factuality was assessed using a fine-grained, claim-level verification
procedure to determine whether information generated in a report was
supported by the evidence available for that patient. Claim-level
verification was preferred to document-level judgement because it allows
individual unsupported or incorrect clinical statements to be identified
rather than being masked within an overall report assessment
\cite{min_factscore_2023,scire_fenice_2024}. This is particularly important
in clinical text, where even a small number of unsupported claims can be
meaningful \cite{bn_fact-controlled_2025,munnangi_factehr_2025}.

Each generated report was first decomposed into atomic clinical claims,
where each claim represented a single, independently verifiable proposition.
The decomposition process retained clinically meaningful statements while
excluding questions, non-informative text, duplicated information, and
unsupported inferences. This transformed each report into a set of discrete
claims that could subsequently be verified against explicit evidence. The
complete decomposition procedure is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_claim_prompt}.

Each claim was then assigned an evidence source according to its type.
Patient-specific claims, including symptoms, clinical history, and
individual findings, were verified exclusively against the corresponding
doctor--parent consultation. Syndrome-level claims were verified against
the KBG syndrome clinical reference provided during report generation. This
source separation is essential because general knowledge about KBG syndrome
should not be treated as evidence that a particular patient exhibits a
specific characteristic.

For each claim $c_i$, the LLM-based verifier assigned a binary support label:

\begin{equation}
v_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } c_i \text{ is supported by its designated evidence source},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

A claim was considered supported when its designated source conveyed the
same clinical meaning, allowing for differences in wording, grammar, or
clinically equivalent terminology. Claims that were unsupported,
contradictory, or unverifiable from the designated source received
$v_i=0$. The assessment therefore measures \textit{source-grounded
factuality}, rather than general medical plausibility.

The report-level factuality score was calculated as the proportion of
supported atomic claims:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Factuality}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_i,
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of extracted claims in report $R$. The score
ranges from $0$ to $1$, with higher values indicating a greater proportion
of claims supported by the designated evidence.

This formulation provides a direct and interpretable measure of factual
reliability while preserving the distinction between patient-specific
evidence and syndrome-level knowledge. It also enables unsupported
information to be identified at claim level rather than being obscured by
an aggregate document-level judgement.

The complete claim-decomposition and verification prompts and scoring
instructions are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_claim_prompt}.
\subsection{Coverage}
\label{sec:coverage}

Coverage was evaluated to determine how effectively a generated report
retained the clinically relevant information required for the target task.
Unlike factuality, which asks whether information stated in the report is
supported by evidence, coverage asks whether important information from the
available sources was included at all. The distinction is important because
a report may be factually accurate yet clinically incomplete
\cite{noauthor_faithfulness_2026,song_finesure_2024}.

The target reports integrate three types of information: patient-specific
findings, syndrome-level background information, and clinically relevant
recommendations. Coverage was therefore assessed separately for
\textit{patient-specific coverage}, \textit{syndrome-background coverage},
and \textit{recommendation coverage}. This decomposition ensures that
omissions in one information type are not obscured by strong performance in
another.

\paragraph{Patient-Specific Coverage.}
Patient-specific coverage was assessed using a claim-based procedure. First,
clinically meaningful facts were extracted from the doctor--parent
consultation, retaining information explicitly reported by the parent about
the child. Complex statements were decomposed into distinct clinical
claims, while non-informative content and unsupported information were
excluded \cite{dejl_comprehensiveness_2026,kim_atomic_2026}. These claims
formed the reference set of patient-specific information that the generated
report was expected to retain.

Each reference claim was then checked against the generated report. A claim
was considered covered when the report conveyed the same clinical meaning,
irrespective of differences in wording or sentence structure. For each
coverage component $k$, an evaluation unit $i$ was assigned:

\begin{equation}
v_i^{(k)} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if the evaluation unit is explicitly addressed in the report},\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
\mathrm{Coverage}_{k}
=
\frac{1}{N_k}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_k}v_i^{(k)}.
\end{equation}

For patient-specific coverage, the evaluation units were the extracted
clinical claims. Claims that were absent, unclear, or unsupported were
assigned zero. This formulation therefore measures semantic information
retention rather than lexical overlap.

\paragraph{Syndrome Background and Recommendation Coverage.}
Syndrome-level coverage was evaluated using feature-specific questions.
For each clinical feature explicitly reported in the consultation, two
questions were generated: one asking what relevant KBG syndrome background
information should be communicated and another asking what
feature-specific clinical recommendations should be provided. The latter
included relevant screening, monitoring, treatment, referral, or therapy
where applicable \cite{scialom_questeval_2021,moramarco_towards_2021}.
Restricting question generation to features present in the consultation
ensured that the evaluation focused on clinically relevant information
rather than rewarding the inclusion of generic syndrome knowledge.

Each generated question was treated as a binary coverage test. A response
received a score of $1$ when the report explicitly addressed the information
requested by the question and $0$ when the information was missing,
ambiguous, or unsupported. The same formulation above was applied to both
question sets, with $k\in\{B,Rec\}$ representing syndrome background and
recommendations, respectively. This produced three component scores:
$\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}$, $\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}$, and
$\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}$.

The three components were retained separately to identify the source of
coverage deficiencies and were also combined using an unweighted arithmetic
mean:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Overall}
=
\frac{
\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}
}{3}.
\end{equation}

An arithmetic mean was used because the three components represent distinct
but equally important information requirements of the target report. It
provides an interpretable overall coverage measure while preventing any
single component from dominating the score.

The complete claim-extraction and question-generation procedures are
provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:patient_coverage_prompt} and
Appendix~\ref{appx:syndrome_coverage_prompt}.
\subsection{Conciseness}
\label{sec:conciseness}

Conciseness was evaluated to determine whether reports communicated the
required clinical information accurately without unnecessary verbosity. In
this setting, conciseness is not equivalent to shortness: a report can be
brief simply because clinically relevant information has been omitted.
Accordingly, conciseness was formulated as a composite of
\textit{information retention, factual reliability, and brevity}, ensuring
that shorter reports were not rewarded when brevity was achieved at the
expense of clinical content or correctness.

For each report $i$, token length $L_i$ was calculated using the tokenizer
of the evaluated model. Report lengths were min--max normalised across the
evaluation set and converted into a brevity score:

\begin{equation}
L_i^{\mathrm{norm}}
=
\frac{L_i-L_{\min}}{L_{\max}-L_{\min}},
\qquad
B_i = 1-L_i^{\mathrm{norm}},
\end{equation}

where $L_{\min}$ and $L_{\max}$ denote the minimum and maximum report
lengths. This produces a relative brevity measure within the evaluation
set, with higher $B_i$ indicating a shorter report, without imposing an
arbitrary externally defined target length.

Brevity was combined with coverage and factuality to reflect the two
clinical constraints that should accompany concise reporting. Coverage
ensures that relevant information is retained, while factuality ensures
that the retained information remains supported by the available evidence.
The resulting score was defined using their geometric mean:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Conciseness}_i
=
\left(
\mathrm{Coverage}_i
\cdot
\mathrm{Factuality}_i
\cdot
B_i
\right)^{1/3}.
\end{equation}

The geometric mean was selected over an arithmetic mean because the three
components represent complementary requirements rather than fully
compensatory objectives. It therefore penalises imbalance: high brevity
cannot produce a high conciseness score when coverage or factuality is poor,
while a comprehensive and factual report is not considered highly concise
if it is unnecessarily long.

The resulting score ranges from $0$ to $1$, with higher values indicating a
more favourable balance between retaining clinically relevant information,
maintaining factual reliability, and avoiding unnecessary length.
\subsection{Readability}
\label{sec:readability}

Readability was evaluated to determine how easily the generated clinical
reports could be understood from a linguistic perspective. This is
distinct from medical tone: a report may use appropriate professional
language while still being difficult to read because of complex sentence
structures, vocabulary, or reading-level requirements. Readability was
therefore assessed independently using four established measures that
capture complementary aspects of textual difficulty.

The evaluation included Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), Flesch--Kincaid Grade
Level (FKGL), Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG), and Dale--Chall. FRE
provides a general measure of reading ease, with higher scores indicating
easier text, while FKGL estimates the educational grade level required for
comprehension \cite{flesch_new_1948,tanprasert_flesch-kincaid_2021}. SMOG
primarily reflects difficulty associated with polysyllabic words
\cite{mclaughlin_smog_1969}, whereas Dale--Chall incorporates word
familiarity and sentence length \cite{crossley_large-scaled_2023}. Using
multiple measures reduces dependence on any single definition of
readability and provides a broader assessment of linguistic accessibility.

Each measure was computed independently for every generated report using
the cleaned report text and the \texttt{textstat} implementation. The
measures were retained as separate indicators rather than combined into a
single score because they use different scales and have different
interpretations; aggregation would therefore obscure meaningful differences
between aspects of readability.


\subsection{Medical Tone}
\label{sec:medical_tone}

Medical tone was evaluated to determine whether generated reports
communicated patient-specific information in a clinically appropriate,
professional, respectful, and patient-centred manner. These dimensions were
selected because effective clinical reporting requires not only appropriate
medical language but also communication that is respectful of the patient
and focused on their individual circumstances
\cite{bowden_evaluating_nodate,rattanapitoon_beyond_2025,omar_impact_2025}.

A two-stage LLM-based procedure was used to isolate and then assess the
language relevant to this objective. First, patient-specific statements
describing the patient's condition and associated screening or treatment
recommendations were extracted from each report. Syndrome-level background
information and general medical information were excluded because these do
not directly reflect how the individual patient is described or advised.
Few-shot prompting was used for extraction, with the complete procedure
provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:medical_tone_prompt}.

The extracted statements were then independently evaluated using a separate
few-shot LLM prompt across three complementary criteria:
\textit{professionalism, respectfulness, and patient-centred
communication}. Each criterion captures a distinct aspect of appropriate
clinical expression, allowing the evaluation to distinguish, for example,
professional language from communication that is specifically respectful
or patient-focused.

For statement $i$ and criterion $j$, a binary score was assigned:

\begin{equation}
s_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if statement } i \text{ satisfies criterion } j,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

For each report, the proportion of statements satisfying each criterion was
then calculated:

\begin{equation}
S_j =
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{ij},
\qquad
j\in\{P,R,C\},
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the number of extracted patient-specific statements, and
$P$, $R$, and $C$ denote professionalism, respectfulness, and
patient-centred communication, respectively. The three scores were retained
separately rather than combined because the criteria represent distinct
communication properties and there was no principled basis for assigning
different weights to them. This preserves interpretability while allowing
specific weaknesses in clinical communication to be identified.
\subsection{Knowledge Transfer and Computational Efficiency}
\label{sec:knowledge_transfer_efficiency}

In addition to clinical quality, the student models were evaluated on
\textit{knowledge transfer} and \textit{computational efficiency}. These
dimensions address two complementary questions: how closely the compact
student reproduces the information and semantic behaviour of the teacher,
and whether this capability can be obtained with lower computational
requirements.

\paragraph{Knowledge Transfer.}
Knowledge transfer was assessed by comparing each student-generated report
with its corresponding teacher-generated report. The objective was to
determine whether the student retained teacher-derived information and
meaning without requiring verbatim reproduction. Lexical similarity was
measured using ROUGE and BLEU, which quantify different forms of surface
word and sequence overlap. Semantic correspondence was assessed using
BERTScore and embedding similarity, providing complementary measures that
are less dependent on exact wording. These metrics were retained
separately because lexical overlap and semantic similarity capture different
aspects of teacher--student correspondence and should not be conflated into
a single measure.

\paragraph{Computational Efficiency.}
Computational efficiency was evaluated to quantify the practical advantage
of transferring the task to a compact student model. Four complementary
measures were considered: \textit{parameter count, inference latency, peak
memory usage, and token generation rate}. Parameter count reflects model
scale; inference latency measures the time required to generate a report;
peak memory captures the maximum memory required during inference; and token
generation rate measures generation throughput.

Latency, peak memory, and token generation rate were measured under identical
inference conditions and hardware for all student variants to ensure that
differences reflected the models rather than changes in the evaluation
environment. Parameter count was obtained from the respective model
architectures. Reporting these measures alongside clinical-quality
performance enables the quality--efficiency trade-off to be assessed,
rather than treating computational efficiency as an independent objective.

\section{Synthetic Patient Profile Generation}
\subsection{Patient Profile Design}

Synthetic patient profiles were designed as structured representations of the
patient characteristics required to generate diverse and personalised
doctor--patient consultations. Each profile combined clinical features drawn
from predefined KBG syndrome-related domains with a parent communication
persona, enabling variation in both clinical content and communication style.
Basic demographic attributes, including age and gender, were also included to
provide contextual information. The resulting profiles provided a consistent
input structure for the subsequent consultation generation process.

\subsection{Controlled Random Sampling Strategy}

To introduce controlled variability across synthetic patients, profile
attributes were randomly sampled from predefined clinical feature and persona
pools. Clinical features were organised into domains including vision,
hearing, dental, cardiac, neurological, gastrointestinal, and other medical
characteristics. Features were sampled from the corresponding domain-specific
pools, with additional characteristics drawn from the other medical feature
pool. A parent communication persona was independently sampled from a
predefined set representing different communication styles. This strategy
allowed the profiles to vary systematically while maintaining a consistent
overall structure.

The sampling of a clinical feature from a domain can be represented as:

\[
x_d \sim P_d
\]

where $x_d$ denotes the sampled feature from domain $d$, and $P_d$ represents
the corresponding predefined feature pool. The complete clinical feature
pools and parent communication personas used for sampling are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:profile_pools}.

\subsection{Profile Generation Procedure}

For each synthetic patient, demographic attributes and a parent communication
persona were first selected from their respective predefined pools. Clinical
features were then sampled from the relevant domain-specific pools, followed
by additional features from the other medical feature pool. The selected
attributes were combined into a structured profile and assigned a unique
patient identifier. This process was repeated until the required number of
profiles had been generated. The resulting profiles were stored in JSON
format, providing a consistent and machine-readable representation for the
subsequent consultation generation stage.

\section{Doctor–Patient Consultation Ibrahim}

\subsection{Consultation Generation Framework}
Synthetic doctor--patient consultations were generated to create realistic and diverse clinical interactions for subsequent personalised report generation. Each synthetic patient profile was provided to \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct} using a structured prompt. The prompt incorporated the patient-specific clinical features, parent communication persona, fixed consultation structure, and explicit generation constraints. A representative consultation was also included as an in-context example to guide the expected response format and style, constituting a few-shot prompting approach.

The model was instructed to reproduce a fixed sequence of ten predefined doctor questions covering the major clinical domains relevant to KBG syndrome, while generating corresponding parent responses grounded in the supplied profile. The parent persona controlled variation in linguistic style, vocabulary, and level of detail. Generation constraints were used to minimise unsupported clinical information and maintain consistency between the source profile and generated consultation.

Each consultation was generated independently and stored with its patient identifier and source profile in JSON Lines format. The resulting synthetic consultations were subsequently used for clinical report generation. \subsection{Diversity Assessment}
The diversity of the generated consultations was evaluated to determine whether the generation process produced sufficiently varied parent responses rather than repeatedly reproducing similar linguistic patterns. Diversity was assessed across lexical, structural, grammatical, and semantic dimensions.

Distinct-2 \cite{guo_curious_2024} was used to measure lexical variation by calculating the proportion of unique bigrams within parent responses. Standard deviation of Mean Length of Utterance (MLU) \cite{potratz_measurement_2022} captured variation in the length of parent responses, providing an indication of differences in response structure and level of detail. POS Shannon entropy \cite{arora_estimating_2022} measured the distribution and variability of grammatical categories used across the consultations. Finally, pairwise cosine similarity between consultation embeddings was used to assess semantic similarity between generated consultations \cite{noauthor_evaluating_2026}.

Together, these complementary measures assessed whether the consultations varied not only in their surface-level wording, but also in response length, grammatical composition, and overall semantic content. This provided a broader assessment of the diversity of the synthetic consultation dataset.
\section{Teacher-Guided Clinical Report Generation}

Following the generation of synthetic doctor--patient consultations, the
consultations were transformed into personalised clinical reports using a
large language model. This stage was designed to generate high-quality
reference reports that could subsequently be evaluated and used in the
student-model training and knowledge distillation stages. Each report was
grounded in the corresponding consultation, while a clinical reference
context provided additional syndrome-level information required for
interpretation and management recommendations.

The report generation process was formulated as a conditional text generation
task:

\[
y = f_{\theta}(x,c,p)
\]

where $x$ represents the doctor--patient consultation, $c$ represents the
KBG syndrome clinical reference context, $p$ represents the structured
generation prompt, $f_{\theta}$ denotes the language model with parameters
$\theta$, and $y$ represents the generated clinical report. The consultation
provided the patient-specific evidence, while the clinical reference
provided supporting syndrome-level information.

The \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct} model
(\texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct}) was used for report generation.
The model was instruction prompted to transform each consultation into a
structured clinical report while maintaining factual grounding and
patient-specificity. To reduce GPU memory requirements during inference, the
model was loaded using 4-bit NF4 quantisation with double quantisation and
bfloat16 computation.

A structured prompting strategy was used to constrain the generation process.
The prompt provided the model with the KBG clinical reference guidelines and
the corresponding doctor--parent consultation. The model was instructed to
first identify the clinical findings present in the consultation, associate
each finding with its appropriate clinical category, and then generate the
final report. This intermediate processing was performed internally and was
not included in the generated output.

To reduce unsupported clinical information, patient-specific statements were
required to originate from the consultation, while syndrome-level information
was restricted to the supplied clinical reference. Recommendations were
generated only when supported by reported patient findings. Where information
was unavailable for a category, the model was instructed to return
``N/A''. This grounding strategy reduced the likelihood of introducing
symptoms or clinical assumptions that were not supported by the source
material.

The generated reports followed a fixed 13-category schema covering major
clinical domains, including cardiology, gastroenterology, immunology,
neurology, ENT, ophthalmology, dental, education, behaviour and development,
and skeletal features, followed by screening and treatment recommendations.
For each clinical domain, the report distinguished between
\textit{patient-specific effects} and \textit{syndrome-level information}.
This consistent schema enabled systematic comparison of reports across
patients and provided a standardised target format for subsequent
distillation.

Generation was performed independently for each consultation. A relatively
low sampling temperature ($T=0.3$) was used to favour factual consistency
and adherence to the prescribed report structure. The resulting outputs
were stored in JSONL format together with the source consultation and
associated generation fields, preserving the correspondence between each
input consultation and its generated report. These consultation--report
pairs formed the teacher-generated reference dataset.

The teacher-generated reports were subsequently subjected to rubric-based
quality assessment. Reports were evaluated against predefined criteria
before being considered for subsequent student-model training and
knowledge-distillation experiments. The complete report-generation prompt,
including the clinical reference context and output schema, is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:report_prompt}.

The overall process is illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:teacher_report_generation}, showing the transformation from
the synthetic consultation and clinical reference context through structured
prompting and language-model generation to the final structured clinical
report.

\section{Evaluation of Generated Personalised Medical Reports}
This section evaluates whether teacher-generated reports are of sufficient quality to serve as training data for the student model. Reports are assessed based on factuality, coverage, medical tone, readability, and conciseness to determine their suitability for knowledge distillation.
\subsection{Factuality}


Factuality was assessed using a fine-grained, claim-level verification procedure to determine whether generated reports were supported by their designated source information. The approach was motivated by methods that decompose generated text into atomic factual units and subsequently verify each unit against an evidence source \cite{min_factscore_2023, scire_fenice_2024}. Similar claim-level approaches have also been applied to medical text, where fine-grained factuality assessment is particularly important for identifying unsupported clinical information \cite{bn_fact-controlled_2025, munnangi_factehr_2025}.

For each generated report, the factual content was first decomposed into atomic clinical claims. An atomic fact is a minimal, self-contained unit of information that expresses a single, distinct proposition and cannot be broken down further without losing its structural meaning \cite{min_factscore_2023}. A structured extraction prompt was used to identify patient-specific information explicitly supported by the doctor--parent consultation, while excluding questions, non-informative responses, inference, and external medical knowledge. Closely related details representing the same clinical fact were retained as a single claim. The complete extraction prompt is provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_claim_prompt}.

Each extracted claim was subsequently verified against its corresponding evidence source. Patient-specific claims, including reported symptoms and clinical history, were checked against the original doctor--parent consultation, whereas syndrome-level claims were checked against the KBG syndrome background information used during report generation. Verification therefore assessed whether each claim was grounded in the information available to the report-generation process rather than relying on external medical knowledge.

Each claim $c_i$ was assigned a binary verification label:

\begin{equation}
v_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } c_i \text{ is supported by its corresponding source},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

A claim was considered supported when the source explicitly conveyed the same clinical meaning, allowing for differences in wording, grammar, or clinically equivalent terminology. Unsupported, contradictory, or source-unverifiable claims were assigned a value of zero. The complete verification prompt and evaluation criteria are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_verification_prompt}.

The report-level factuality score was calculated as the proportion of supported atomic claims:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Factuality}(R)
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i}{N},
\end{equation}

where $N$ denotes the total number of extracted claims in report $R$. The resulting score ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater source-grounded factuality.

The complete evaluation process can therefore be represented as:

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    enhanced,
    colback=blue!3,
    colframe=blue!45!black,
    boxrule=0.7pt,
    arc=1.5mm,
    left=4mm,
    right=4mm,
    top=3mm,
    bottom=3mm
]
\centering
\textbf{Factuality Evaluation Pipeline}

\vspace{2mm}

\text{Patient Report}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Atomic Claims}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Source Verification}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Binary Labels}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Factuality Score}

\end{tcolorbox}

For example, a generated statement such as ``The patient experiences speech delay'' would be treated as an atomic claim and verified against the corresponding consultation. If the consultation explicitly supports the statement, the claim receives $v_i=1$; otherwise, it receives $v_i=0$. Further examples of claim decomposition, evidence sources, and verification decisions are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_verification_prompt}.

This procedure provides a fine-grained measure of source-grounded factuality and allows unsupported information to be identified at the individual claim level rather than being masked within an overall document-level judgement.

\subsection{Coverage}

Coverage was evaluated to measure the extent to which clinically relevant
information from the source material was retained in the generated reports.
Unlike factuality, which assesses whether generated claims are supported by
source evidence, coverage assesses whether relevant source information is
represented in the report \cite{noauthor_faithfulness_2026, song_finesure_2024}.
Because the report integrates information from different sources, coverage
was evaluated separately for patient-specific information, syndrome-level
background information, and recommendations.

\subsubsection{Patient-Specific Coverage}

Patient-specific coverage was assessed using a two-stage claim-based
procedure. First, clinically meaningful patient-specific claims were
extracted from the doctor--parent consultation. Only information explicitly
stated by the parent about the child was retained; doctor's questions,
\textit{N/A} responses, emotions, uncertainty, opinions, intentions, and
unsupported inferences were excluded. Complex statements were decomposed
into distinct clinical facts while avoiding duplicate claims \cite{dejl_comprehensiveness_2026, kim_atomic_2026}. The extraction prompt is
provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:patient_coverage_prompt}.

Each claim was then evaluated against the generated report. A claim was
considered covered if the report conveyed the same clinical meaning,
irrespective of differences in wording or sentence structure. Claims that
were absent, unclear, or unsupported were assigned $0$. Thus,

\begin{equation}
v_i^{P} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if claim } c_i \text{ is covered by the report},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The patient-specific coverage score was:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_P}\sum_{i=1}^{N_P}v_i^{P},
\end{equation}

where $N_P$ is the number of extracted patient-specific claims.

\subsubsection{Syndrome Background and Recommendation Coverage}

Syndrome-level coverage was evaluated using feature-specific questions.
For each clinical feature explicitly reported by the parent, two questions
were generated: one assessing coverage of relevant KBG syndrome background
information (\textit{syndrome\_background}) and one assessing coverage of
relevant recommendations, screening, treatment, monitoring, referral, or
therapy (\textit{recommendation}). Question generation was restricted to
features present in the consultation \cite{scialom_questeval_2021, moramarco_towards_2021}. The corresponding prompts are provided
in Appendix~\ref{appx:syndrome_coverage_prompt}.

Each question was evaluated against the generated report and assigned:

\begin{equation}
v_j =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if the requested information is explicitly addressed},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Separate scores were calculated for syndrome background and recommendations:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_B}\sum_{j=1}^{N_B}v_j^{B},
\qquad
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_{Rec}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{Rec}}v_j^{Rec}.
\end{equation}

Here, $N_B$ and $N_{Rec}$ denote the numbers of background and
recommendation questions, respectively. Background coverage required the
report to address how the relevant feature affects people with KBG syndrome,
while recommendation coverage required explicit, feature-relevant clinical
advice. Missing, ambiguous, or inferred information was assigned $0$.

The three component scores were retained separately and subsequently
averaged to obtain the overall coverage score:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Overall}(R)
=
\frac{
\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}(R)
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}(R)
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}(R)
}{3}.
\end{equation}

This provides separate and overall measures of retention of patient-specific
findings, syndrome-level information, and recommendations.
\subsection{Conciseness Assessment}
\label{sec:conciseness}

Conciseness was evaluated as a composite measure of information retention,
factual reliability, and report brevity. This reflects the requirement that
generated clinical reports should preserve relevant information and factual
accuracy without unnecessary verbosity.

For each report, token length $L_i$ was calculated using the tokenizer of the
evaluated model. Token lengths were min--max normalised across the evaluation
set:

\begin{equation}
L_i^{\mathrm{norm}}
=
\frac{L_i-L_{\min}}
{L_{\max}-L_{\min}},
\end{equation}

where $L_{\min}$ and $L_{\max}$ are the minimum and maximum report lengths.
A brevity factor was then defined as:

\begin{equation}
B_i = 1-L_i^{\mathrm{norm}},
\end{equation}

so that shorter reports receive higher brevity scores. The final conciseness
score was calculated using the geometric mean of coverage, factuality, and
brevity:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Conciseness}_i
=
\left(
\mathrm{Coverage}_i
\cdot
\mathrm{Factuality}_i
\cdot
B_i
\right)^{1/3}.
\end{equation}

The geometric mean ensures that high performance in one dimension cannot
fully compensate for poor performance in another. Thus, a high score requires
the report to be sufficiently informative, factually reliable, and concise.
\subsection{Readability Assessment}
\label{sec:readability}

Readability was evaluated to assess the linguistic accessibility of the
generated clinical reports using four established text-based measures. The
Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) score provides a general measure of reading ease,
with higher scores indicating greater readability \cite{flesch_new_1948}. The Flesch--Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) estimates the
educational grade level required to comprehend the text \cite{tanprasert_flesch-kincaid_2021}. The Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) index
estimates reading difficulty based primarily on the use of polysyllabic words \cite{mclaughlin_smog_1969}. The Dale--Chall score assesses readability based
on word familiarity and sentence length \cite{crossley_large-scaled_2023}.

All four measures were computed for each generated report using the cleaned
report text and the \texttt{textstat} implementation. The measures were
retained independently to capture complementary aspects of textual
difficulty and account for their different scoring interpretations. The
resulting readability scores were summarised across the evaluation set using
the mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and quartiles.
\subsection{Medical Tone Assessment}
\label{sec:medical_tone}

Medical tone was evaluated to assess whether generated reports used
professional, respectful, and patient-centred clinical language. The
assessment followed a two-stage LLM-based procedure. First, patient-specific
content was extracted from each report, including statements describing how
the condition affects the patient and recommendations for screening or
treatment. Syndrome-level background information and general medical
information were excluded. The extraction used few-shot prompting, with the
complete prompt provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:medical_tone_prompt}.

The extracted statements were then evaluated using a separate few-shot
evaluation prompt across three binary dimensions: \textit{professionalism},
\textit{respectfulness}, and \textit{patient-centred communication}. These
dimensions reflect the importance of clear, appropriate, respectful, and
patient-focused communication in clinical contexts \cite{bowden_evaluating_nodate, rattanapitoon_beyond_2025, omar_impact_2025}.
Each statement received a binary score:

\begin{equation}
s_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if statement } i \text{ satisfies criterion } j,\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $j \in \{P,R,C\}$ represents professionalism, respectfulness, and
patient-centred communication, respectively. For each report, the score for
criterion $j$ was calculated as the proportion of extracted statements
receiving a positive evaluation:

\begin{equation}
S_j =
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{ij},
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the number of extracted patient-specific statements. The three
dimensions were retained separately to distinguish different aspects of
medical tone rather than combining them into a single score. Scores were
calculated for all generated reports and subsequently summarised across the
evaluation set.
\section{Knowledge Distillation}
\subsection{Teacher and Student Models}
The proposed framework adopts a teacher--student knowledge
distillation paradigm, in which knowledge generated by a larger teacher
model is transferred to a substantially smaller student model
\cite{hinton_distilling_2015}. This architecture separates
knowledge generation from efficient deployment, allowing
the student to acquire task-specific capabilities from the teacher while
operating with considerably lower computational and memory requirements \cite{moslemi_survey_2024-1}.

The teacher model was
\textit{Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507}, an instruction-tuned
mixture-of-experts (MoE) model with approximately 30 billion total
parameters and approximately 3 billion activated parameters per token \cite{yang_qwen3_2025}. The teacher was used to generate the
synthetic doctor--patient consultations and corresponding personalised
medical reports that formed the primary source of supervision for
subsequent student training. During inference, the teacher was loaded using
4-bit NormalFloat-4 (NF4) quantisation with bfloat16 computation to reduce
memory requirements while preserving the required generation capability.

The student model was
\textit{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct}, an instruction-tuned model containing
approximately 1.5 billion parameters \cite{qwen_qwen25_2025}. It
was selected as the compact deployment model because its substantially
smaller parameter count makes it more suitable for resource-constrained and
local inference. Rather than reproducing the full capacity of the teacher,
the student is trained to acquire the task-specific behaviour required for
personalised medical report generation.

Let $T$ denote the teacher and $S_{\theta}$ denote the student parameterised
by $\theta$. Given a consultation $x$, the teacher generates a target
response $y_T$:

\begin{equation}
y_T = T(x).
\end{equation}

The resulting teacher-generated response provides supervision for the
student, which models the conditional distribution

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y\mid x).
\end{equation}

The substantial difference in model scale provides a direct measure of the
intended efficiency gain. Let $N_T$ and $N_S$ denote the total parameter
counts of the teacher and student, respectively. The relative parameter
reduction is defined as

\begin{equation}
R = 1-\frac{N_S}{N_T}.
\end{equation}

Using approximately $30$B parameters for the teacher and $1.5$B for the
student gives

\begin{equation}
R \approx 1-\frac{1.5}{30}=0.95,
\end{equation}

corresponding to an approximately 95\% reduction in total
parameter count.

The resulting pipeline therefore consists of a large
teacher model for knowledge and supervision generation and a
compact student model for efficient task execution. The student
was subsequently established through supervised fine-tuning and further
optimised using the proposed quality-weighted distillation and
preference-based learning approaches. This provides the foundation for
investigating whether clinically relevant report-generation capabilities
can be transferred from a large model to a substantially smaller model
without compromising the desired report quality.
\subsection{Supervised Fine-Tuning of the Student Model}
\label{sec:student_sft}

Following the teacher--student model selection, the student model was first
adapted to the personalised medical report generation task using supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) \cite{chakrabarti_automated_2025, zhou_enhancing_2025}. This stage establishes the baseline task-specific student
before applying the subsequent quality-weighted distillation and preference
optimisation approaches. The training data consisted of
teacher-generated consultation--report pairs, where each doctor--parent
consultation was used as the input and its corresponding personalised
medical report served as the target output. This formulation allows the
student to learn the reporting structure, clinical information selection,
and response characteristics demonstrated by the teacher \cite{kim_sequence-level_2016}.

The student, \textit{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct}, was adapted using
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), a parameter-efficient fine-tuning
method that introduces trainable low-rank updates while keeping the
pretrained model parameters frozen \cite{hu_lora_2021}. LoRA reduces the
number of parameters requiring optimisation and consequently lowers the
memory requirements of fine-tuning while retaining the pretrained
representations of the underlying language model.

For a pretrained weight matrix
$W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, LoRA parameterises its task-specific
adaptation as

\begin{equation}
W = W_0 + \Delta W,
\qquad
\Delta W = BA,
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r},
\qquad
A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k},
\qquad
r \ll \min(d,k).
\end{equation}

Here, $r$ denotes the LoRA rank. The pretrained matrix $W_0$ is frozen
during optimisation, while the low-rank matrices $A$ and $B$ are updated
to capture the task-specific parameter adaptation. In this work, LoRA
adapters were applied to the attention projection layers
(\textit{q\_proj}, \textit{k\_proj}, \textit{v\_proj}, and
\textit{o\_proj}) and the feed-forward projection layers
(\textit{gate\_proj}, \textit{up\_proj}, and \textit{down\_proj}) \cite{lelle_token-level_2026}.
The complete LoRA and optimisation configuration is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

Given a training dataset

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i)
\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ represents a formatted doctor--parent consultation and $y_i$
represents the corresponding teacher-generated report, the student was
optimised using the standard autoregressive language-modelling objective.
The conditional probability of the target sequence is factorised
token-by-token as

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y_i\mid x_i)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T_i}
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right),
\end{equation}

where $T_i$ is the number of target tokens and $y_{i,<t}$ denotes the
sequence of target tokens preceding position $t$. The corresponding
negative log-likelihood objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\end{equation}

Thus, SFT minimises the token-level cross-entropy between the student's
predicted distribution and the teacher-provided target sequence \cite{li_preserving_2025}. Only the
trainable LoRA parameters are updated during optimisation, while the
pretrained student parameters remain frozen \cite{hu_lora_2021}.

The implementation used the \textit{SFTTrainer} framework from TRL for
causal language-model fine-tuning \cite{trl_sft}. Training was performed
with a maximum sequence length of 4096 tokens and an effective batch size
of 8, achieved through gradient accumulation. The optimisation employed
AdamW with 8-bit parameter states, a cosine learning-rate schedule, and a
warm-up phase. The student was trained for five epochs, with validation
performed at the end of each epoch. The complete training configuration,
including the LoRA rank, scaling factor, target modules, learning rate,
batch configuration, optimiser, scheduler, and sequence length, is
reported in Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

The resulting SFT model provides the principal student baseline against
which the subsequent distillation strategies are evaluated. However, the
standard SFT objective treats each training pair uniformly, irrespective
of the quality or clinical reliability of the teacher-generated target.
Consequently, lower-quality examples can contribute to the optimisation
objective with the same magnitude as higher-quality examples. This
limitation motivates the subsequent Quality-Weighted Knowledge
Distillation stage, where the contribution of individual teacher-generated
examples is explicitly modulated according to their evaluated quality.
\subsection{Quality-Weighted Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:quality_weighted_distillation}

The supervised fine-tuning stage treats all teacher-generated
consultation--report pairs as equally informative. However, the quality of
synthetic supervision can vary across examples. In the present clinical
report generation task, this variation is particularly important because
the generated reports are required to preserve clinically relevant
information, remain factually grounded, and maintain an appropriate medical
tone. Consequently, uniformly weighting all training examples may cause
lower-quality teacher outputs to exert the same optimisation influence as
higher-quality outputs.

To address this limitation, a \textbf{quality-weighted response-based
knowledge distillation} objective was introduced. The method retains the
hard teacher-generated responses used during SFT, while incorporating an
example-level quality signal into the optimisation objective. This extends
the teacher--student distillation framework introduced by
Hinton et al.~\cite{hinton2015distilling} by explicitly accounting for the
reliability of individual teacher demonstrations.

Let the teacher-generated training dataset be

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i)
\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ denotes a doctor--parent consultation and $y_i$ denotes the
corresponding personalised report generated by the teacher. For each
report, three independently evaluated quality dimensions were obtained:
clinical information coverage ($C_i$), factuality ($F_i$), and medical
tone ($M_i$). Each dimension was represented as a normalised score in
$[0,1]$.

A single supervision-quality score was obtained by combining these
dimensions using their geometric mean:

\begin{equation}
q_i =
\left(
C_i F_i M_i
\right)^{1/3}.
\label{eq:quality_score}
\end{equation}

The geometric mean was selected because it imposes a multiplicative
interaction between the quality dimensions. In contrast to an arithmetic
mean, a high score in one dimension cannot fully compensate for a
substantially lower score in another. Thus, the quality signal favours
teacher demonstrations that provide a balanced combination of clinical
coverage, factual reliability, and appropriate medical communication.

For an autoregressive student model parameterised by $\theta$, the
conditional probability of a target report is factorised as

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y_i\mid x_i)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T_i}
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right),
\end{equation}

where $T_i$ is the number of target tokens and $y_{i,<t}$ denotes the
target-token prefix preceding position $t$. The conventional SFT
negative log-likelihood objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\label{eq:sft_loss}
\end{equation}

The proposed quality-weighted objective modifies this loss by introducing
the example-specific quality coefficient $q_i$:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QKD}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
q_i
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\label{eq:qkd_loss}
\end{equation}

Hence, the gradient contribution of training example $i$ is scaled by its
quality score:

\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathrm{QKD}}
=
q_i
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathrm{SFT}}.
\end{equation}

This formulation provides a direct mechanism through which the quality of
teacher supervision influences student learning. A teacher-generated
example with a high value of $q_i$ contributes strongly to the optimisation
gradient, whereas an example with a lower quality score contributes less.
Importantly, the weighting is applied at the example level, meaning that
all target tokens belonging to a given consultation--report pair inherit
the same quality coefficient. The autoregressive token-prediction
objective itself is therefore unchanged; only the relative importance of
the training examples is modified.

The proposed approach can consequently be viewed as
\textit{quality-aware selective knowledge transfer}. Standard SFT assumes
that every teacher demonstration provides equally reliable supervision,
whereas the proposed objective induces a quality-dependent weighting over
the teacher-generated dataset. The student is therefore encouraged to
prioritise behaviours demonstrated by examples that simultaneously exhibit
strong clinical coverage, factual consistency, and appropriate medical
tone.

This formulation also establishes a direct link between the evaluation and
distillation stages of the framework. Rather than using coverage,
factuality, and medical tone solely as post-hoc evaluation metrics, their
combined score is transformed into a training signal that determines the
relative contribution of each synthetic example. The evaluation framework
therefore functions both as a measure of generated-report quality and as a
mechanism for controlling the transfer of teacher knowledge.

The student architecture, LoRA parameterisation, and underlying
autoregressive training procedure remain unchanged from the SFT baseline.
The principal methodological difference is therefore the introduction of
the quality-dependent weighting term $q_i$ in Equation~\ref{eq:qkd_loss}.
The complete optimisation and implementation configuration is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

This formulation enables a controlled comparison between uniformly
weighted SFT and quality-weighted distillation, isolating the effect of
supervision quality on the transfer of teacher-generated knowledge to the
compact student model.
\section{Direct Preference Optimisation}

\label{sec:dpo}

Following supervised fine-tuning and quality-weighted knowledge
distillation, Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) was investigated as a
preference-based optimisation stage. Unlike SFT, which learns from a single
target response, and quality-weighted distillation, which assigns different
importance to teacher-generated examples, DPO learns from \textit{relative
preferences} between two candidate responses for the same input \cite{rafailov_direct_2024}.

For each consultation $x_i$, a preference pair was constructed consisting
of a preferred response $y_i^{+}$ and a rejected response $y_i^{-}$.
The preferred response represents the higher-quality candidate according
to the established report-evaluation framework, while the rejected response
represents the comparatively lower-quality candidate. The resulting
preference dataset is therefore defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i^{+},y_i^{-})
\right\}_{i=1}^{N}.
\end{equation}

This formulation allows the student to learn not only what constitutes a
valid response, but also which of two plausible responses is preferable
for the same clinical input.

\subsubsection*{DPO Objective}

DPO provides a direct optimisation objective for preference learning without
requiring a separately trained reward model\cite{rafailov_direct_2024}. Let $\pi_{\theta}$ denote the trainable student
policy and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ denote a frozen reference policy. For a
given consultation $x_i$, the DPO objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)
=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+},y^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}}
\left[
\log
\sigma
\left(
\beta
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}\mid x)}
-
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}\mid x)}
\right]
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:dpo_loss}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function and
$\beta$ controls the strength of the preference optimisation relative to
the reference policy.

For an autoregressive language model, the sequence-level log-probability
appearing in Equation~\ref{eq:dpo_loss} is decomposed into token-level
conditional probabilities:

\begin{equation}
\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\log
\pi_{\theta}
\left(
y_t
\mid
x,y_{<t}
\right).
\end{equation}

Consequently, the optimisation compares the relative likelihood assigned
by the student to the preferred and rejected responses, while simultaneously
measuring their deviation from the reference policy.

The central quantity in the DPO objective is therefore the relative
log-probability difference

\begin{equation}
\Delta_i =
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i^{+}\mid x_i)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{+}\mid x_i)}
\right]
-
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i^{-}\mid x_i)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{-}\mid x_i)}
\right].
\end{equation}

Minimising Equation~\ref{eq:dpo_loss} encourages $\Delta_i$ to increase,
thereby increasing the student's relative preference for $y_i^{+}$ over
$y_i^{-}$. The reference-policy terms constrain this optimisation by
penalising uncontrolled deviation from the behaviour represented by the
reference model. This provides a preference-learning mechanism without
requiring explicit reward-model training or reinforcement-learning policy
updates\cite{rafailov_direct_2024}.

\subsubsection*{Preference Construction}

Preference pairs were constructed from candidate personalised reports
generated for the same doctor--parent consultation. The previously defined
quality evaluation framework provided the basis for determining the
relative quality of candidate responses. Coverage, factuality, and medical
tone were used to assess the clinical suitability of the candidates.

For a candidate report $y$, the corresponding composite quality score was
defined as

\begin{equation}
q(y)
=
\left(
C(y)\,F(y)\,M(y)
\right)^{1/3},
\end{equation}

where $C(y)$, $F(y)$, and $M(y)$ represent the coverage, factuality, and
medical-tone scores, respectively. For two candidate responses generated
for the same consultation, $y_a$ and $y_b$, the preferred response was
selected according to their relative quality:

\begin{equation}
y^{+}
=
\arg\max_{y\in\{y_a,y_b\}} q(y),
\qquad
y^{-}
=
\arg\min_{y\in\{y_a,y_b\}} q(y).
\end{equation}

This construction converts the multidimensional report-quality assessment
into pairwise supervision. Rather than requiring an absolute quality label
during optimisation, DPO receives only the ordering between the preferred
and rejected responses.

\subsubsection*{Relationship to SFT and Quality-Weighted Distillation}

The three optimisation stages differ in the form of supervision supplied
to the student. SFT learns from individual input--target pairs and
minimises the negative log-likelihood of the teacher-generated target.
Quality-weighted distillation retains this formulation but scales each
example according to its composite quality score. DPO instead provides
pairwise preference supervision and explicitly optimises the relative
probability of preferred versus rejected responses.

Formally, the progression can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
\text{SFT}
\rightarrow
\text{quality-weighted response distillation}
\rightarrow
\text{preference optimisation}.
\end{equation}

SFT therefore answers the question of \textit{what response should be
generated}, quality-weighted distillation additionally determines
\textit{which demonstrations should have greater training influence}, and
DPO learns \textit{which of two candidate responses should be preferred}.
This provides three progressively different mechanisms for transferring
and refining the teacher-derived behaviour of the compact student model.

Unlike reinforcement-learning-based preference optimisation, DPO does not
require explicit reward-model fitting or online policy sampling during the
optimisation stage \cite{rafailov_direct_2024}. Its direct likelihood-based
objective makes it particularly suitable for controlled offline
optimisation using the preference pairs constructed from the generated
clinical reports.

The student model and parameter-efficient LoRA configuration remained
consistent with the preceding optimisation stages, allowing the effect of
the preference objective to be evaluated without introducing changes to
the underlying student architecture. The complete DPO hyperparameters and
preference-pair construction configuration are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.
\section{Evaluation of Student-Generated Reports}
\label{sec:student_evaluation}

Following supervised fine-tuning, quality-weighted knowledge distillation,
and preference optimisation, the resulting student models were evaluated
on a held-out test set to determine whether the knowledge transferred from
the teacher was retained while reducing model size and computational cost.
The same evaluation framework established for the teacher-generated reports
was applied to the student-generated reports, ensuring that performance
could be compared consistently across models.

The evaluation was performed across two complementary aspects: 
\textit{clinical report quality} and \textit{output-level knowledge
transfer}. Clinical quality was assessed using the previously defined
coverage, factuality, conciseness, readability, and medical-tone measures.
These metrics were applied without modification to the student outputs,
allowing the quality of the distilled reports to be compared directly with
the corresponding teacher/reference reports. Their definitions and
calculation procedures are provided in the preceding evaluation sections.

In addition, output-level knowledge transfer was assessed using lexical and
semantic similarity between student-generated reports and the corresponding
teacher/reference reports. ROUGE and BLEU were used to measure lexical
correspondence, while BERTScore and embedding-based similarity were used to
assess semantic alignment. These measures provide complementary evidence
of whether the student reproduces the information and semantic content
expressed by the teacher, while allowing for differences in surface wording
\cite{lin2004rouge,papineni2002bleu,zhang2020bertscore}.

The evaluation was conducted independently for the SFT, quality-weighted
distillation, and DPO student models. For each model, the same held-out
test consultations were used and reports were generated using a consistent
inference procedure. This controlled evaluation isolates differences
arising from the training strategy rather than differences in the
evaluation data or protocol.

complement the quality evaluation by determining whether improvements in
efficiency are achieved without unacceptable degradation in report quality.

The overall evaluation therefore examines three related outcomes:
\textit{clinical quality}, \textit{knowledge transfer}, and
\textit{computational efficiency}. The resulting measurements enable direct
comparison of SFT, quality-weighted distillation, and DPO and provide the
basis for determining whether the proposed distillation strategies retain
teacher-level behaviour while enabling substantially more efficient
student-model inference.

\chapter{Patient-Specific Coverage Evaluation Prompts}
\label{appx:patient_coverage_prompt}

The following prompts were used for patient-specific coverage evaluation
using a two-stage zero-shot strategy: atomic claim extraction from the
doctor--parent consultation followed by independent verification of each
claim against the generated report. No task-specific demonstrations or
labelled examples were provided.


\section*{Stage 1: Patient-Specific Claim Extraction}

A zero-shot instruction-based prompt was used to extract clinically
meaningful patient-specific claims. Only information explicitly stated by
the parent about the child was retained, while questions, \textit{N/A}
responses, subjective content, unsupported inferences, and duplicates were
excluded. Complex statements were decomposed into distinct clinical facts
where appropriate.


\begin{lstlisting}[
    breaklines=true,
    breakatwhitespace=false,
    basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,
    columns=fullflexible,
    keepspaces=true
]

Extract patient-specific clinical claims from a doctor-parent
consultation.

Rules:

- Extract only clinical information explicitly stated by the parent
  about the child.
- Do not extract the parent's emotions, uncertainty, opinions,
  concerns, intentions, or plans.
- Do not infer information or use external medical knowledge.

- Split a statement only when it contains multiple distinct clinical facts.
