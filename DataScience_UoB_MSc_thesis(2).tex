\chapter*{Abstract}
Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) comprise a diverse group of conditions
whose clinical presentation can vary substantially between individuals. This
heterogeneity can make it challenging to communicate complex clinical
information in a way that is both accessible and tailored to an individual
patient. The challenge is particularly pronounced for rare disorders such as
KBG syndrome, where a personalised report must distinguish between
characteristics supported by a patient's clinical information and those known
to occur more broadly within the syndrome. Large language models (LLMs) offer
considerable potential for generating such reports by synthesising complex
clinical information into coherent patient-specific narratives. However, their
use is constrained by hallucination, inadequate grounding in patient-specific
evidence, privacy requirements, and substantial computational demands. Small
language models offer a more efficient alternative, but may lack the knowledge
and capabilities required for complex clinical generation. This motivates the
use of knowledge distillation to transfer useful capabilities from larger
models to compact models. This study
therefore investigates whether knowledge distillation can be improved by
explicitly accounting for variation in the quality of teacher-generated
supervision. Rather than treating all teacher reports equally, the proposed
approach assesses reports across multiple dimensions of clinical quality and
uses these assessments to determine their influence during student training.
The study examines whether this multidimensional quality-aware approach can
improve clinically grounded personalised report generation in a substantially
smaller language model.

A controlled synthetic KBG syndrome report-generation pipeline was developed
using a 70B-parameter Llama-3.3-Instruct teacher model and a 1.5B-parameter
Qwen2.5-Instruct student model. A dataset of 800 synthetic doctor--parent
consultations and teacher-generated reports was constructed for training and
evaluation. Teacher reports were assessed across factuality, coverage,
conciseness, readability, and medical tone. Reports meeting a minimum
factuality requirement were retained, while multidimensional quality
assessments were incorporated into supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to determine
the influence of individual training examples. Quality-aware SFT was evaluated
against a baseline model, conventional SFT, and Direct Preference Optimisation
(DPO) using clinical quality, teacher--student similarity, and
inference-efficiency measures.

Quality-aware SFT achieved the strongest overall balance of clinical report
quality. Compared with conventional SFT, coverage increased from 0.46 to 0.55
(+0.10) and conciseness from 0.46 to 0.59 (+0.13), with both improvements
statistically significant. Factuality increased from 0.67 to 0.71 (+0.04),
although this difference was not statistically significant. DPO produced no
statistically significant improvement over quality-aware SFT across the
evaluated clinical dimensions. The student retained 72.6--78.1\% of teacher
performance across factuality, coverage, and conciseness while reducing
parameter count by 46.7-fold, peak GPU memory usage by 95.7\%, and increasing
token throughput by 36.8\%. However, per-report latency increased because the
student generated substantially longer outputs, highlighting a trade-off
between throughput and generation length. High semantic similarity also did
not reliably correspond to factual correctness, demonstrating the limitations
of similarity-based evaluation alone.

Overall, the findings show that multidimensional quality-aware supervision can
improve selected aspects of clinically grounded report generation in compact
language models while substantially reducing computational requirements. The
results further demonstrate that successful model compression should be
evaluated jointly in terms of clinical quality, grounding, and computational
efficiency rather than resource reduction alone. Within the controlled
synthetic KBG syndrome setting, this study provides evidence that
multidimensional quality-aware knowledge distillation is a promising approach
for developing more efficient clinical language models, while further
validation on real clinical data and broader medical domains remains necessary.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) comprise a diverse group of conditions
affecting cognition, communication, learning, behaviour, motor abilities, and
social functioning. Clinical presentations can vary substantially even within
the same diagnosis, in terms of the type, combination, and severity of symptoms
\cite{gao_genetic_2025,assadourian_precision_2025}. This heterogeneity is
particularly challenging in rare genetic NDDs, where parents may need to
understand complex conditions and communicate their child's needs to healthcare
professionals, who may have limited familiarity with rare disorders. A
personalised, parent-held report could help address this challenge by combining
patient-specific findings with condition-specific knowledge, including
associated features and monitoring recommendations. However, producing such
reports manually requires considerable clinical knowledge and time, limiting
their routine use \cite{patel_descriptive_2023, carter_qualitative_2025, low_identifying_2025}.

KBG syndrome (KBGS) illustrates this challenge. It is a rare genetic
neurodevelopmental disorder associated with pathogenic variants or deletions
involving the \textit{ANKRD11} gene, with considerable variation in clinical
presentation between individuals \cite{martinez-cayuelas_clinical_2023}.
Personalised reporting must therefore relate individual clinical findings to
syndrome-level knowledge while remaining comprehensive, factually grounded,
and understandable to its intended audience.

Given these challenges, automated clinical report generation has attracted
growing interest. Large language models (LLMs) can integrate information from
multiple sources and generate coherent clinical narratives, supporting
applications such as medical summarisation, documentation, and patient
information generation
\cite{yuan_continued_2024,rehman_advancement_2025,alabed_large_2026,
che_llm-driven_2026,makram_ai_2024}. However, fluency does not guarantee
clinical reliability, as LLMs may hallucinate, omit relevant information, or
introduce inconsistencies \cite{zeba_mitigating_2026}. Clinical reports must
therefore accurately reflect patient information, provide relevant coverage,
and avoid unsupported claims
\cite{rossi_future_2023,yang_knowledge_2022,dahlberg_measuring_2026}.
Privacy requirements and the computational cost of large models can further
limit local deployment for sensitive clinical data
\cite{annas_hipaa_2003,noauthor_eu_nodate,zhong_considerations_2025,
li_collm_2024}. An alternative is provided by small language models (SLMs),
which offer lower memory and inference requirements but generally have less
knowledge and reasoning capacity than larger models
\cite{wang_comprehensive_2025,turki_evaluating_2025}. This motivates methods
for transferring useful capabilities from larger models to compact models
while retaining their efficiency advantages.

Knowledge distillation provides such a mechanism by transferring useful
capabilities from a larger teacher model to a smaller student model
\cite{hinton_distilling_2015,wang_knowledge_2022,gou_knowledge_2021}. In
language models, knowledge can be transferred through internal
representations, output distributions, or generated responses
\cite{yang_categories_2023,habib_knowledge_2024,park_relational_2019}.
Response-based distillation is particularly relevant to generative tasks
because teacher-generated responses provide direct, task-specific examples of
the behaviour the student is expected to reproduce
\cite{chen_distilling_2021}. For personalised clinical report generation, this
provides a natural framework in which a larger model can integrate
patient-specific information with clinical knowledge and generate reports that
supervise a more compact student model.

However, the effectiveness of response-based distillation depends not only on
teacher capability but also on the quality of the supervision it provides.
Teacher-generated responses may contain factual errors, unsupported claims,
omissions, irrelevant information, or undesirable response characteristics
\cite{zhang_lifting_2023,montenegro_what_2025,davidson_reasoning-driven_2026}.
Because these responses become training targets, variation in their quality
can affect knowledge transfer. Treating all responses as equally valuable
therefore assumes uniform supervision quality that may not hold in practice.

Existing approaches have addressed this problem through filtering and
weighting \cite{liu_air_2025-1,lei_learning_2025,lee_what_2026}. Filtering
removes responses that fail a predefined quality criterion, but may also
discard responses that are imperfect according to one criterion while
retaining useful information \cite{shen_logitwise_2025}. Weighting provides a less restrictive
alternative by allowing responses to contribute to training to different
degrees \cite{zhao_rethinking_2026}. Its effectiveness, however, depends on
the signal used to determine each response's contribution.

Recent response-based distillation methods use signals related to teacher
reliability, example difficulty, token-level importance, or the functional
relevance of response components
\cite{chen_knowledge_2024,jiang_difficulty-aware_2027,tang_smartad_2026}.
These approaches move beyond uniform supervision, but their signals generally
capture a particular property of a response rather than its broader quality.
A response may be reliable according to one signal while omitting important
information or expressing it inefficiently. The limitation is therefore not
weighting itself, but the narrow basis on which the contribution of a response
is determined.

This issue is particularly important for personalised clinical reports,
where quality is inherently multidimensional. Factual correctness is
necessary, but a factually correct response may still provide incomplete
coverage of relevant patient-specific information, while greater coverage
does not necessarily result in concise or appropriately expressed
communication \cite{li_llm-as--judge_2026-1,ahmad_finetuning_2026}. These dimensions provide complementary, and potentially competing, signals of teacher supervision value. A single quality or importance measure may therefore fail to capture important differences between otherwise plausible responses \cite{liu_g-eval_2023, fabbri_summeval_2021}.

Multidimensional assessment of training examples has been explored in
instruction tuning, with approaches such as Deita and XCoder using multiple
criteria to identify and curate higher-quality training data
\cite{liu_what_2024,wang_how_2024}. In parallel, response-based distillation
has increasingly moved beyond treating all teacher outputs equally through
filtering and weighting strategies
\cite{liu_air_2025-1,lei_learning_2025,lee_what_2026}. However, the
intersection of these approaches within response-based distillation has
received comparatively limited attention. Existing methods have largely
treated example selection and response weighting as separate problems, with
less emphasis on using multiple explicitly defined dimensions of
teacher-response quality to determine the contribution of each response to
the distillation objective. This leaves open the question of whether a multidimensional view of response quality can provide more informed supervision during response-based distillation.

This gap is particularly relevant to personalised clinical report generation,
where factuality, information coverage, conciseness and communication quality represent distinct
aspects of report quality. This dissertation therefore investigates a
quality-aware distillation approach in which each teacher-generated response's
contribution is informed by multiple dimensions of response quality. This
allows useful but imperfect supervision to be retained while reducing the
influence of lower-quality responses, rather than relying on a single
retain-or-discard decision or notion of response importance.

The approach is investigated using KBG syndrome as a controlled clinical case
study. A synthetic patient-profile pipeline generates patient-specific
doctor--parent consultations, which are combined with KBG syndrome reference
knowledge to provide context for a larger teacher language model to generate
personalised clinical reports. These reports provide response-based
supervision for a substantially smaller student model. The quality-aware
approach uses multiple dimensions of teacher-response quality to determine
each response's contribution during training. The resulting student is
compared with conventional supervised fine-tuning and Direct Preference
Optimisation (DPO), alongside an untrained baseline.

A multi-stage evaluation framework is used to assess both teacher and student reports. A rubric-based LLM-as-a-judge evaluates reports across multiple dimensions of clinical quality. To support fine-grained assessment, the evaluation uses atomic-fact decomposition for relevant dimensions and formulates other assessment criteria as atomic binary questions. These assessments also provide the signals used to determine teacher-response contributions during quality-aware distillation. Complementary lexical and semantic similarity metrics provide additional evidence of correspondence with reference texts. Finally, the student model is assessed not only for report quality but also for its practical efficiency, using model size, memory consumption, throughput, and latency.

The study is guided by the following research question:
\vspace{-0.2em}
\begin{quote}
\textit{Can multidimensional quality-aware knowledge distillation improve the
clinical quality of reports generated by compact language models compared
with conventional supervised fine-tuning?}
\end{quote}
\vspace{-0.2em}
To investigate this question, the dissertation makes the following four
contributions:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt, leftmargin=*]

    \item Developed a controlled synthetic data-generation pipeline for diverse patient profiles, doctor--parent consultations, and personalised clinical reports.

    \item Proposed and evaluated a multidimensional quality-aware response-based distillation framework that uses teacher-response quality to determine the contribution of individual demonstrations during student training.

    \item Compared an untrained baseline, conventional SFT, quality-aware SFT, and DPO for personalised clinical report generation under a controlled experimental setting.

    \item Evaluated clinical report quality, knowledge transfer, and computational efficiency using multidimensional quality measures, model size, memory consumption, throughput, and latency.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}
\section{Personalised Clinical Report Generation}
\label{sec:personalised_clinical_report_generation}

\subsection{Neurogenetic and Neurodevelopmental Disorders}
\label{sec:neurogenetic_disorders}

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of
conditions arising from atypical brain development and functioning, with
effects on cognition, communication, learning, behaviour, and motor and
social development. Genetic factors contribute substantially to many NDDs,
and their clinical manifestations can vary considerably both between and
within diagnostic categories \cite{gao_genetic_2025,assadourian_precision_2025}.
This heterogeneity makes accurate representation of individual patient
characteristics an important consideration in clinical documentation and
reporting.

KBG syndrome (KBGS) is a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder primarily
associated with pathogenic variants or deletions involving the
\textit{ANKRD11} gene. It is characterised by a broad and variable clinical
spectrum that may include developmental delay, intellectual and learning
difficulties, behavioural and neurological abnormalities, characteristic
craniofacial features, macrodontia, short stature, and skeletal
abnormalities. However, the presence and severity of these features vary
substantially between individuals \cite{martinez-cayuelas_clinical_2023}.
Consequently, a generic description of the syndrome cannot fully represent
the clinical characteristics of a particular patient.
\subsection{Personalised Clinical Reporting and Grounding}
\label{sec:clinical_personalisation_grounding}

Personalised clinical reporting refers to adapting clinical information to
the characteristics, clinical presentation, and circumstances of an
individual patient, rather than providing the same generic description of a
condition for every patient. It aims to make clinical information more
relevant to the individual by reflecting their specific presentation and
circumstances \cite{yang_knowledge_2022}. Personalisation may involve
tailoring the description of symptoms and clinical features, as well as
diagnostic considerations, screening, monitoring, or other clinically
relevant information according to the individual patient (citation needed).

This is particularly important in rare genetic and neurodevelopmental
disorders, where individuals with the same diagnosis can exhibit different
combinations and severities of clinical features
\cite{martinez-cayuelas_clinical_2023}. A personalised report can therefore
provide a more meaningful representation of an individual's condition than
a generic disease description, with its content reflecting the patient's
particular clinical presentation and circumstances.
\subsection{LLM-Based Clinical Report Generation}
\label{sec:llm_clinical_report_generation}

Large language models (LLMs) have expanded automated clinical text generation across tasks including clinical summarisation, medical documentation, and patient information generation \cite{yuan_continued_2024,rehman_advancement_2025,alabed_large_2026,che_llm-driven_2026,makram_ai_2024}. Their ability to condition outputs on clinical context makes them particularly relevant to personalised report generation, where patient information can be transformed into structured, patient-specific narratives. For example, Ganzinger et al. \cite{ganzinger_automated_2025} generated German discharge summaries from structured information extracted from electronic health records, while Tariq et al. \cite{tariq_patient_2024} developed patient-sensitive radiology summaries by combining chest CT reports with patient-related characteristics and audience-specific considerations. Similarly, Piya and Beheshti \cite{piya_contextual_2025} showed that clinical summarisation can benefit from combining clinical text with structured domain knowledge retrieved from a knowledge graph. Together, these studies demonstrate how LLMs can integrate patient and contextual information from multiple sources to generate clinically relevant narratives.

However, fluent generation does not guarantee clinical reliability, as LLM-generated clinical text may contain hallucinations, omissions, or inconsistencies \cite{zeba_mitigating_2026}. Evaluation in healthcare settings must therefore consider factual grounding, completeness, and communication quality alongside linguistic quality \cite{dahlberg_measuring_2026}. At the same time, deploying LLMs in healthcare requires careful consideration of privacy and governance when processing sensitive patient information, particularly when using external cloud-based services \cite{annas_hipaa_2003,noauthor_eu_nodate,zhong_considerations_2025,al-dhabyani_data_2026}. While local deployment can help keep sensitive data within the healthcare environment, it often requires substantial memory and computational resources \cite{li_collm_2024}. This trade-off between model capability, clinical reliability, privacy, and deployability motivates the investigation of smaller models suitable for reliable clinical text generation in resource-constrained healthcare settings.
\subsection{Small Language Models}
\label{sec:small_language_models}
Small language models (SLMs) offer a resource-efficient alternative to large
language models, with lower memory, storage, and inference requirements
\cite{wang_comprehensive_2025}. This makes them attractive for healthcare
applications where computational efficiency and local processing of sensitive
clinical information are important \cite{garg_rise_2026}. However, their
smaller parameter count can limit their pre-trained knowledge and reasoning
capacity compared with larger models \cite{turki_evaluating_2025,wang_comprehensive_2025}.
Importantly, these limitations can be mitigated through appropriate
task-specific training, with compact models demonstrating competitive
performance on specialised medical tasks such as biomedical language
processing and medical reasoning \cite{kim_small_2025,rohanian_effectiveness_2022}.

Recent work further shows that well-trained smaller models can approach the
performance of substantially larger models on specialised tasks through
targeted supervision \cite{ye_teaching_2026}. Similarly, the Qwen3 study
demonstrates that smaller models can acquire strong capabilities from larger
models through appropriate training \cite{yang_qwen3_2025}. These findings
highlight that performance on specialised tasks depends not only on model
size, but also on how effectively a model is trained and adapted. Nevertheless,
the remaining capability gap motivates approaches for transferring useful
knowledge from larger models to resource-efficient models, discussed in the
following section.









\section{Knowledge Distillation}
\subsection{Fundamentals of Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:kd_fundamentals}

The need to transfer useful capabilities from larger models to
resource-efficient models has motivated the use of knowledge distillation
(KD), a knowledge-transfer and model-compression technique in which a
smaller student model learns from a larger and typically more capable teacher
model \cite{hinton_distilling_2015,wang_knowledge_2022}. Rather than directly
reducing model size through techniques such as pruning or quantisation, KD
transfers information from the teacher through additional supervision
signals, allowing the student to learn aspects of the teacher's behaviour
beyond conventional hard targets \cite{hinton_distilling_2015,mansourian_comprehensive_2025}.

The general KD framework consists of a teacher that provides informative
training signals and a student that is optimised to reproduce the desired
teacher behaviour while retaining the computational advantages of its
smaller architecture \cite{wang_knowledge_2022}. The transferred knowledge
may take different forms, including output distributions, generated
responses, intermediate representations, or other teacher-derived signals
\cite{gou_knowledge_2021}. The choice of transferred knowledge depends on
the teacher--student setting and the objective of distillation.

The effectiveness of KD therefore depends on how knowledge is defined, what
level of access is available to the teacher, and how the transferred
information is used to train the student \cite{mansourian_comprehensive_2025}.
These considerations have led to several complementary classifications of
KD, including distinctions based on training configuration, teacher access,
and the form of transferred knowledge. The approaches most relevant to
language-model distillation are discussed below.

\subsection{Distillation Paradigms}
\label{sec:distillation_paradigms}

Knowledge distillation can be characterised by the training configuration,
level of teacher access, and form of knowledge transferred. These represent
complementary aspects of the teacher--student relationship rather than
mutually exclusive categories \cite{wang_knowledge_2022,mansourian_comprehensive_2025}.

Based on training configuration, \textit{offline distillation} uses a
pretrained, fixed teacher from which the student learns, whereas
\textit{online distillation} trains teacher and student jointly
\cite{hinton_distilling_2015,wang_knowledge_2022,chen_online_2020}.
\textit{Self-distillation} transfers knowledge within the same model, such
as across training stages or model components, without requiring a separate
teacher \cite{furlanello_born_2018,zhang_be_2019}.

Distillation can also be distinguished by teacher access.
\textit{White-box distillation} uses internal information such as logits,
hidden representations, or attention patterns, whereas
\textit{black-box distillation} relies only on information exposed through
the teacher's outputs \cite{donisch_inference_2024,shi_knowledge_2025}.
The latter is particularly relevant when teacher parameters or internal
representations are inaccessible.

Finally, knowledge may be transferred through teacher responses, output
probability distributions, or intermediate representations, corresponding
to \textit{response-based}, \textit{logit-based}, and
\textit{feature-based} distillation, respectively
\cite{yang_categories_2023,habib_knowledge_2024,park_relational_2019}.
Response-based distillation is particularly suited to generative language
models because teacher-generated text provides direct task-specific
supervision without requiring access to internal model states. This forms
the basis for the LLM-focused distillation considered in the following
subsection.
\subsection{Response-Based Distillation and Teacher-Generated Supervision}
\label{sec:response_based_distillation_llms}
Response-based distillation transfers knowledge from a teacher model to a
smaller student through its observable outputs rather than internal
representations or logits. The teacher generates task-specific responses that
are then used as supervision for the student \cite{wang_knowledge_2026}. In healthcare, this approach has
been used to transfer clinical prediction capabilities to smaller models. For
example, Kehl et al. used teacher-generated predictions to supervise student
models for cancer outcome extraction from imaging reports and medical
oncologist notes \cite{kehl_shareable_2024}. Similarly, Riaz et al. employed a
teacher--student framework to extract overall response and disease progression
according to RECIST from free-text radiology reports
\cite{riaz_empirical_2025}.

Beyond structured outcome extraction, the same principle can be extended to
clinical report generation, where the desired output is a richer natural
language representation of patient information. A teacher LLM can be provided
with patient-specific information and relevant clinical knowledge and prompted
to generate a personalised clinical report. These teacher-generated reports
can then serve as supervision for a smaller student model, enabling it to learn
both how to integrate relevant clinical information and how to communicate it
in an appropriate clinical structure and style.

However, because the student learns directly from teacher outputs, the quality
of this supervision is critical. Errors, omissions, unsupported claims, and
undesirable patterns may be propagated to the student
\cite{zhang_lifting_2023,montenegro_what_2025}. Teacher-generated responses
may also vary in factual correctness, coverage, and clinical communication
quality \cite{davidson_reasoning-driven_2026}. Although low-quality examples
can be filtered, this may discard useful information. An alternative is to
weight examples according to their quality, giving greater importance to
higher-quality responses while reducing the contribution of lower-quality ones
\cite{kuo_not_2025}. This motivates quality-aware knowledge distillation,
where the quality of teacher-generated supervision is explicitly incorporated
into student training.

\subsection{Quality-Aware Knowledge Distillation and Research Gap}
\label{sec:quality_aware_kd_research_gap}
The effectiveness of response-based knowledge distillation depends not only on
the capability of the teacher model, but also on the quality of the supervision
it provides \cite{wen_preparing_2020}. Because teacher-generated responses become training targets for
the student, factual errors, omissions, irrelevant information, and undesirable
response characteristics may also be transferred during distillation \cite{zhang_can_2024}.
Treating all teacher responses as equally reliable therefore assumes that the
quality of the supervision is relatively uniform, which may not always be the
case. This has motivated quality-aware approaches that account for differences
in the quality of teacher-generated supervision
\cite{alba_honest_2026}.

One common approach is quality-aware filtering. In this setting, teacher
responses are evaluated using criteria such as correctness, relevance,
semantic similarity, uncertainty, or difficulty, and responses that do not
meet a specified criterion are removed from the training data \cite{cai_when_2026, zafar_confidence-based_2025}. For example,
Pham et al. filter teacher-generated summaries using
a reference-free quality measure after selecting suitable in-domain
conversations and prompts. Their results show that filtering low-quality
synthetic supervision can improve student performance ~\cite{pham_select_2023}. Such approaches show
that the quality of teacher-generated responses can have an important effect
on the usefulness of the resulting training data.

However, filtering makes a binary decision about each training example. A
response is either retained or discarded. This can be limiting when the
quality of a response varies across different aspects. A response that falls
just below a filtering threshold may still contain useful information, while
a response that passes the threshold may still contain weaknesses that are
not captured by the selected criterion \cite{shen_logitwise_2025}. Filtering can therefore remove
partially useful supervision rather than simply removing harmful supervision.
This issue is particularly relevant in specialised domains such as
rare-disease report generation, where high-quality patient-specific training
data can be difficult to obtain. Retaining imperfect but informative examples
while reducing their influence may therefore provide an alternative to
discarding them completely. QR-Distill, for example, discusses how quality
filtering can reduce the range of knowledge and reasoning patterns available
to the student \cite{lei_learning_2025}.

These limitations have motivated quality-aware weighting as a different way
of handling variation in teacher-generated supervision. Rather than deciding
whether a response should contribute to training, weighting methods allow
different responses to contribute to different degrees. Higher-quality
responses can therefore have a greater influence on the training objective,
while lower-quality responses can remain in the dataset and still provide
potentially useful information. This provides a more gradual way of accounting
for differences in supervision quality than a simple retain-or-discard
decision \cite{sun_uncertainty-aware_2026}.

Recent response-based distillation methods have adopted this idea of
non-uniform supervision, although they differ in how the importance of the
supervision is determined. Proxy-KD weights the
distillation loss according to how reliably a proxy model represents the
black-box teacher~\cite{chen_knowledge_2024}. RDistillRec combines
instance-level difficulty with learned token weights for teacher-generated
rationales~\cite{jiang_difficulty-aware_2027}. SmartAD assigns different weights to
segments of teacher-generated trajectories according to their functional
importance~\cite{tang_smartad_2026}. These approaches therefore move beyond treating all teacher
responses or response components equally. However, their weighting signals
are based on particular notions of importance, such as reliability,
difficulty, token importance, or functional relevance.

The distinction between these weighting signals becomes important when considering
the quality of a generated response more broadly. A response may appear
reliable according to a teacher--proxy agreement measure while still omitting
important information. Conversely, a response may provide comprehensive
information but use language that is unsuitable for its intended audience.
These different aspects of response quality are not necessarily captured by a
single measure of importance. The limitation, therefore, is not the use of
weighting itself, but the narrow basis on which the weighting signal is
defined. Existing approaches provide ways to determine which supervision
should receive greater emphasis, but their weighting signals do not jointly
account for multiple explicit dimensions of generated-response quality.

A multidimensional view of training-data quality has instead been explored in
instruction tuning. Deita evaluates instruction-tuning
data according to complexity, quality, and diversity before selecting training
examples~\cite{liu_what_2024}. XCoder similarly considers instruction
complexity, response quality, and instruction diversity when selecting
code-instruction data~\cite{wang_how_2024}. These studies show that the value of a training
example can be considered from several different perspectives rather than
through a single criterion. However, this multidimensional assessment
is mainly used for data selection and curation in instruction tuning. It is
different from using several quality dimensions to determine how strongly an
individual teacher-generated response should influence a response-based
distillation objective.

Taken together, the literature shows two related but distinct directions.
Quality-aware response-based distillation has introduced non-uniform
supervision through filtering and weighting, while instruction-tuning research
has demonstrated the value of assessing training examples across multiple
dimensions. What remains underexplored is the use of multiple explicitly
defined dimensions of teacher-response quality to determine the contribution
of each teacher-generated response during response-based knowledge
distillation. This distinction is particularly relevant to clinical report
generation, where factual correctness, information coverage, and appropriate
clinical expression represent different aspects of response quality. A
response may be factually reliable but incomplete, or comprehensive but
poorly expressed for its intended audience. These characteristics suggest
that a single quality or importance signal may not fully capture the
differences between teacher-generated responses in such settings.


\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Evaluation is central to knowledge distillation because the quality of the
final student model depends not only on its ability to generate plausible
outputs, but also on the quality of the supervision it receives and the
extent to which this supervision is successfully transferred
\cite{wang_knowledge_2026}. Evaluation therefore needs to consider three
related aspects: whether the teacher provides reliable supervision, whether
the student preserves relevant information during knowledge transfer, and
whether the resulting outputs are clinically useful. Importantly, the same
evaluation signals used to assess clinical quality can also serve as quality
signals for quality-aware knowledge distillation. By quantifying dimensions
such as factuality, coverage, and conciseness, these signals can distinguish
stronger from weaker teacher-generated responses and determine how much
emphasis each supervision example should receive. Evaluation therefore
supports both assessment of the final student model and quality-aware
weighting of the supervision used during distillation.

Clinical report quality is inherently multidimensional because clinical
usefulness cannot be captured by a single similarity or accuracy measure.
Recent work therefore evaluates clinical text across multiple dimensions.
For example, DischargeSim evaluates generated discharge summaries using
automatic metrics and an LLM-based judge across semantic coverage,
factuality, consistency, informativeness, coherence, relevance, fluency,
and personalisation \cite{yao_dischargesim_2025}. Similarly, CLEVER evaluates
clinical LLM outputs across factuality, clinical relevance, and conciseness,
highlighting the need to assess both correctness and usefulness
\cite{kocaman_clinical_2025}. These approaches recognise that a report may
be factually correct yet omit important information, include unnecessary
content, or be poorly suited to its intended purpose. Evaluating these
properties separately therefore provides a more informative assessment of
clinical report quality.

Human evaluation can provide richer judgements of these properties, but it
is costly, time-consuming, and difficult to scale across large collections
of generated reports \cite{yan_automatic_2026}. This has motivated structured
evaluation approaches that define explicit criteria for assessing different
aspects of generated text. Rubrics can specify dimensions such as factuality,
coverage, conciseness, readability, and medical tone, allowing these
properties to be assessed separately \cite{chen_automated_2026}. LLM-based
judges can then apply such rubrics at scale, providing multidimensional
quality scores without relying on a single overall judgement \cite{hashemi_llm-rubric_2024}. These scores
can also serve as quality signals for distinguishing stronger and weaker
teacher-generated supervision.

Such evaluation can be made more fine-grained by decomposing clinical text into individual claims or atomic facts. FactEHR decomposes clinical notes into atomic facts and checks whether each is supported by the source, enabling fine-grained factuality assessment \cite{munnangi_factehr_2025}. Similarly, Ye et al. decompose complex claims into atomic facts and verify them individually against a grounding document \cite{ye_teaching_2026}. AtomiMed applies a similar approach to medical reports, using hierarchical Atomic Clinical Facts to assess whether reference facts are recovered in generated reports, providing measures of coverage and recall \cite{wang_atomimed_2026}. Another approach decomposes evaluation criteria into atomic binary questions, which an LLM evaluator answers with yes-or-no judgements; these can then be aggregated into interpretable scores for different evaluation dimensions \cite{cho_ask_2026}. Together, these approaches show how atomic decomposition can support fine-grained evaluation of factuality, coverage, and completeness rather than treating an entire report as a single unit.

Alongside these structured approaches, automatic similarity measures can
provide complementary evidence about information preservation between
teacher-generated and student-generated reports. Metrics such as ROUGE
\cite{lin_rouge_2004}, BLEU \cite{ward_bleu_2001}, and BERTScore
\cite{zhang_bertscore_2020} quantify lexical or semantic similarity between
generated and reference texts. Although these measures do not directly
establish clinical factuality or patient-specific coverage, they can indicate
the extent to which information expressed in a reference or teacher report
is reflected in the student output \cite{dahlberg_measuring_2026}. They are therefore more appropriately
treated as complementary evidence of knowledge transfer rather than as
standalone measures of clinical quality.

Finally, evaluating a compact model also requires consideration of the
computational resources needed to produce its outputs. Measures such as
parameter count, memory usage, inference time, and token generation speed
provide indicators of model efficiency and practical deployability
\cite{garg_rise_2025}. Considering clinical quality, knowledge transfer, and
computational efficiency together therefore provides a broader basis for
assessing the practical value of compact language models and whether
improvements in generated text are achieved without losing the efficiency
advantages that motivate knowledge distillation.

\section{Problem Formulation and Overall Framework}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}
This study investigates quality-aware response-based knowledge distillation
for transferring personalised clinical report-generation capabilities from a
large language model to a compact student model, with particular emphasis
on how teacher supervision quality influences this transfer. The target task
is the generation of clinically grounded, personalised reports for patients
with KBG syndrome from doctor--parent consultation information and
syndrome-level clinical knowledge.

The framework follows a controlled pipeline in which synthetic data
construction provides the task inputs, teacher generation provides the
supervision, quality assessment determines its value, and student
optimisation evaluates the resulting transfer. Curated patient profiles
first introduce clinically relevant variation, which is used to generate
patient-specific doctor--parent consultations. These consultations are
then combined with syndrome-level clinical knowledge and provided to a
large teacher model to generate personalised reports, forming the
task-specific supervision data for student adaptation.

Let $x_i$ denote the doctor--parent consultation for patient $i$,
$c$ the fixed KBG syndrome clinical reference, and $T$ the teacher model.
The teacher generates a personalised report $y_i$ as

\begin{equation}
y_i = T(x_i,c),
\qquad
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{teacher}}
=
\left\{(x_i,c,y_i)\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{teacher}}$ denotes the resulting teacher
supervision dataset. The compact student model $S_{\theta}$ is then adapted
to model

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y\mid x,c),
\end{equation}

using these teacher-generated demonstrations.

Before training, the synthetic doctor--parent consultations are assessed for
diversity and variability across patient interactions, including lexical,
structural, grammatical, and semantic variation, to ensure that they capture
the heterogeneous nature of realistic patient communication. The validated
consultations are then passed to the teacher model for personalised report
generation, after which the resulting reports undergo multidimensional
clinical-quality assessment. This assessment evaluates each teacher-generated
report across multiple dimensions of quality and provides the signals for
quality-aware response-based distillation. Specifically, the resulting
quality scores determine the relative contribution of individual teacher
responses during student training, allowing higher-quality supervision to
exert greater influence while reducing the contribution of lower-quality
responses. The same quality assessments are used to construct preferred and
less-preferred responses for Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO).

The effect of quality-aware supervision is examined through a controlled
comparison of four student configurations. The pretrained student is first
evaluated without task-specific training to establish an untrained baseline.
Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) provides the conventional
response-based baseline, treating all teacher demonstrations equally.
Quality-aware response-based distillation then extends SFT by assigning
different weights to teacher demonstrations according to their assessed
quality. Finally, DPO is applied after quality-aware distillation as a
preference-alignment stage, using relative preferences between alternative
clinical reports. All four conditions use the same student architecture,
initialisation, task, and evaluation protocol, allowing the effect of the
supervision strategy to be examined under controlled conditions.

The resulting student models are evaluated not only for report quality but
also for the extent and practicality of the transferred capabilities.
Student-generated reports are assessed using the same multidimensional
clinical-quality framework, covering factuality, coverage, medical tone,
conciseness, and readability. These assessments are complemented by lexical
and semantic similarity measures comparing student- and teacher-generated
reports to examine knowledge transfer. Finally, computational efficiency is
evaluated in terms of model size, memory consumption, throughput, and
latency. Together, these evaluations provide a comprehensive assessment of
whether quality-aware supervision improves clinical report generation,
transfers relevant teacher knowledge, and retains the practical efficiency
advantages of a compact student model.

\section{Synthetic Data Construction}
\label{sec:data_construction}

A controlled synthetic data-generation pipeline was developed to generate
diverse doctor--parent consultations and personalised clinical reports for
student-model training while maintaining consistency with established KBG
syndrome knowledge. The pipeline comprised three stages:
\textit{(i)} synthetic patient-profile construction,
\textit{(ii)} doctor--parent consultation generation, and
\textit{(iii)} consultation diversity assessment.


\subsection{Synthetic Patient Profile Generation}
\label{sec:patient_profile_generation}

Synthetic patient profiles were constructed to introduce controlled variation
in patient characteristics and parent communication styles before
doctor--parent consultation generation. The use of varied communication
personas was motivated in part by prior work showing that persona prompting
can increase lexical diversity in LLM-generated text
\cite{lahoti_improving_2023}. The profiles therefore provided structured
patient-specific inputs and communication cues to support variation across
synthetic consultations.

Each profile comprised demographic attributes, KBG syndrome-related clinical
features, and a parent communication persona. Clinical features were
organised into predefined syndrome-relevant domains, including vision,
hearing, dental, cardiac, neurological, gastrointestinal, and other medical
characteristics. The feature pools were derived from the supplied KBG
syndrome clinical reference material, and the complete pools and personas
are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:profile_pools}.

For each synthetic patient, features were sampled from the predefined
clinical domains and combined with a communication persona to form a
structured profile with a unique identifier. Each profile was then provided
to the language model as the basis for generating a corresponding
doctor--parent consultation.

\subsection{Doctor--Parent Consultations Generation}
\label{sec:consultation_generation}
The structured synthetic patient profiles were used to generate synthetic
doctor--parent consultations with \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct},
following established approaches to LLM-based synthetic patient--physician
conversation generation \cite{haider_synthetic_2025}. For each case, a
few-shot prompt incorporated the corresponding synthetic patient profile,
parent communication persona, a fixed consultation structure, and explicit
generation constraints. A representative consultation was provided as an
in-context example to guide the model towards the required format. The
complete prompt is provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:consultation_prompt}.

To maintain consistency across cases, the doctor followed the same sequence
of ten predefined questions covering the principal clinical domains.
Variation was introduced through the synthetic patient profiles and their
associated communication personas, allowing consultations to differ in
clinical content, vocabulary, response style, and level of detail while
retaining a common structure.

Each consultation was generated independently and stored in JSON Lines
format with its patient identifier and source profile. The generated
consultations were then used for the subsequent diversity assessment.

\subsection{Assessment of Consultation Diversity}
\label{sec:consultation_diversity}

Controlled variation in the synthetic patient profiles does not necessarily
translate into diverse generated language. Consultation diversity was therefore
assessed to determine whether profile-level variation was reflected in the
generated consultations rather than producing repetitive linguistic patterns.

Diversity was evaluated across four complementary dimensions:
\textit{lexical, response-length, grammatical, and semantic variation}.
Lexical diversity was measured using the Measure of Textual Lexical
Diversity (MTLD), which is less dependent on text length
\cite{mccarthy_mtld_2010}. Variation in parent-response length was measured
using the standard deviation of Mean Length of Utterance (MLU)
\cite{potratz_measurement_2022}. Grammatical variation was assessed using
POS Shannon entropy to capture diversity in part-of-speech usage
\cite{arora_estimating_2022}. Semantic variation was evaluated using pairwise
cosine similarity between consultation embeddings, with lower similarity
indicating greater semantic variation \cite{noauthor_evaluating_2026}.

Together, these measures assess whether profile-based generation produced
diversity across lexical, response-length, grammatical, and semantic levels.

\section{Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:teacher_student_models}

This section describes the teacher--student framework for transferring
task-specific clinical report-generation capabilities from a large language
model to a compact student model. It comprises three stages:
\textit{(i)} task-specific training-data generation using a large teacher
model, \textit{(ii)} standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and
\textit{(iii)} quality-aware response-based distillation. The same student architecture and training data are
used across conditions for controlled comparison.
\subsection{Teacher and Student Models}
\label{sec:teacher_student_models}
The proposed framework uses a teacher--student architecture in which a
large language model generates task-specific clinical reports that serve
as supervision for a substantially smaller student model. The teacher
provides the target behaviour, while the student learns to reproduce the
same report-generation task with considerably lower model capacity. rephrase it and tighten it 

\paragraph{Teacher Model:}
The teacher model was \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct}, used to generate
task-specific reports as supervision for the compact student model. Its role
was to transform each synthetic doctor--parent consultation and the supplied
KBG syndrome knowledge into a structured personalised report.

For each consultation, the teacher received two explicitly separated sources
of information: the doctor--parent consultation and a fixed KBG syndrome
clinical reference. The consultation was the sole source of patient-specific
information, while the reference supplied general syndrome-level knowledge,
including relevant screening and treatment recommendations. This separation
distinguished patient evidence from general clinical knowledge and helped
limit unsupported patient-specific claims.

A structured prompt guided the teacher to extract patient-specific findings,
organise them into predefined clinical categories, and generate the
corresponding personalised report. Prior work shows that prompt design and
explicit output structures can influence the consistency and quality of
LLM-generated clinical text
\cite{huang_critical_2024,tang_ignitioninnovators_2024, yao_clinicians_2024}.
This motivated the use of explicit structural and source-specific
instructions in the present study.

Each report followed a fixed 13-part structure covering the principal
clinical domains, screening, and treatment recommendations. Within each
section, \textit{patient-specific findings} were distinguished from
\textit{syndrome-level information}. When information for a category was
absent from the consultation, the teacher was instructed to output ``N/A''
rather than infer unsupported findings, while recommendations were
constrained by the available patient information and the provided KBG
reference. This provided a consistent target representation across cases.

Generated reports were stored with their corresponding consultations and
metadata for subsequent quality assessment and student training. The
complete generation prompt, clinical reference, and inference configuration
are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:report_prompt}.

\paragraph{Student Model:}
The compact student model was \textit{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct}, trained on
teacher-generated clinical reports to transfer task-specific
report-generation capability at substantially lower model scale. The student
was trained to map doctor--parent consultations to structured personalised
reports, learning to preserve the teacher's ability to integrate
patient-specific findings with relevant syndrome-level knowledge, follow the
defined reporting structure, and produce clinically appropriate
recommendations.

The same \textit{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct} backbone and initialisation were used
across all student experiments, ensuring that differences in performance
were attributable to the training strategy rather than model architecture
or initialisation. The specific training procedures for standard SFT and
quality-weighted SFT are described in the following sections.
\subsection{Standard Supervised Fine-Tuning}

Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) was used as the primary baseline for
response-based knowledge transfer from the teacher to the compact student.
The teacher was treated as a black-box model, with only its generated
responses used for supervision and no access to its parameters, logits, or
internal representations. This provides a conventional response-based
distillation setting against which the effect of quality-aware supervision
can be evaluated \cite{song_exploring_2023}.

The student was trained on teacher-generated examples containing the
doctor--parent consultation, the fixed KBG syndrome clinical reference, and
the corresponding teacher-generated report. The consultation provided
patient-specific information, while the clinical reference supplied
syndrome-level knowledge. The teacher-generated report served as the target
response. The training set was therefore defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}
=
\left\{
(x_i,c,y_i)
\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ denotes the doctor--parent consultation for patient $i$, $c$
denotes the fixed KBG syndrome clinical reference, $y_i$ denotes the
corresponding teacher-generated report, and $N$ is the number of training
examples.

Given $(x_i,c)$, SFT minimises the autoregressive negative log-likelihood of
the teacher-generated report:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
p_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,c,y_{i,<t}
\right),
\label{eq:sft_loss}
\end{equation}

where $y_{i,t}$ is the target token at position $t$, $y_{i,<t}$ denotes the
preceding target tokens, and $p_{\theta}$ is the probability assigned by the
student to the target token. Minimising this objective trains the student to
reproduce the teacher's personalised report from the same patient-specific
and syndrome-level information.

The teacher responses therefore provide direct supervision for both the
content and organisation of the task, including the presentation of
patient-specific findings, incorporation of relevant syndrome-level
knowledge, and generation of screening and treatment recommendations.
Standard SFT treats all retained examples equally, regardless of the quality
of their corresponding teacher reports. This establishes the baseline for
testing whether incorporating teacher-response quality improves knowledge
transfer.

The student was adapted using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which freezes the
pretrained parameters and learns low-rank updates to selected weight
matrices. For a pretrained weight matrix
$W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, the adapted matrix is

\begin{equation}
W = W_0 + BA,
\label{eq:lora_update}
\end{equation}

where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and
$A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k}$ are trainable low-rank matrices with
$r \ll \min(d,k)$. Only these low-rank parameters are updated, while
$W_0$ remains frozen, reducing the number of trainable parameters relative
to full fine-tuning \cite{hu_lora_2021}.

The same student initialisation, training data, input formatting, and LoRA
adaptation framework were maintained for the subsequent quality-weighted SFT
experiments. Thus, differences between standard and quality-weighted SFT
reflect the incorporation of teacher-response quality into the training
objective rather than changes in model architecture or adaptation method.
\subsection{Quality-Weighted Supervision}
\label{sec:quality_weighted_sft}

Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) assigns equal importance to all
retained teacher-generated demonstrations, regardless of differences in
their quality. In synthetic clinical data, this may allow weaker responses
to contribute as strongly as more reliable supervision. The proposed
quality-aware response-based distillation therefore assigns each retained
demonstration a continuous weight based on its assessed quality, allowing
higher-quality teacher responses to have greater influence during training.

For each retained teacher-generated report $y_i$, a scalar quality score
$q_i$ was constructed from three rubric dimensions:
\textit{factuality}, \textit{coverage}, and \textit{medical tone}. These
dimensions capture complementary properties of the information being
transferred. Factuality measures whether reported information is supported
by the available evidence, coverage measures whether relevant clinical
information is included, and medical tone measures whether it is expressed
in the intended clinical reporting style.

Conciseness and readability were not included as weighting dimensions.
Conciseness was defined in this study as a composite measure of factuality,
coverage, and brevity. Including it directly in the quality score would
therefore overlap with dimensions already represented by factuality and
coverage and give them disproportionate influence.
Readability was assessed separately using Flesch Reading Ease (FRE),
Flesch--Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), SMOG, and Dale--Chall. These measures
primarily characterise reading difficulty and accessibility rather than
clinical reliability or information completeness [citation needed].
Because the generated reports showed a relatively similar readability
range, readability was retained as an evaluation criterion rather than used
to determine training importance.

The quality score was defined as the geometric mean of the three selected
dimensions, followed by within-batch mean normalisation:

\begin{equation}
q_i=(F_iC_iM_i)^{1/3},
\qquad
\tilde q_i=
\frac{q_i}
{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}}q_j},
\quad i\in\mathcal{B},
\label{eq:quality_score}
\end{equation}

where $F_i$, $C_i$, and $M_i$ denote the factuality, coverage, and
medical-tone scores for report $i$, respectively, and $\mathcal{B}$ is the
current training batch. The geometric mean was chosen because the three
dimensions represent complementary requirements for useful clinical
supervision and should contribute jointly. Unlike an arithmetic mean, it
limits compensation between dimensions, so poor performance on one
dimension reduces the overall score. The resulting value therefore reflects
\textit{consistent quality} rather than average performance alone. Mean
normalisation preserves the relative differences between examples while
maintaining an average weight of one within each batch.

Quality filtering was applied before weighting to limit the risk of
transferring unreliable teacher supervision. Reports with a factuality score
below $0.90$ were excluded, while those meeting the threshold were retained
for quality-weighted training. The $0.90$ threshold was chosen as a
conservative criterion to retain high factual reliability while avoiding
unnecessary exclusion due to limitations in automated assessment. Manual
review of lower-scoring cases showed that some could still be clinically
acceptable, indicating that the automated score should not be treated as
definitive ground truth. The threshold therefore served as a quality-control
safeguard, after which the retained reports were further differentiated
using their continuous quality scores.

Let $\ell_{i,t}$ denote the causal language-model cross-entropy loss for
token $t$ of report $i$, and let $m_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether that
token contributes to the supervised objective. The quality-weighted loss
was defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QW}}
=
\frac{
\displaystyle
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_t
m_{i,t}\tilde q_i\ell_{i,t}}
{
\displaystyle
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_t m_{i,t}
}.
\label{eq:quality_weighted_loss}
\end{equation}

Here, the same example-level weight $\tilde q_i$ is applied to all
supervised tokens in report $i$, while masked tokens are excluded. This
matches the report-level quality assessment and allows higher-quality
reports to exert greater influence while retaining lower-quality examples
that meet the factuality criterion.

All other aspects of training were held consistent with standard SFT,
including the student architecture, initialisation, input formatting, LoRA
adaptation, training data, and optimisation procedure. The comparison
therefore isolates the effect of incorporating multidimensional
teacher-response quality into the supervision objective.
\section{Direct Preference Optimization}
\label{sec:dpo}

Following quality-weighted supervised fine-tuning, Direct Preference
Optimization (DPO) was applied as a final preference-optimization stage.
SFT establishes the basic task behaviour, whereas DPO provides a more direct
way of refining the student by learning which of two responses should be
preferred. This staged approach is motivated by Ye et al.~\cite{ye_teaching_2026},
who first use SFT to establish the required task behaviour and subsequently
apply DPO to correct undesirable student outputs. DPO was therefore used
here to further align the quality-weighted student with the desired
quality signal without requiring a separate reward model or
reinforcement-learning stage \cite{rafailov_direct_2024}.

DPO was initialized from the quality-weighted SFT model. The trained
quality-weighted LoRA adapter remained trainable during DPO, while a
separate copy of the quality-weighted SFT model was kept frozen as the
reference policy. Thus, DPO refined the same student model after
quality-aware supervised learning.

For each consultation $x_i$, the teacher-generated reports were evaluated
using the multidimensional quality criteria described in
Section~\ref{sec:quality_weighted_sft}. The highest-scoring teacher report
was selected as the preferred response $y_i^{w}$, while the corresponding
report generated by the quality-weighted student was used as the rejected
response $y_i^{l}$. Thus, the same underlying multidimensional quality
assessment used to weight examples during quality-weighted SFT was used
here to construct preference pairs for DPO. This changed the role of the
quality signal from determining the contribution of each training example
to directly encouraging the student to prefer the higher-quality teacher
response.

For a preference triple $(x,y^{w},y^{l})$, the DPO objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
=
-\mathbb{E}
\left[
\log \sigma
\left(
\beta
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{w}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{w}\mid x)}
-
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{l}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{l}\mid x)}
\right]
\right)
\right],
\end{equation}

where $\pi_{\theta}$ denotes the trainable student policy,
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ the frozen reference policy, $\sigma$ the sigmoid
function, and $\beta$ controls the strength of the preference signal
\cite{rafailov_direct_2024}. The objective increases the relative
likelihood of the preferred response with respect to the rejected response
while regularising the updated policy towards the reference policy.

The overall training strategy therefore progressed from learning the
clinical reporting task through SFT, to incorporating multidimensional
teacher quality through quality-weighted SFT, and finally to directly
refining response preferences through DPO. The held-out test set was
excluded from DPO training and retained for final evaluation.

\section{Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:evaluation_framework}
Generated clinical reports were evaluated using a rubric-guided
\textit{LLM-as-a-Judge} framework, with
\textit{Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507} serving as the evaluator. Recent work
has shown that LLM-based evaluation can assess generated text against
explicit criteria and capture semantic and qualitative aspects of quality
that are difficult to measure using surface-level metrics alone
\cite{liu_g-eval_2023,moslemi_survey_2024}. Building on this approach, a
task-specific rubric was used to evaluate the reports across distinct
clinical dimensions rather than relying on a single overall judgement.
This was particularly relevant to the clinical report generation task,
where factual grounding, information coverage, conciseness, and appropriate
medical expression represent distinct aspects of quality.

Evaluation was conducted at two stages with distinct purposes. First,
teacher-generated reports were evaluated to characterise the quality of the
synthetic supervision. These assessments provided the underlying quality
signal used in quality-weighted supervised fine-tuning and in the
construction of DPO preference pairs. Second, the trained student variants
were evaluated on the held-out test set using the same clinical-quality
framework, allowing the effect of the proposed training approaches to be
assessed consistently.

Clinical-quality assessment was complemented by lexical and semantic
similarity measures to examine the extent to which student-generated
reports reproduced teacher-derived content. Model size and inference
measurements were additionally used to assess the computational efficiency
of the resulting compact models. Together, these evaluations assess both
the clinical quality of the generated reports and the practical efficiency
of the distilled models. The detailed quality criteria, scoring procedures,
similarity measures, efficiency measures, and evaluation prompts are
described in the following subsections.
\subsection{Factuality}
\label{sec:factuality}

Factuality was assessed using fine-grained, claim-level verification to
determine whether information in each report was supported by the available
evidence. Claim-level evaluation enables unsupported statements to be
identified individually rather than obscured by a report-level judgement
\cite{min_factscore_2023,scire_fenice_2024}. Building on approaches that
decompose generated text into atomic facts for fine-grained verification
\cite{kim_atomic_2026}, each report was decomposed into independently
verifiable clinical claims. This is particularly important in clinical text,
where unsupported claims may have meaningful consequences
\cite{bn_fact-controlled_2025,munnangi_factehr_2025}.

Each claim was assigned an evidence source according to its type.
Patient-specific claims were verified against the corresponding
doctor--parent consultation, while syndrome-level claims were verified
against the KBG syndrome clinical reference. Questions, non-informative
text, duplicated information, and unsupported inferences were excluded.
This distinction ensured that general KBG knowledge was not treated as
evidence that a specific patient exhibited a particular characteristic.

For each claim $c_i$, the LLM-based verifier assigned a binary support label:

\begin{equation}
v_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } c_i \text{ is supported by its designated evidence source},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

A claim was considered supported when its designated source conveyed the
same clinical meaning, allowing differences in wording or clinically
equivalent terminology. Unsupported, contradictory, or unverifiable claims
were assigned $v_i=0$. The resulting measure therefore captures
\textit{source-grounded factuality} rather than general medical plausibility.

The report-level factuality score was calculated as:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Factuality}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_i,
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of extracted claims in report $R$. The score
ranges from $0$ to $1$, with higher values indicating a greater proportion
of claims supported by the designated evidence.

The complete claim-decomposition and verification prompts are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_claim_prompt}.
\subsection{Coverage}
\label{sec:coverage}

Coverage was evaluated to determine whether the generated reports retained
the clinically relevant information required for the target task. Whereas
factuality assesses whether information stated in a report is supported by
evidence, coverage assesses whether relevant information available in the
source material is included. A report may therefore be factually accurate
while remaining clinically incomplete \cite{noauthor_faithfulness_2026,
song_finesure_2024}.

The target reports contain three distinct information requirements:
patient-specific findings, syndrome-level background information, and
clinically relevant recommendations. Coverage was therefore evaluated
separately for \textit{patient-specific coverage},
\textit{syndrome-background coverage}, and
\textit{recommendation coverage}. Retaining these components separately
allows omissions within different information types to be distinguished.

\paragraph{Patient-Specific Coverage.}

Patient-specific coverage was assessed using a fine-grained, claim-based
approach, following prior work on measuring the comprehensiveness of
generated text through decomposition of source information into individual
facts \cite{dejl_comprehensiveness_2026}. The doctor--parent consultation
was first analysed to identify clinically meaningful information explicitly
reported by the parent about the child. Complex statements were decomposed
into distinct clinical claims, while non-informative and unsupported
information was excluded. These claims formed the reference set of
patient-specific information to be retained in the generated report.

Each reference claim was then checked against the report for semantic
equivalence, allowing differences in wording and sentence structure. The
coverage score was calculated as:

\begin{equation}
v_i^{(k)} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if evaluation unit } i \text{ is addressed in the report},\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
\mathrm{Coverage}_{k}
=
\frac{1}{N_k}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_k}v_i^{(k)}.
\label{eq:component_coverage}
\end{equation}

For patient-specific coverage, the evaluation units were the extracted
clinical claims. Claims that were absent, ambiguous, or unsupported were
assigned zero, making the measure a semantic assessment of information
retention rather than lexical overlap.

\paragraph{Syndrome Background and Recommendation Coverage.}

Syndrome-background and recommendation coverage were assessed using
feature-specific binary question--answer evaluation, following question-based
approaches for determining whether generated text contains information
required to answer targeted questions \cite{scialom_questeval_2021,
moramarco_towards_2021}. For each clinical feature explicitly reported in
the consultation, two questions were generated: one targeting relevant KBG
syndrome background information and another targeting feature-specific
clinical recommendations, including screening, monitoring, treatment,
referral, or therapy where applicable.

Question generation was restricted to features present in the consultation
to focus evaluation on clinically relevant information. Each question was
answered with either \textit{Yes} or \textit{No}, with \textit{Yes}
assigned $1$ and \textit{No} assigned $0$. Equation~\ref{eq:component_coverage}
was then applied to both question sets, with $k\in\{B,Rec\}$ denoting
syndrome background and recommendations.

This produced three component scores,
$\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}$, $\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}$, and
$\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}$, which were combined using an unweighted
arithmetic mean:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Overall}
=
\frac{
\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}
}{3}.
\label{eq:overall_coverage}
\end{equation}

An arithmetic mean was used because the three components represent distinct
but equally important information requirements of the target report. It
provides an interpretable overall coverage measure while preventing any
single component from dominating the score. The complete claim-extraction and question-generation
procedures are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:patient_coverage_prompt} and
Appendix~\ref{appx:syndrome_coverage_prompt}.


\subsection{Conciseness}
\label{sec:conciseness}

Conciseness was evaluated to determine whether reports communicated the
required clinical information without unnecessary verbosity. Conciseness
was treated as distinct from completeness, since a shorter report may
achieve brevity by omitting clinically relevant information. This
distinction is consistent with prior summarisation research that evaluates
completeness and conciseness as separate dimensions
\cite{song_finesure_2024}. Accordingly, conciseness was formulated as a
composite of \textit{information retention, factual reliability, and
brevity}, ensuring that shorter reports were not rewarded when brevity was
achieved at the expense of clinical content or correctness.

For each report $i$, token length $L_i$ was calculated using the tokenizer
of the evaluated model. Report lengths were min--max normalised across the
evaluation set and converted into a brevity score:

\begin{equation}
L_i^{\mathrm{norm}}
=
\frac{L_i-L_{\min}}{L_{\max}-L_{\min}},
\qquad
B_i = 1-L_i^{\mathrm{norm}},
\end{equation}

where $L_{\min}$ and $L_{\max}$ denote the minimum and maximum report
lengths. This provides a relative measure of brevity within the evaluation
set, with higher $B_i$ indicating a shorter report, without imposing an
arbitrary target length.

Brevity was combined with coverage and factuality to reflect the clinical
constraints of concise reporting. Coverage ensures that relevant
information is retained, while factuality ensures that the retained
information remains supported by the available evidence. The resulting
score was defined using their geometric mean:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Conciseness}_i
=
\left(
\mathrm{Coverage}_i
\cdot
\mathrm{Factuality}_i
\cdot
B_i
\right)^{1/3}.
\end{equation}

The geometric mean was selected over an arithmetic mean because the three
components represent complementary requirements rather than fully
compensatory objectives. It therefore penalises imbalance: high brevity
cannot produce a high conciseness score when coverage or factuality is
poor, while a comprehensive and factual report is not considered highly
concise if it is unnecessarily long. The resulting score ranges from $0$
to $1$, with higher values indicating a more favourable balance between
retaining clinically relevant information, maintaining factual reliability,
and avoiding unnecessary length.
\subsection{Medical Tone}
\label{sec:medical_tone}

Medical tone was evaluated to determine whether generated reports
communicated patient-specific information in a clinically appropriate,
professional, respectful, and patient-centred manner. These dimensions were
selected because effective clinical reporting requires not only appropriate
medical language but also communication that is respectful of the patient
and focused on their individual circumstances
\cite{bowden_evaluating_nodate,rattanapitoon_beyond_2025,omar_impact_2025}.

A two-stage LLM-based procedure was used to isolate and assess the language
relevant to these criteria. First, patient-specific statements describing
the patient's condition and associated screening or treatment
recommendations were extracted from each report. Syndrome-level background
and general medical information were excluded because they do not directly
reflect how the individual patient is described or advised. Few-shot
prompting was used for extraction, with the complete procedure provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:medical_tone_prompt}.

The extracted statements were then evaluated using a separate few-shot LLM
prompt based on three binary question--answer criteria:
\textit{professionalism, respectfulness, and patient-centred
communication}. For each statement, the evaluator answered \textit{Yes} or
\textit{No} to the following questions: whether it used professional and
appropriate clinical language; whether it described the patient in a
respectful and non-stigmatising manner; and whether it communicated the
patient's condition in a supportive and patient-focused manner. A
\textit{Yes} response was assigned a score of $1$ and a \textit{No}
response a score of $0$.

For statement $i$ and criterion $j$, the binary score was therefore defined
as:

\begin{equation}
s_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if statement } i \text{ satisfies criterion } j,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

For each report, the proportion of statements satisfying each criterion was
then calculated:

\begin{equation}
S_j =
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{ij},
\qquad
j\in\{P,R,C\},
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the number of extracted patient-specific statements, and
$P$, $R$, and $C$ denote professionalism, respectfulness, and
patient-centred communication, respectively. The three scores were retained
separately because the criteria represent distinct communication properties
and there was no principled basis for assigning different weights to them.
This preserves interpretability while allowing specific weaknesses in
clinical communication to be identified.
\subsection{Readability}
\label{sec:readability}

Readability was evaluated to determine how easily the generated clinical
reports could be understood from a linguistic perspective. This is distinct
from medical tone: a report may use appropriate professional language while
still being difficult to read because of complex sentence structures,
vocabulary, or reading-level requirements. Readability was therefore
assessed using three established measures that capture complementary
aspects of textual difficulty.

The evaluation included Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), Simple Measure of
Gobbledygook (SMOG), and Dale--Chall. FRE provides a general measure of
reading ease based primarily on sentence length and word complexity, with
higher scores indicating easier text \cite{flesch_new_1948}. SMOG estimates
the reading difficulty of a text from sentence length and the frequency of
polysyllabic words, providing an indication of the level of education
required for comprehension \cite{mclaughlin_smog_1969}. Dale--Chall
incorporates sentence length and the familiarity of words, with less
familiar vocabulary contributing to greater estimated difficulty
\cite{crossley_large-scaled_2023}. Together, these measures capture
different aspects of linguistic accessibility rather than relying on a
single readability criterion.

Each measure was computed independently for every generated report using
the cleaned report text and the \texttt{textstat} implementation. The
measures were retained separately because they use different scales and
capture different aspects of readability; combining them into a single
score could obscure these distinctions.



\subsection{Teacher--Student Correspondence and Efficiency}
\label{sec:complementary_evaluation}

The clinical-quality measures described above assess the quality of the
generated reports from multiple linguistic and clinical perspectives.
To complement this multidimensional evaluation, additional measures were
used to assess teacher--student correspondence and computational efficiency.
These measures capture different aspects of model behaviour that are not
directly reflected by clinical-quality scores, enabling a more complete
assessment of the proposed distillation approach.

\paragraph{Teacher--Student Correspondence.}

Teacher--student correspondence was assessed by comparing each
student-generated report with its corresponding teacher-generated report.
The objective was to determine whether the student retained teacher-derived
information and meaning without requiring verbatim reproduction. Lexical
correspondence was measured using ROUGE and BLEU, which capture different
forms of word and sequence overlap. Semantic correspondence was assessed
using BERTScore and embedding similarity, providing complementary measures
that are less dependent on exact wording. These measures were retained
separately because lexical overlap and semantic similarity capture
different aspects of teacher--student correspondence \cite{lin_rouge_2004, ward_bleu_2001, zhang_bertscore_2020}.

\paragraph{Computational Efficiency.}

Computational efficiency was evaluated to assess the practical benefits of
transferring the task to a compact student model. Four complementary
measures were considered: \textit{parameter count, inference latency, peak
memory usage, and token generation rate}. Parameter count reflects model
scale, inference latency measures the time required to generate a report,
peak memory captures the maximum memory required during inference, and
token generation rate measures generation throughput \cite{zhen_taming_2025}.

Latency, peak memory, and token generation rate were measured under
identical inference conditions and hardware for all student variants,
ensuring that differences reflected the models rather than the evaluation
environment. Parameter count was obtained from the respective model
architectures. Reporting these measures alongside the multidimensional
clinical-quality evaluation enables the quality--efficiency trade-off of
the distilled models to be assessed .

\section{Teacher--Student Correspondence and Efficiency}

higher, with BERTScore F1 and embedding similarity of 0.914 and 0.967.

have been transferred, but it cannot replace direct evaluation of clinical
quality.

Overall, Weighted SFT achieved high semantic similarity to the teacher and
retained a substantial proportion of its clinical quality, while the
correlation analysis showed that similarity alone does not guarantee
better factuality. Together, these findings support using teacher--student
similarity as an indicator of knowledge transfer alongside direct
evaluation of factuality, coverage, conciseness, readability, and medical
tone.

averaged 1,186 tokens compared with 738 tokens for the teacher. Therefore,
the higher time per report does not imply slower token generation.

Overall, the distilled student achieved substantial reductions in model
size and GPU memory while providing higher token-level throughput. Although
end-to-end latency was higher under the present generation settings, this
was influenced by the longer output length. The results therefore
demonstrate a substantially smaller computational footprint while
maintaining high semantic correspondence with the teacher.

\paragraph{Baseline vs. Weighted SFT}
Qualitative comparison with the baseline demonstrates that Weighted SFT improved patient-specific grounding, section-level alignment, structural coherence, and restraint in generation. The baseline frequently propagated findings across unrelated sections and introduced unsupported patient-level information. For example, although the consultation reported only \textit{bowel problems sometimes} the baseline expanded this to \textit{hypermetropia and scoliosis-related issues} under Gastroenterology and attributed scoliosis to the patient across Respiratory, Ear Nose Throat, and Ophthalmology. In contrast, Weighted SFT mapped the reported bowel problems to Gastroenterology and restricted the explicit scoliosis finding to the Skeletal section. Similar improvements were observed in preserving consultation evidence: findings including \textit{hypermetropia,fluid in his ear,} easily chipped teeth, physical fatigue, and a structural heart abnormality were retained more faithfully, while unsupported inferences were reduced. The handling of absent findings also improved; despite the consultation specifying N/A for immunological problems, the baseline generated \textit{a weakened immune system,} whereas Weighted SFT correctly preserved N/A. This improved evidence-to-section mapping also produced a more coherent report skeleton, with patient-specific information more consistently placed within the appropriate clinical domains rather than being repeated across sections. Improvements extended to the recommendations structure: while the baseline generated broad interventions such as \textit{bracing or surgery for scoliosis} and multiple therapies not directly supported by the consultation, Weighted SFT produced a more focused set of screening and referral recommendations linked to the reported clinical findings. Nevertheless, the improvement was not complete, as the student still introduced unsupported details, such as stating that the patient's bowel problems \textit{require occasional supplementation with nasogastric tube feeding.} Overall, the comparison indicates that Weighted SFT did not merely reproduce the report template more effectively; it improved the mapping of source evidence to clinical sections and reduced unsupported patient-level elaboration, while retaining some residual limitations that are examined in the following error analysis.
\paragraph{Weighted SFT Errors}
Despite the overall improvements observed quantitatively, qualitative comparison of the consultation, teacher-generated report, and Weighted SFT output revealed several residual failure modes. First, the student occasionally failed to preserve clinically relevant patient-specific information despite its presence in the source consultation and teacher supervision. For example, the consultation reported that the child was \textit{bothered by loud noises and gets frustrated when there's too much going on} which the teacher correctly retained under Ear, Nose and Throat. However, the student omitted this finding and instead populated the section with vision-related information. Similarly, the consultation and teacher explicitly identified an undescended testicle, whereas the student reduced this established patient finding to a generic recommendation to \textit{consider a testicular examination.} These examples indicate that the student can retain the overall report structure while incompletely preserving the fine-grained mapping between patient evidence and its corresponding clinical domain.

A second limitation was the generation of unsupported patient-specific elaborations. The consultation only stated that the child appeared \textit{a bit behind} developmentally, and the teacher preserved this uncertainty. In contrast, the student expanded this into specific difficulties involving \textit{rolling, sitting, walking, playing with toys, using cutlery, using zips and buttons, and toilet training} none of which were reported in the consultation. More importantly, the student occasionally blurred the boundary between syndrome-level knowledge and patient-specific evidence. For instance, characteristics described by the teacher as affecting \textit{other individuals with KBG syndrome} were incorrectly attributed directly to the patient, such as \textit{The patient has an unusual structure of their spinal bones, giving rise to\dots scoliosis} This represents a clinically important attribution error: although the generated information is medically plausible within the context of KBG syndrome, there is no evidence that the individual patient exhibited the condition.

Finally, the student demonstrated occasional degradation of semantic alignment across report sections. Most notably, the same syndrome-level statement regarding hearing difficulties---\textit{Around 20--40\% of people with KBG syndrome experience hearing issues}---was repeated across unrelated sections, including Gastroenterology, Neurology, Dental, Education, and Behaviour and Development. This suggests that the model learned the structural template of the teacher reports but did not consistently preserve the semantic relationship between section headings and their associated syndrome-specific information. Overall, these examples indicate that Weighted SFT substantially improves the ability of the compact student model to generate structured and clinically relevant reports, but residual errors remain in evidence preservation, unsupported elaboration, and the separation of patient-specific observations from population-level syndrome knowledge. This distinction is particularly important in clinical report generation, where medically accurate information may nevertheless become misleading when incorrectly attributed to an individual patient.

\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:Discussion}
\section{Interpretation of the Main Findings}
\label{sec:interpretation_main_findings}

The findings reveal a clear progression in how different adaptation strategies influenced the compact student model. Standard supervised fine-tuning substantially improved factuality relative to the pretrained baseline, but did not produce significant improvements in coverage or conciseness. This suggests that task-specific supervision primarily helped the model learn a more reliable mapping between consultation evidence and the required clinical-reporting format, thereby reducing unsupported generation. Although this improvement is consistent with training on highly reliable teacher reports, the experiment cannot isolate the contribution of factuality filtering from the broader effect of supervised adaptation.

The absence of comparable gains in coverage and conciseness highlights a limitation of conventional SFT. Teacher reports varied in how completely and efficiently they communicated information, yet standard SFT assigned equal importance to all examples. The model could therefore learn the overall reporting structure and improve factual grounding without consistently learning which information should be prioritised or how selectively it should be expressed.

Quality-weighted SFT addressed this limitation by making the influence of each training example dependent on its assessed quality. Its strongest gains consequently occurred in coverage and conciseness, while the additional improvement in factuality was small and non-significant. This suggests that once basic factual grounding had been established through SFT, quality weighting was particularly useful for refining how information was selected and communicated. In contrast, DPO provided no significant improvement over quality-weighted SFT. The weighted objective may already have captured much of the useful quality signal contained in the teacher demonstrations, while preference optimisation does not inherently enforce consultation-grounded correctness. Within this setting, improving the supervised learning signal therefore appeared more beneficial than adding a subsequent preference-optimisation stage.

These findings show that successful adaptation depends not only on the presence of teacher supervision, but also on how its quality is incorporated into training. The next question is whether this improved performance represents meaningful knowledge transfer. Weighted SFT achieved high semantic similarity to the teacher, with a BERTScore of \(0.914\) and embedding similarity of \(0.967\), alongside more moderate lexical overlap. This suggests that the student retained underlying information and reporting behaviour without simply reproducing the teacher's wording.

However, similarity alone did not establish clinical utility. The student retained \(72.6\%\) of teacher factuality, \(74.8\%\) of coverage, and \(78.1\%\) of conciseness, demonstrating substantial but incomplete transfer. Moreover, none of the similarity measures showed a significant association with factuality after correction, and embedding similarity was almost unrelated to factuality (\(\rho=-0.011\)). A student output can therefore resemble the teacher semantically while still omitting relevant patient information or making unsupported attributions. Successful knowledge transfer must consequently be understood as retaining clinically useful information while preserving the distinction between patient-specific evidence and general syndrome-level knowledge.

This partial retention of clinical capability was achieved with substantial computational savings. Replacing the \(70\)B-parameter teacher with the \(1.5\)B-parameter student reduced model size by \(97.9\%\) and peak GPU memory consumption by \(95.7\%\), from \(39.37\) GB to \(1.71\) GB. The student also achieved \(36.8\%\) higher token-generation throughput. End-to-end latency was nevertheless higher because the student generated substantially longer reports, illustrating that throughput and total response time capture different aspects of efficiency.

Taken together, the findings demonstrate the central trade-off of the study. Quality-weighted SFT provided the strongest overall balance of factuality, coverage, and conciseness, while DPO offered no measurable additional benefit in the present setting. The resulting student did not fully reproduce teacher-level clinical quality, but retained a substantial proportion of clinically relevant reporting behaviour while dramatically reducing computational requirements. The practical value of distillation therefore lies not simply in producing a smaller model, but in achieving an appropriate balance between \textit{knowledge retention, source-grounded clinical quality, and computational efficiency}.
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The findings presented above should be interpreted within several methodological limitations that define the boundaries of the study. These concern the data, supervision signal, evaluation framework, adaptation experiments, and measures of knowledge transfer and efficiency.

The use of synthetic patient profiles and consultations limits clinical realism and generalisability. Although the consultations exhibited linguistic variation, reflected by an MTLD of \(143.90 \pm 36.17\), mean response length of \(26.70 \pm 8.36\) words, and normalised POS entropy of \(0.7993\), these measures capture textual diversity rather than the complexity of real clinical encounters. Synthetic consultations may not reproduce ambiguity, contradictions, incomplete histories, uncertainty, or natural conversational behaviour. Furthermore, the KBG-specific construction limits the extent to which the findings can be generalised to other clinical conditions.

The supervision signal also imposes constraints. Only \(400\) teacher-generated reports were used for SFT training, limiting the amount of clinical variation available for adaptation. Although the teacher generally preserved consultation information, qualitative analysis identified occasional leakage of syndrome-level knowledge into patient-specific descriptions, inconsistent preservation of uncertainty, and redundancy caused by the fixed report structure. For example, a report describing brittle teeth additionally introduced weak enamel, despite this not being explicitly reported by the parent. Distillation may therefore transfer imperfections in the teacher alongside useful reporting behaviour. In addition, the quality-weighting scheme, based on the geometric mean of factuality, coverage, and conciseness, represents only one operational definition of report quality and does not explicitly capture uncertainty, clinical relevance, or claim attribution.

The evaluation framework introduces further limitations. Generated reports were decomposed into atomic claims before factuality and coverage were assessed, making the evaluation partly dependent on how individual statements were segmented. The subsequent binary classification of claims as supported or unsupported also simplifies clinical evidence, which may instead be partial, uncertain, qualified, or expressed at different levels of specificity. The use of a single LLM judge adds further evaluator dependence. The error analysis identified false-negative judgements for clinically valid information expressed through paraphrase or uncertainty, indicating that the resulting scores should be interpreted as rubric- and judge-dependent estimates rather than definitive measures of clinical correctness. Similarly, the readability measures describe textual complexity but are influenced by specialised clinical terminology and therefore do not directly establish human-perceived readability.

The evaluation scale also limits the strength of the conclusions. The main paired analysis comprised \(100\) held-out cases, restricting the ability to characterise rare errors and small performance differences. The DPO experiment has an additional methodological limitation because teacher reports were used as preferred responses and Weighted SFT outputs as rejected responses. These pairs therefore represent teacher-versus-student preference rather than independently validated clinical preference. It is also possible that DPO exploited superficial distinctions between the preferred and rejected responses rather than learning the intended quality criteria. Such shortcut learning provides one plausible explanation for the lack of significant improvement, although the present experiment cannot establish whether this occurred.

The measures used for knowledge transfer and efficiency also provide only partial evidence of practical effectiveness. ROUGE, BLEU, BERTScore, and embedding similarity measure correspondence between teacher and student outputs rather than clinical correctness; notably, embedding similarity showed essentially no relationship with factuality (\(\rho=-0.011\)). Similarly, the efficiency measurements were obtained under a specific inference configuration and on a limited number of cases. The higher student latency was also influenced by its substantially longer outputs, meaning that throughput and end-to-end latency represent different aspects of efficiency.

Overall, the study provides evidence for quality-guided distillation within a controlled synthetic KBG syndrome setting, but the findings should not be interpreted as establishing clinical readiness or broad real-world generalisability. The conclusions are bounded by the synthetic data, limited supervision size, imperfections in teacher reports, operational quality definitions, atomic-claim and single-judge evaluation, DPO preference construction, and reference-based measures of knowledge transfer and efficiency.
\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This study investigated whether quality-aware knowledge distillation could improve the clinical quality of reports generated by a compact language model compared with conventional supervised fine-tuning. The findings suggest that it can, particularly when the quality of teacher supervision is incorporated directly into the learning process. Conventional supervised fine-tuning provided a strong foundation by improving the factual grounding of generated reports, but its benefits were less consistent across other aspects of report quality. Introducing quality-weighted supervision produced a more balanced improvement, particularly in the coverage and conciseness of the generated reports, while maintaining comparable factuality. This demonstrates that the quality of teacher demonstrations can influence not only what a compact model learns, but also how effectively it learns to communicate clinically relevant information.

The subsequent comparison with DPO showed that further preference optimisation did not provide a significant improvement beyond quality-weighted supervision. Together, these results suggest that carefully designing the supervision signal may be more important than continually adding optimisation stages. The study therefore contributes a quality-aware approach to knowledge distillation in which teacher-generated examples are not treated as equally informative, but are weighted according to their assessed quality.

The work also examined what was actually transferred from the large teacher to the compact student. The student showed strong semantic correspondence with the teacher and retained a substantial proportion of its clinical reporting performance, while the weak relationship between output similarity and factuality demonstrated that similarity alone is not sufficient evidence of successful clinical knowledge transfer. This highlights the importance of evaluating whether distilled models preserve clinically supported information rather than simply reproducing teacher-like language. The study consequently brings together quality assessment, claim-level clinical grounding, and knowledge-transfer evaluation within a single distillation framework.

Importantly, these capabilities were transferred to a model that was substantially smaller and more computationally efficient than the teacher. Although longer student outputs introduced a trade-off in end-to-end latency, the reduction in model scale and memory requirements, together with improved token-generation throughput, demonstrates the potential of distillation to make clinically grounded language generation more practical under constrained computational resources. The contribution of this work therefore extends beyond model compression: it demonstrates how useful capabilities from a large language model can be selectively transferred into a compact model while retaining important aspects of clinical report quality.

Future work should build on this foundation by expanding the training data and increasing its clinical diversity, allowing the model to learn from a broader range of patient presentations and reporting contexts. Validation on real clinical consultations and clinician-authored reports, alongside direct assessment by medical professionals, would provide stronger evidence of clinical usefulness. Extending the framework across additional rare diseases and clinical domains would further test its generalisability. The quality signal could also be developed to capture patient-specific attribution, uncertainty, clinical relevance, and evidence completeness more explicitly, while clinician-validated teacher reports and independently constructed preference data could provide stronger supervision for future optimisation. Finally, combining quality-aware distillation with more efficient architectures and decoding strategies could help address the remaining trade-off between model compactness, output length, and inference latency.

\section{Synthetic Patient Profile Generation}
\subsection{Patient Profile Design}

Synthetic patient profiles were designed as structured representations of the
patient characteristics required to generate diverse and personalised
doctor--patient consultations. Each profile combined clinical features drawn
from predefined KBG syndrome-related domains with a parent communication
persona, enabling variation in both clinical content and communication style.
Basic demographic attributes, including age and gender, were also included to
provide contextual information. The resulting profiles provided a consistent
input structure for the subsequent consultation generation process.

\subsection{Controlled Random Sampling Strategy}

To introduce controlled variability across synthetic patients, profile
attributes were randomly sampled from predefined clinical feature and persona
pools. Clinical features were organised into domains including vision,
hearing, dental, cardiac, neurological, gastrointestinal, and other medical
characteristics. Features were sampled from the corresponding domain-specific
pools, with additional characteristics drawn from the other medical feature
pool. A parent communication persona was independently sampled from a
predefined set representing different communication styles. This strategy
allowed the profiles to vary systematically while maintaining a consistent
overall structure.

The sampling of a clinical feature from a domain can be represented as:

\[
x_d \sim P_d
\]

where $x_d$ denotes the sampled feature from domain $d$, and $P_d$ represents
the corresponding predefined feature pool. The complete clinical feature
pools and parent communication personas used for sampling are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:profile_pools}.

\subsection{Profile Generation Procedure}

For each synthetic patient, demographic attributes and a parent communication
persona were first selected from their respective predefined pools. Clinical
features were then sampled from the relevant domain-specific pools, followed
by additional features from the other medical feature pool. The selected
attributes were combined into a structured profile and assigned a unique
patient identifier. This process was repeated until the required number of
profiles had been generated. The resulting profiles were stored in JSON
format, providing a consistent and machine-readable representation for the
subsequent consultation generation stage.

\section{Doctor–Patient Consultation Ibrahim}

\subsection{Consultation Generation Framework}
Synthetic doctor--patient consultations were generated to create realistic and diverse clinical interactions for subsequent personalised report generation. Each synthetic patient profile was provided to \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct} using a structured prompt. The prompt incorporated the patient-specific clinical features, parent communication persona, fixed consultation structure, and explicit generation constraints. A representative consultation was also included as an in-context example to guide the expected response format and style, constituting a few-shot prompting approach.

The model was instructed to reproduce a fixed sequence of ten predefined doctor questions covering the major clinical domains relevant to KBG syndrome, while generating corresponding parent responses grounded in the supplied profile. The parent persona controlled variation in linguistic style, vocabulary, and level of detail. Generation constraints were used to minimise unsupported clinical information and maintain consistency between the source profile and generated consultation.

Each consultation was generated independently and stored with its patient identifier and source profile in JSON Lines format. The resulting synthetic consultations were subsequently used for clinical report generation. The overall generation pipeline is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:consultation_framework}, while the complete prompt is provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:consultation_prompt}.

\subsection{Diversity Assessment}
The diversity of the generated consultations was evaluated to determine whether the generation process produced sufficiently varied parent responses rather than repeatedly reproducing similar linguistic patterns. Diversity was assessed across lexical, structural, grammatical, and semantic dimensions.

Distinct-2 \cite{guo_curious_2024} was used to measure lexical variation by calculating the proportion of unique bigrams within parent responses. Standard deviation of Mean Length of Utterance (MLU) \cite{potratz_measurement_2022} captured variation in the length of parent responses, providing an indication of differences in response structure and level of detail. POS Shannon entropy \cite{arora_estimating_2022} measured the distribution and variability of grammatical categories used across the consultations. Finally, pairwise cosine similarity between consultation embeddings was used to assess semantic similarity between generated consultations \cite{noauthor_evaluating_2026}.

Together, these complementary measures assessed whether the consultations varied not only in their surface-level wording, but also in response length, grammatical composition, and overall semantic content. This provided a broader assessment of the diversity of the synthetic consultation dataset.
\section{Teacher-Guided Clinical Report Generation}

Following the generation of synthetic doctor--patient consultations, the
consultations were transformed into personalised clinical reports using a
large language model. This stage was designed to generate high-quality
reference reports that could subsequently be evaluated and used in the
student-model training and knowledge distillation stages. Each report was
grounded in the corresponding consultation, while a clinical reference
context provided additional syndrome-level information required for
interpretation and management recommendations.

The report generation process was formulated as a conditional text generation
task:

\[
y = f_{\theta}(x,c,p)
\]

where $x$ represents the doctor--patient consultation, $c$ represents the
KBG syndrome clinical reference context, $p$ represents the structured
generation prompt, $f_{\theta}$ denotes the language model with parameters
$\theta$, and $y$ represents the generated clinical report. The consultation
provided the patient-specific evidence, while the clinical reference
provided supporting syndrome-level information.

The \textit{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct} model
(\texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct}) was used for report generation.
The model was instruction prompted to transform each consultation into a
structured clinical report while maintaining factual grounding and
patient-specificity. To reduce GPU memory requirements during inference, the
model was loaded using 4-bit NF4 quantisation with double quantisation and
bfloat16 computation.

A structured prompting strategy was used to constrain the generation process.
The prompt provided the model with the KBG clinical reference guidelines and
the corresponding doctor--parent consultation. The model was instructed to
first identify the clinical findings present in the consultation, associate
each finding with its appropriate clinical category, and then generate the
final report. This intermediate processing was performed internally and was
not included in the generated output.

To reduce unsupported clinical information, patient-specific statements were
required to originate from the consultation, while syndrome-level information
was restricted to the supplied clinical reference. Recommendations were
generated only when supported by reported patient findings. Where information
was unavailable for a category, the model was instructed to return
``N/A''. This grounding strategy reduced the likelihood of introducing
symptoms or clinical assumptions that were not supported by the source
material.

The generated reports followed a fixed 13-category schema covering major
clinical domains, including cardiology, gastroenterology, immunology,
neurology, ENT, ophthalmology, dental, education, behaviour and development,
and skeletal features, followed by screening and treatment recommendations.
For each clinical domain, the report distinguished between
\textit{patient-specific effects} and \textit{syndrome-level information}.
This consistent schema enabled systematic comparison of reports across
patients and provided a standardised target format for subsequent
distillation.

Generation was performed independently for each consultation. A relatively
low sampling temperature ($T=0.3$) was used to favour factual consistency
and adherence to the prescribed report structure. The resulting outputs
were stored in JSONL format together with the source consultation and
associated generation fields, preserving the correspondence between each
input consultation and its generated report. These consultation--report
pairs formed the teacher-generated reference dataset.

The teacher-generated reports were subsequently subjected to rubric-based
quality assessment. Reports were evaluated against predefined criteria
before being considered for subsequent student-model training and
knowledge-distillation experiments. The complete report-generation prompt,
including the clinical reference context and output schema, is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:report_prompt}.

The overall process is illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:teacher_report_generation}, showing the transformation from
the synthetic consultation and clinical reference context through structured
prompting and language-model generation to the final structured clinical
report.

\section{Evaluation of Generated Personalised Medical Reports}
This section evaluates whether teacher-generated reports are of sufficient quality to serve as training data for the student model. Reports are assessed based on factuality, coverage, medical tone, readability, and conciseness to determine their suitability for knowledge distillation.
\subsection{Factuality}


Factuality was assessed using a fine-grained, claim-level verification procedure to determine whether generated reports were supported by their designated source information. The approach was motivated by methods that decompose generated text into atomic factual units and subsequently verify each unit against an evidence source \cite{min_factscore_2023, scire_fenice_2024}. Similar claim-level approaches have also been applied to medical text, where fine-grained factuality assessment is particularly important for identifying unsupported clinical information \cite{bn_fact-controlled_2025, munnangi_factehr_2025}.

For each generated report, the factual content was first decomposed into atomic clinical claims. An atomic fact is a minimal, self-contained unit of information that expresses a single, distinct proposition and cannot be broken down further without losing its structural meaning \cite{min_factscore_2023}. A structured extraction prompt was used to identify patient-specific information explicitly supported by the doctor--parent consultation, while excluding questions, non-informative responses, inference, and external medical knowledge. Closely related details representing the same clinical fact were retained as a single claim. The complete extraction prompt is provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_claim_prompt}.

Each extracted claim was subsequently verified against its corresponding evidence source. Patient-specific claims, including reported symptoms and clinical history, were checked against the original doctor--parent consultation, whereas syndrome-level claims were checked against the KBG syndrome background information used during report generation. Verification therefore assessed whether each claim was grounded in the information available to the report-generation process rather than relying on external medical knowledge.

Each claim $c_i$ was assigned a binary verification label:

\begin{equation}
v_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } c_i \text{ is supported by its corresponding source},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

A claim was considered supported when the source explicitly conveyed the same clinical meaning, allowing for differences in wording, grammar, or clinically equivalent terminology. Unsupported, contradictory, or source-unverifiable claims were assigned a value of zero. The complete verification prompt and evaluation criteria are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_verification_prompt}.

The report-level factuality score was calculated as the proportion of supported atomic claims:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Factuality}(R)
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i}{N},
\end{equation}

where $N$ denotes the total number of extracted claims in report $R$. The resulting score ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater source-grounded factuality.

The complete evaluation process can therefore be represented as:

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    enhanced,
    colback=blue!3,
    colframe=blue!45!black,
    boxrule=0.7pt,
    arc=1.5mm,
    left=4mm,
    right=4mm,
    top=3mm,
    bottom=3mm
]
\centering
\textbf{Factuality Evaluation Pipeline}

\vspace{2mm}

\text{Patient Report}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Atomic Claims}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Source Verification}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Binary Labels}
$\rightarrow$
\text{Factuality Score}

\end{tcolorbox}

For example, a generated statement such as ``The patient experiences speech delay'' would be treated as an atomic claim and verified against the corresponding consultation. If the consultation explicitly supports the statement, the claim receives $v_i=1$; otherwise, it receives $v_i=0$. Further examples of claim decomposition, evidence sources, and verification decisions are provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:factuality_verification_prompt}.

This procedure provides a fine-grained measure of source-grounded factuality and allows unsupported information to be identified at the individual claim level rather than being masked within an overall document-level judgement.

\subsection{Coverage}

Coverage was evaluated to measure the extent to which clinically relevant
information from the source material was retained in the generated reports.
Unlike factuality, which assesses whether generated claims are supported by
source evidence, coverage assesses whether relevant source information is
represented in the report \cite{noauthor_faithfulness_2026, song_finesure_2024}.
Because the report integrates information from different sources, coverage
was evaluated separately for patient-specific information, syndrome-level
background information, and recommendations.

\subsubsection{Patient-Specific Coverage}

Patient-specific coverage was assessed using a two-stage claim-based
procedure. First, clinically meaningful patient-specific claims were
extracted from the doctor--parent consultation. Only information explicitly
stated by the parent about the child was retained; doctor's questions,
\textit{N/A} responses, emotions, uncertainty, opinions, intentions, and
unsupported inferences were excluded. Complex statements were decomposed
into distinct clinical facts while avoiding duplicate claims \cite{dejl_comprehensiveness_2026, kim_atomic_2026}. The extraction prompt is
provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:patient_coverage_prompt}.

Each claim was then evaluated against the generated report. A claim was
considered covered if the report conveyed the same clinical meaning,
irrespective of differences in wording or sentence structure. Claims that
were absent, unclear, or unsupported were assigned $0$. Thus,

\begin{equation}
v_i^{P} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if claim } c_i \text{ is covered by the report},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The patient-specific coverage score was:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_P}\sum_{i=1}^{N_P}v_i^{P},
\end{equation}

where $N_P$ is the number of extracted patient-specific claims.

\subsubsection{Syndrome Background and Recommendation Coverage}

Syndrome-level coverage was evaluated using feature-specific questions.
For each clinical feature explicitly reported by the parent, two questions
were generated: one assessing coverage of relevant KBG syndrome background
information (\textit{syndrome\_background}) and one assessing coverage of
relevant recommendations, screening, treatment, monitoring, referral, or
therapy (\textit{recommendation}). Question generation was restricted to
features present in the consultation \cite{scialom_questeval_2021, moramarco_towards_2021}. The corresponding prompts are provided
in Appendix~\ref{appx:syndrome_coverage_prompt}.

Each question was evaluated against the generated report and assigned:

\begin{equation}
v_j =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if the requested information is explicitly addressed},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Separate scores were calculated for syndrome background and recommendations:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_B}\sum_{j=1}^{N_B}v_j^{B},
\qquad
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}(R)
=
\frac{1}{N_{Rec}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{Rec}}v_j^{Rec}.
\end{equation}

Here, $N_B$ and $N_{Rec}$ denote the numbers of background and
recommendation questions, respectively. Background coverage required the
report to address how the relevant feature affects people with KBG syndrome,
while recommendation coverage required explicit, feature-relevant clinical
advice. Missing, ambiguous, or inferred information was assigned $0$.

The three component scores were retained separately and subsequently
averaged to obtain the overall coverage score:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Overall}(R)
=
\frac{
\mathrm{Coverage}_{P}(R)
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{B}(R)
+
\mathrm{Coverage}_{Rec}(R)
}{3}.
\end{equation}

This provides separate and overall measures of retention of patient-specific
findings, syndrome-level information, and recommendations.
\subsection{Conciseness Assessment}
\label{sec:conciseness}

Conciseness was evaluated as a composite measure of information retention,
factual reliability, and report brevity. This reflects the requirement that
generated clinical reports should preserve relevant information and factual
accuracy without unnecessary verbosity.

For each report, token length $L_i$ was calculated using the tokenizer of the
evaluated model. Token lengths were min--max normalised across the evaluation
set:

\begin{equation}
L_i^{\mathrm{norm}}
=
\frac{L_i-L_{\min}}
{L_{\max}-L_{\min}},
\end{equation}

where $L_{\min}$ and $L_{\max}$ are the minimum and maximum report lengths.
A brevity factor was then defined as:

\begin{equation}
B_i = 1-L_i^{\mathrm{norm}},
\end{equation}

so that shorter reports receive higher brevity scores. The final conciseness
score was calculated using the geometric mean of coverage, factuality, and
brevity:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Conciseness}_i
=
\left(
\mathrm{Coverage}_i
\cdot
\mathrm{Factuality}_i
\cdot
B_i
\right)^{1/3}.
\end{equation}

The geometric mean ensures that high performance in one dimension cannot
fully compensate for poor performance in another. Thus, a high score requires
the report to be sufficiently informative, factually reliable, and concise.
\subsection{Readability Assessment}
\label{sec:readability}

Readability was evaluated to assess the linguistic accessibility of the
generated clinical reports using four established text-based measures. The
Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) score provides a general measure of reading ease,
with higher scores indicating greater readability \cite{flesch_new_1948}. The Flesch--Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) estimates the
educational grade level required to comprehend the text \cite{tanprasert_flesch-kincaid_2021}. The Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) index
estimates reading difficulty based primarily on the use of polysyllabic words \cite{mclaughlin_smog_1969}. The Dale--Chall score assesses readability based
on word familiarity and sentence length \cite{crossley_large-scaled_2023}.

All four measures were computed for each generated report using the cleaned
report text and the \texttt{textstat} implementation. The measures were
retained independently to capture complementary aspects of textual
difficulty and account for their different scoring interpretations. The
resulting readability scores were summarised across the evaluation set using
the mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and quartiles.
\subsection{Medical Tone Assessment}
\label{sec:medical_tone}

Medical tone was evaluated to assess whether generated reports used
professional, respectful, and patient-centred clinical language. The
assessment followed a two-stage LLM-based procedure. First, patient-specific
content was extracted from each report, including statements describing how
the condition affects the patient and recommendations for screening or
treatment. Syndrome-level background information and general medical
information were excluded. The extraction used few-shot prompting, with the
complete prompt provided in Appendix~\ref{appx:medical_tone_prompt}.

The extracted statements were then evaluated using a separate few-shot
evaluation prompt across three binary dimensions: \textit{professionalism},
\textit{respectfulness}, and \textit{patient-centred communication}. These
dimensions reflect the importance of clear, appropriate, respectful, and
patient-focused communication in clinical contexts \cite{bowden_evaluating_nodate, rattanapitoon_beyond_2025, omar_impact_2025}.
Each statement received a binary score:

\begin{equation}
s_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if statement } i \text{ satisfies criterion } j,\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $j \in \{P,R,C\}$ represents professionalism, respectfulness, and
patient-centred communication, respectively. For each report, the score for
criterion $j$ was calculated as the proportion of extracted statements
receiving a positive evaluation:

\begin{equation}
S_j =
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{ij},
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the number of extracted patient-specific statements. The three
dimensions were retained separately to distinguish different aspects of
medical tone rather than combining them into a single score. Scores were
calculated for all generated reports and subsequently summarised across the
evaluation set.
\section{Knowledge Distillation}
\subsection{Teacher and Student Models}
The proposed framework adopts a teacher--student knowledge
distillation paradigm, in which knowledge generated by a larger teacher
model is transferred to a substantially smaller student model
\cite{hinton_distilling_2015}. This architecture separates
knowledge generation from efficient deployment, allowing
the student to acquire task-specific capabilities from the teacher while
operating with considerably lower computational and memory requirements \cite{moslemi_survey_2024-1}.

The teacher model was
\textit{Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507}, an instruction-tuned
mixture-of-experts (MoE) model with approximately 30 billion total
parameters and approximately 3 billion activated parameters per token \cite{yang_qwen3_2025}. The teacher was used to generate the
synthetic doctor--patient consultations and corresponding personalised
medical reports that formed the primary source of supervision for
subsequent student training. During inference, the teacher was loaded using
4-bit NormalFloat-4 (NF4) quantisation with bfloat16 computation to reduce
memory requirements while preserving the required generation capability.

The student model was
\textit{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct}, an instruction-tuned model containing
approximately 1.5 billion parameters \cite{qwen_qwen25_2025}. It
was selected as the compact deployment model because its substantially
smaller parameter count makes it more suitable for resource-constrained and
local inference. Rather than reproducing the full capacity of the teacher,
the student is trained to acquire the task-specific behaviour required for
personalised medical report generation.

Let $T$ denote the teacher and $S_{\theta}$ denote the student parameterised
by $\theta$. Given a consultation $x$, the teacher generates a target
response $y_T$:

\begin{equation}
y_T = T(x).
\end{equation}

The resulting teacher-generated response provides supervision for the
student, which models the conditional distribution

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y\mid x).
\end{equation}

The substantial difference in model scale provides a direct measure of the
intended efficiency gain. Let $N_T$ and $N_S$ denote the total parameter
counts of the teacher and student, respectively. The relative parameter
reduction is defined as

\begin{equation}
R = 1-\frac{N_S}{N_T}.
\end{equation}

Using approximately $30$B parameters for the teacher and $1.5$B for the
student gives

\begin{equation}
R \approx 1-\frac{1.5}{30}=0.95,
\end{equation}

corresponding to an approximately 95\% reduction in total
parameter count.

The resulting pipeline therefore consists of a large
teacher model for knowledge and supervision generation and a
compact student model for efficient task execution. The student
was subsequently established through supervised fine-tuning and further
optimised using the proposed quality-weighted distillation and
preference-based learning approaches. This provides the foundation for
investigating whether clinically relevant report-generation capabilities
can be transferred from a large model to a substantially smaller model
without compromising the desired report quality.
\subsection{Supervised Fine-Tuning of the Student Model}
\label{sec:student_sft}

Following the teacher--student model selection, the student model was first
adapted to the personalised medical report generation task using supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) \cite{chakrabarti_automated_2025, zhou_enhancing_2025}. This stage establishes the baseline task-specific student
before applying the subsequent quality-weighted distillation and preference
optimisation approaches. The training data consisted of
teacher-generated consultation--report pairs, where each doctor--parent
consultation was used as the input and its corresponding personalised
medical report served as the target output. This formulation allows the
student to learn the reporting structure, clinical information selection,
and response characteristics demonstrated by the teacher \cite{kim_sequence-level_2016}.

The student, \textit{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct}, was adapted using
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), a parameter-efficient fine-tuning
method that introduces trainable low-rank updates while keeping the
pretrained model parameters frozen \cite{hu_lora_2021}. LoRA reduces the
number of parameters requiring optimisation and consequently lowers the
memory requirements of fine-tuning while retaining the pretrained
representations of the underlying language model.

For a pretrained weight matrix
$W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, LoRA parameterises its task-specific
adaptation as

\begin{equation}
W = W_0 + \Delta W,
\qquad
\Delta W = BA,
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r},
\qquad
A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k},
\qquad
r \ll \min(d,k).
\end{equation}

Here, $r$ denotes the LoRA rank. The pretrained matrix $W_0$ is frozen
during optimisation, while the low-rank matrices $A$ and $B$ are updated
to capture the task-specific parameter adaptation. In this work, LoRA
adapters were applied to the attention projection layers
(\textit{q\_proj}, \textit{k\_proj}, \textit{v\_proj}, and
\textit{o\_proj}) and the feed-forward projection layers
(\textit{gate\_proj}, \textit{up\_proj}, and \textit{down\_proj}) \cite{lelle_token-level_2026}.
The complete LoRA and optimisation configuration is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

Given a training dataset

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i)
\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ represents a formatted doctor--parent consultation and $y_i$
represents the corresponding teacher-generated report, the student was
optimised using the standard autoregressive language-modelling objective.
The conditional probability of the target sequence is factorised
token-by-token as

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y_i\mid x_i)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T_i}
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right),
\end{equation}

where $T_i$ is the number of target tokens and $y_{i,<t}$ denotes the
sequence of target tokens preceding position $t$. The corresponding
negative log-likelihood objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\end{equation}

Thus, SFT minimises the token-level cross-entropy between the student's
predicted distribution and the teacher-provided target sequence \cite{li_preserving_2025}. Only the
trainable LoRA parameters are updated during optimisation, while the
pretrained student parameters remain frozen \cite{hu_lora_2021}.

The implementation used the \textit{SFTTrainer} framework from TRL for
causal language-model fine-tuning \cite{trl_sft}. Training was performed
with a maximum sequence length of 4096 tokens and an effective batch size
of 8, achieved through gradient accumulation. The optimisation employed
AdamW with 8-bit parameter states, a cosine learning-rate schedule, and a
warm-up phase. The student was trained for five epochs, with validation
performed at the end of each epoch. The complete training configuration,
including the LoRA rank, scaling factor, target modules, learning rate,
batch configuration, optimiser, scheduler, and sequence length, is
reported in Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

The resulting SFT model provides the principal student baseline against
which the subsequent distillation strategies are evaluated. However, the
standard SFT objective treats each training pair uniformly, irrespective
of the quality or clinical reliability of the teacher-generated target.
Consequently, lower-quality examples can contribute to the optimisation
objective with the same magnitude as higher-quality examples. This
limitation motivates the subsequent Quality-Weighted Knowledge
Distillation stage, where the contribution of individual teacher-generated
examples is explicitly modulated according to their evaluated quality.
\subsection{Quality-Weighted Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:quality_weighted_distillation}

The supervised fine-tuning stage treats all teacher-generated
consultation--report pairs as equally informative. However, the quality of
synthetic supervision can vary across examples. In the present clinical
report generation task, this variation is particularly important because
the generated reports are required to preserve clinically relevant
information, remain factually grounded, and maintain an appropriate medical
tone. Consequently, uniformly weighting all training examples may cause
lower-quality teacher outputs to exert the same optimisation influence as
higher-quality outputs.

To address this limitation, a \textbf{quality-weighted response-based
knowledge distillation} objective was introduced. The method retains the
hard teacher-generated responses used during SFT, while incorporating an
example-level quality signal into the optimisation objective. This extends
the teacher--student distillation framework introduced by
Hinton et al.~\cite{hinton2015distilling} by explicitly accounting for the
reliability of individual teacher demonstrations.

Let the teacher-generated training dataset be

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i)
\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}

where $x_i$ denotes a doctor--parent consultation and $y_i$ denotes the
corresponding personalised report generated by the teacher. For each
report, three independently evaluated quality dimensions were obtained:
clinical information coverage ($C_i$), factuality ($F_i$), and medical
tone ($M_i$). Each dimension was represented as a normalised score in
$[0,1]$.

A single supervision-quality score was obtained by combining these
dimensions using their geometric mean:

\begin{equation}
q_i =
\left(
C_i F_i M_i
\right)^{1/3}.
\label{eq:quality_score}
\end{equation}

The geometric mean was selected because it imposes a multiplicative
interaction between the quality dimensions. In contrast to an arithmetic
mean, a high score in one dimension cannot fully compensate for a
substantially lower score in another. Thus, the quality signal favours
teacher demonstrations that provide a balanced combination of clinical
coverage, factual reliability, and appropriate medical communication.

For an autoregressive student model parameterised by $\theta$, the
conditional probability of a target report is factorised as

\begin{equation}
P_{\theta}(y_i\mid x_i)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T_i}
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right),
\end{equation}

where $T_i$ is the number of target tokens and $y_{i,<t}$ denotes the
target-token prefix preceding position $t$. The conventional SFT
negative log-likelihood objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\label{eq:sft_loss}
\end{equation}

The proposed quality-weighted objective modifies this loss by introducing
the example-specific quality coefficient $q_i$:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QKD}}(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
q_i
\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log
P_{\theta}
\left(
y_{i,t}
\mid
x_i,y_{i,<t}
\right).
\label{eq:qkd_loss}
\end{equation}

Hence, the gradient contribution of training example $i$ is scaled by its
quality score:

\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathrm{QKD}}
=
q_i
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathrm{SFT}}.
\end{equation}

This formulation provides a direct mechanism through which the quality of
teacher supervision influences student learning. A teacher-generated
example with a high value of $q_i$ contributes strongly to the optimisation
gradient, whereas an example with a lower quality score contributes less.
Importantly, the weighting is applied at the example level, meaning that
all target tokens belonging to a given consultation--report pair inherit
the same quality coefficient. The autoregressive token-prediction
objective itself is therefore unchanged; only the relative importance of
the training examples is modified.

The proposed approach can consequently be viewed as
\textit{quality-aware selective knowledge transfer}. Standard SFT assumes
that every teacher demonstration provides equally reliable supervision,
whereas the proposed objective induces a quality-dependent weighting over
the teacher-generated dataset. The student is therefore encouraged to
prioritise behaviours demonstrated by examples that simultaneously exhibit
strong clinical coverage, factual consistency, and appropriate medical
tone.

This formulation also establishes a direct link between the evaluation and
distillation stages of the framework. Rather than using coverage,
factuality, and medical tone solely as post-hoc evaluation metrics, their
combined score is transformed into a training signal that determines the
relative contribution of each synthetic example. The evaluation framework
therefore functions both as a measure of generated-report quality and as a
mechanism for controlling the transfer of teacher knowledge.

The student architecture, LoRA parameterisation, and underlying
autoregressive training procedure remain unchanged from the SFT baseline.
The principal methodological difference is therefore the introduction of
the quality-dependent weighting term $q_i$ in Equation~\ref{eq:qkd_loss}.
The complete optimisation and implementation configuration is provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.

This formulation enables a controlled comparison between uniformly
weighted SFT and quality-weighted distillation, isolating the effect of
supervision quality on the transfer of teacher-generated knowledge to the
compact student model.
\section{Direct Preference Optimisation}

\label{sec:dpo}

Following supervised fine-tuning and quality-weighted knowledge
distillation, Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) was investigated as a
preference-based optimisation stage. Unlike SFT, which learns from a single
target response, and quality-weighted distillation, which assigns different
importance to teacher-generated examples, DPO learns from \textit{relative
preferences} between two candidate responses for the same input \cite{rafailov_direct_2024}.

For each consultation $x_i$, a preference pair was constructed consisting
of a preferred response $y_i^{+}$ and a rejected response $y_i^{-}$.
The preferred response represents the higher-quality candidate according
to the established report-evaluation framework, while the rejected response
represents the comparatively lower-quality candidate. The resulting
preference dataset is therefore defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i^{+},y_i^{-})
\right\}_{i=1}^{N}.
\end{equation}

This formulation allows the student to learn not only what constitutes a
valid response, but also which of two plausible responses is preferable
for the same clinical input.

\subsubsection*{DPO Objective}

DPO provides a direct optimisation objective for preference learning without
requiring a separately trained reward model\cite{rafailov_direct_2024}. Let $\pi_{\theta}$ denote the trainable student
policy and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ denote a frozen reference policy. For a
given consultation $x_i$, the DPO objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)
=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+},y^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}}
\left[
\log
\sigma
\left(
\beta
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}\mid x)}
-
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}\mid x)}
\right]
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:dpo_loss}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function and
$\beta$ controls the strength of the preference optimisation relative to
the reference policy.

For an autoregressive language model, the sequence-level log-probability
appearing in Equation~\ref{eq:dpo_loss} is decomposed into token-level
conditional probabilities:

\begin{equation}
\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\log
\pi_{\theta}
\left(
y_t
\mid
x,y_{<t}
\right).
\end{equation}

Consequently, the optimisation compares the relative likelihood assigned
by the student to the preferred and rejected responses, while simultaneously
measuring their deviation from the reference policy.

The central quantity in the DPO objective is therefore the relative
log-probability difference

\begin{equation}
\Delta_i =
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i^{+}\mid x_i)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{+}\mid x_i)}
\right]
-
\left[
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i^{-}\mid x_i)}
     {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{-}\mid x_i)}
\right].
\end{equation}

Minimising Equation~\ref{eq:dpo_loss} encourages $\Delta_i$ to increase,
thereby increasing the student's relative preference for $y_i^{+}$ over
$y_i^{-}$. The reference-policy terms constrain this optimisation by
penalising uncontrolled deviation from the behaviour represented by the
reference model. This provides a preference-learning mechanism without
requiring explicit reward-model training or reinforcement-learning policy
updates\cite{rafailov_direct_2024}.

\subsubsection*{Preference Construction}

Preference pairs were constructed from candidate personalised reports
generated for the same doctor--parent consultation. The previously defined
quality evaluation framework provided the basis for determining the
relative quality of candidate responses. Coverage, factuality, and medical
tone were used to assess the clinical suitability of the candidates.

For a candidate report $y$, the corresponding composite quality score was
defined as

\begin{equation}
q(y)
=
\left(
C(y)\,F(y)\,M(y)
\right)^{1/3},
\end{equation}

where $C(y)$, $F(y)$, and $M(y)$ represent the coverage, factuality, and
medical-tone scores, respectively. For two candidate responses generated
for the same consultation, $y_a$ and $y_b$, the preferred response was
selected according to their relative quality:

\begin{equation}
y^{+}
=
\arg\max_{y\in\{y_a,y_b\}} q(y),
\qquad
y^{-}
=
\arg\min_{y\in\{y_a,y_b\}} q(y).
\end{equation}

This construction converts the multidimensional report-quality assessment
into pairwise supervision. Rather than requiring an absolute quality label
during optimisation, DPO receives only the ordering between the preferred
and rejected responses.

\subsubsection*{Relationship to SFT and Quality-Weighted Distillation}

The three optimisation stages differ in the form of supervision supplied
to the student. SFT learns from individual input--target pairs and
minimises the negative log-likelihood of the teacher-generated target.
Quality-weighted distillation retains this formulation but scales each
example according to its composite quality score. DPO instead provides
pairwise preference supervision and explicitly optimises the relative
probability of preferred versus rejected responses.

Formally, the progression can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
\text{SFT}
\rightarrow
\text{quality-weighted response distillation}
\rightarrow
\text{preference optimisation}.
\end{equation}

SFT therefore answers the question of \textit{what response should be
generated}, quality-weighted distillation additionally determines
\textit{which demonstrations should have greater training influence}, and
DPO learns \textit{which of two candidate responses should be preferred}.
This provides three progressively different mechanisms for transferring
and refining the teacher-derived behaviour of the compact student model.

Unlike reinforcement-learning-based preference optimisation, DPO does not
require explicit reward-model fitting or online policy sampling during the
optimisation stage \cite{rafailov_direct_2024}. Its direct likelihood-based
objective makes it particularly suitable for controlled offline
optimisation using the preference pairs constructed from the generated
clinical reports.

The student model and parameter-efficient LoRA configuration remained
consistent with the preceding optimisation stages, allowing the effect of
the preference objective to be evaluated without introducing changes to
the underlying student architecture. The complete DPO hyperparameters and
preference-pair construction configuration are provided in
Appendix~\ref{appx:training_config}.
\section{Evaluation of Student-Generated Reports}
\label{sec:student_evaluation}

Following supervised fine-tuning, quality-weighted knowledge distillation,
and preference optimisation, the resulting student models were evaluated
on a held-out test set to determine whether the knowledge transferred from
the teacher was retained while reducing model size and computational cost.
The same evaluation framework established for the teacher-generated reports
was applied to the student-generated reports, ensuring that performance
could be compared consistently across models.

The evaluation was performed across two complementary aspects: 
\textit{clinical report quality} and \textit{output-level knowledge
transfer}. Clinical quality was assessed using the previously defined
coverage, factuality, conciseness, readability, and medical-tone measures.
These metrics were applied without modification to the student outputs,
allowing the quality of the distilled reports to be compared directly with
the corresponding teacher/reference reports. Their definitions and
calculation procedures are provided in the preceding evaluation sections.

In addition, output-level knowledge transfer was assessed using lexical and
semantic similarity between student-generated reports and the corresponding
teacher/reference reports. ROUGE and BLEU were used to measure lexical
correspondence, while BERTScore and embedding-based similarity were used to
assess semantic alignment. These measures provide complementary evidence
of whether the student reproduces the information and semantic content
expressed by the teacher, while allowing for differences in surface wording
\cite{lin2004rouge,papineni2002bleu,zhang2020bertscore}.

The evaluation was conducted independently for the SFT, quality-weighted
distillation, and DPO student models. For each model, the same held-out
test consultations were used and reports were generated using a consistent
inference procedure. This controlled evaluation isolates differences
arising from the training strategy rather than differences in the
evaluation data or protocol.

\subsection{Quality-Aware Knowledge Distillation and Research Gap}
\label{sec:quality_aware_kd_research_gap}
The effectiveness of response-based knowledge distillation depends not only on teacher capability, but also on the quality of the supervision it provides. Since teacher-generated responses become training targets for the student, factual errors, omissions, irrelevant information, or undesirable response characteristics may also be transferred to the student. Treating all teacher responses as equally reliable therefore assumes a uniformity of supervision that may not hold in practice. This has motivated quality-aware approaches that adjust the contribution of individual training examples through filtering, example selection, or quality- and importance-weighted supervised fine-tuning \cite{liu_air_2025-1, lei_learning_2025, lee_what_2026}. However, these approaches raise a more fundamental question: beyond determining how much an example should contribute to training, what characteristics make a teacher response valuable as supervision? The answer is necessarily task-dependent, since a response that appears high-quality according to one criterion may still be unsuitable for the requirements of another task.

A key distinction is therefore between filtering and weighting. Filtering removes examples that fall below a predefined quality criterion, reducing the influence of unreliable supervision but potentially discarding useful information alongside limited errors \cite{liu_air_2025, lee_what_2026}. This trade-off between reducing unreliable supervision and preserving potentially valuable training data is particularly important in data-scarce domains such as rare diseases, where limited examples may contain unique information that is difficult to replace \cite{poddar_mitigating_2025, alsentzer_few_2025}. Weighting instead allows examples to influence optimisation to different degrees, preserving potentially useful supervision while reducing the influence of less reliable responses \cite{zhao_rethinking_2026}. From a data-efficiency perspective, this provides a potential alternative to hard filtering when imperfect examples still contain partially valuable information. However, weighting alone does not resolve the problem of defining quality: its effectiveness ultimately depends on whether the resulting quality signal captures properties that are genuinely relevant to the target task rather than simply those that are easiest to measure \cite{lin_lead_2025}.

This issue is particularly important for personalised clinical report generation, where quality is inherently multidimensional and different desirable properties may not always align. A report may be factually correct while omitting important patient-specific information, or provide broad coverage while becoming unnecessarily verbose. Conversely, a concise report may improve efficiency while excluding clinically relevant details. Beyond the information conveyed, the quality of its communication, including readability and medical tone, influences whether that information is presented clearly, accessibly, and appropriately. Consequently, improving one dimension does not necessarily imply improvement in overall report quality, suggesting that clinical report quality cannot be adequately represented by a single property alone. Complementary assessment of factuality, coverage, conciseness, and communication quality may therefore be required \cite{li_llm-as--judge_2026-1, ahmad_finetuning_2026}.

Recent research further demonstrates the potential of knowledge distillation for developing lightweight models for medical report generation and related clinical generation tasks. However, existing approaches have largely focused on transferring representations, multimodal alignment, or other task-specific knowledge rather than explicitly considering the quality of individual teacher-generated responses when determining their contribution to student learning \cite{huang_medical_2026-1, su_medkit_2026}. This is an important distinction because quality assessment can play different roles in the learning pipeline: it may be used to evaluate the quality of a generated response after generation, or it may be incorporated into distillation to determine how strongly that response should influence the student. The latter treats response quality not merely as an evaluation criterion, but as a signal that can shape the learning process.

This exposes a more specific research gap. While knowledge distillation has been applied to medical report generation and quality-aware optimisation has been explored separately, there remains limited investigation into incorporating a multidimensional, clinically motivated assessment of teacher-generated responses directly into response-based distillation for personalised report generation \cite{huang_medical_2026-1,wu_error_2026, khan_radiology_2026, garg_rise_2025}. Such a quality signal should reflect complementary dimensions of report quality, including factuality, completeness, conciseness, and communication quality, rather than relying on a single measure that may not adequately represent overall clinical usefulness. Thus, the challenge is not simply to identify and remove low-quality responses, but to determine how different dimensions of response quality should influence the learning signal. This distinction is particularly relevant in data-scarce settings, where partially useful supervision may be costly to discard and a binary filtering decision may unnecessarily reduce the diversity and quantity of available training data.

Motivated by this gap, this thesis evaluates teacher-generated reports across multiple clinically relevant dimensions and uses these assessments to determine their contribution during supervised fine-tuning. Rather than filtering across all quality dimensions, the proposed approach first applies a minimum factuality requirement, then weights the remaining responses according to their multidimensional quality. Thus, factuality serves as a reliability constraint, while the remaining quality dimensions determine the relative influence of each response during learning.
The central research question is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Can multidimensional quality-aware knowledge distillation improve the clinical quality of reports generated by compact language models compared with conventional supervised fine-tuning?}
\end{quote}
